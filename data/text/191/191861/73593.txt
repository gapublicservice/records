PURPOSE AND SUMMARY
what is the purpose of your testimony?
I am offering recommendations to improve upon certain rate schedules proposed by Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”).
please summarize your review and recommendations.
First, I address the Customer Baseline Load (“CBL”) reduction criteria. I would like the Commission to consider a revision to the Real-Time Pricing (“RTP”) tariff schedules to allow for CBL reductions based on two years of historical load data, and to allow RTP customers to use back-up service, which would firm up reliable on-site customer generation for a CBL load reduction. I will explain the detailed tariff language modifications necessary to allow these options.
Second, I offer recommendations for the return on equity (“ROE”) earnings band. The Commission should consider some modifications to the ROE band and earnings sharing design. Specifically, an alternative for consideration would be to increase the percentage sharing of earnings above the band with customers (e.g., from 80% to 95%).  This would allow for larger refund relief to customers in years the Company has healthy earnings. 
Third, I address the Company’s proposed across-the-board rate increase and advocate for the importance of movement toward cost-based rate changes. This will help to mitigate the likelihood of large disparities between rates and costs before the next rate case. The Commission should consider ordering movement toward cost-based rates in this case rather than the across-the-board increase proposed.  
Finally, I ask the Commission to order inclusion of schedules, similar to the Minimum Filing Requirement (“MFR”) schedules, for the next rate case that match the actual revenue proposed by the Company for the first, second and third years of its three-year Alternate Rate Plan (“ARP”) so that these schedules better match the increases requested by the Company under the ARP.  Likewise, if the Company proposes a levelized rate increase, as is the case in the current proceeding, then the Company should file similar MFR schedules that match the rate increases on a levelized basis.
RTP CUSTOMERS AND TARIFF TERMS FOR CBL REDUCTION
what are the current criteria used by georgia power to consider customer applications for cbl reduction?
These criteria are found in “Special Provisions” Section C – CBL Revisions of the RTP rate schedules.  Noteworthy for discussion here is the last statement in that section: 
If an existing RTP customer, without approved load removal, wants to come off RTP in an attempt to lower the CBL level, the customer must remain off RTP for a full twenty-four months.  The new CBL would be established using current methods as previously described in this tariff.
Although load removal verification is covered in earlier language within Section C, missing is the possibility that customers through time may simply achieve load reductions that can be documented through historical meter data.  Also missing is the possibility that a customer may invest in on-site generation capacity producing at least 6,000 hours of firm power each year, such as a combined heat and power (“CHP”) plant.  If combined with the purchase of Georgia Power back-up service, such a CHP has the potential to provide firm power to serve base load when combined with Georgia Power back-up service and should be considered for a CBL reduction. These circumstances are worthy of CBL reduction and will require some tariff language revisions.
is the requirement to come off rtp for two years to reduce cbl a concern for customers?
Yes. The provisions in the RTP tariffs do not allow for CBL reduction assessment based on historical load unless the customer were to “come off RTP” for two years. One reason for coming off RTP for two years may be to collect sufficient load data for consideration in establishing new CBL levels.  However, it is not clear why it is necessary to come off RTP to assess the load shape data of the customer when those data already exist for an RTP customer.  If a customer currently has two years in which actual loads have been below the CBL demand in many hours throughout the year, that should be sufficient reason for a reassessment of the CBL load shape.
Another consideration is that customers whose historical rate schedule has been discontinued, for example, Time Of Use – Revenue Neutral Schedule (“TOU-RN”) customers, may lose their revenue-neutral rate schedule if they were to “come off RTP” for two years. In other words, these customers may be required to change their underlying rate schedule to receive RTP service in the future. These customers would be unduly harmed by virtue of their desire to have their CBL levels reviewed.
what happens when a customer’s load is below the cbl level?
The customer pays for the unused portion of the CBL under the non-RTP standard service rates but then receives a credit for the unused energy based on the interval power price marginal costs because Georgia Power either does not need to procure that energy or can sell excess energy into the market. 
if a customer has many hours in which its load is below cbl, does this send the appropriate price signals to the customer as you would expect with a real-time pricing option?
No. This is a perverse scenario in which the customer is not fully seeing the real-time and marginal pricing incentives and Georgia Power is crediting the customer for the unused portion of the CBL power.  The Commission should allow the opportunity for CBL reduction in circumstances such as this.
is the requirement to come off the rtp tariff for two years to reduce cbl problematic for the military bases?
Yes.  Most of these customers are Multiple Load Management (“MLM”) customers, but the MLM rate schedule no longer exists because it was discontinued in the last rate case.  When the MLM rate schedule was discontinued in the 2019 rate case, MLM customers were moved to the TOU-RN rate schedule to calculate the Standard Bill within the RTP rate schedules using CBL billing determinants.  Because the MLM schedule no longer exists, it is not clear where these customers would take service if they came off RTP for two years in an attempt to reduce their CBL. Some other rate schedule may cause significantly higher billing because these customers may lose the revenue neutrality promised in the last rate case.
Do you have a tariff language recommendation for the commission?
Yes. I recommend the Commission consider approving the following revision to the previously quoted provision in both the RTP Day-Ahead (“RTP-DA”) and RTP Hour-Ahead (“RTP-HA”) rate schedules:
If an existing RTP customer, without approved load removal, wants to come off RTP in an attempt to lower the CBL level, the customer must remain off RTP for a full twenty-four months.request reassessment of the CBL shape on an hourly basis. Such reassessment of the CBL shape by Georgia Power shall be based on twenty-four months of historical hourly metered load data for that customer.  The new CBL would be established using current methods as previously described in this tariff. 
if georgia power provides a good reason as to why the two years of data analysis should be prospective, can this be done without the customer coming off rtp?
Yes. For example, as an alternative to my recommendation to use historical load data, the customer could request prospective analysis without being forced to move off of RTP.
does a cbl reduction for one customer potentially cause an undue cost shifting to other customers?
No. If the customer’s load profile supports a reduction in CBL, the reduction in the customer’s actual load also supports a reduction in cost allocation to that customer.  Therefore, there is no undue cost shifting onto other customers.  
does a cbl reduction for one customer potentially cause a revenue shortfall for georgia power?
There is a possibility that a CBL reduction could cause a short-term reduction in revenue that temporarily may be foregone; however, this is true with any customer who currently qualifies for a CBL reduction as well as other customers on standard tariffs who alter their energy usage and demand.  Some customers on the system may increase their demand for electricity and others may decrease their demand, but with the diversity on the Georgia Power system, these effects tend to balance each other, and any differences will be corrected in the next rate case.  It is more important that customers who increase their demand, pay for that increased demand, and those customers who decrease their demand should not be forced to pay for unneeded electricity service.
can you explain why customer on-site generation capacity should be considered for a cbl reduction?
RTP customers who invest in on-site generation capacity should be considered for a CBL reduction as long as the customer subscribes to and pays for Georgia Power Back-Up Service Schedule “BU.”  Failure of an RTP customer with on-site generation to produce power would then be automatically billed under the CBL Standard Bill, pursuant to the RTP rate schedule. The addition of such a CHP plant will bring additional firm generation capacity to Georgia Power’s service territory, benefiting other customers.  
does this type of generation capacity investment by an RTP customer qualify for consideration of a cbl reduction under the current tariff language?
No.  The qualifying examples for a possible CBL reduction within the RTP rate schedules include load reductions due to customer equipment removal but not the installation of firm generation capacity that reduces load. 
do rtp customers qualify for georgia power’s back-up service?
The applicability section of the Back-Up Service Schedule states: 
Applicable upon request as a modification of the rate schedules for Power and Light (PL), and Full Use Service to Governmental Institutions (G), if the customer is a PURPA qualifying facility, or has customer-owned generation that normally operates at least 6,000 hours per year.
There is no mention of RTP rate schedules, but there is a reference to Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (“PURPA”) qualifying facilities (“QFs”), which a customer’s CHP may satisfy.  Therefore, it appears that RTP customers may not qualify to subscribe to back-up service unless perhaps a case can be made that the customer generation is a PURPA QF. When asked about this applicability at the hearing, Georgia Power’s rate design witness, Mr. Legg, indicated that he would need to check and added: “You know, off the top of my head, I don't know. We'd have to discuss the specifics of the account.”
can you suggest a possible reason why rtp customers may not qualify for back-up service?
Georgia Power can also address this question in its scheduled rebuttal testimony, but one reason may be a perspective that customer generation does not serve load within the customer’s CBL, but instead is viewed as simply part of the RTP portion of the customer’s load.
do you agree with such a view?
No.  Firm generation capacity such as a CHP plant will change the load shape of the customer on the Georgia Power grid.  If allowed to subscribe to and pay for Georgia Power’s back-up service, then there is no concern regarding reliability of the customer’s CHP capacity, nor is there a concern for insufficient revenue in the event that the customer stops producing power. This is because the Georgia Power Back-Up Service Schedule requires at least 6,000 hours of power and will bill for incremental back-up power not produced by the customer’s CHP under the RTP rate schedule Standard Bill rates used for the CBL portion of load.  This will require additional Georgia Power metering of the customer’s generator and other RTP customer-specific terms within the back-up service contract.  
why should rtp customer-sited generation under back-up service be considered base load capacity?
BU mandates a minimum of 6,000 operating hours per year.  That is, the customer’s generator must operate above a 68% annual capacity factor, which is consistent with other base load generation capacity.  Intermittent generation or generation not operating in this fashion will not qualify for back-up service and, therefore, would not qualify for a CBL reduction under my recommendation.  Therefore, any concern that the customer may not operate its generation in a reliable manner is not an issue because that customer will not stay on BU and will lose its CBL reduction.
are there components in the design of the georgia power back-up service that need to be carefully considered to allow an rtp customer to subscribe to back-up service while still maintaining the distinction between cbl and rtp load?
Yes.  The Back-Up Service Schedule includes the following four defined service categories: Standby Reserve Capacity, Back-Up Power, Supplementary Power, and Maintenance Power.  The rates for Standby Reserve Capacity are specified within the BU rate schedule and should not pose any difficulty.  On the other hand, Back-Up Power, Supplementary Power, and Maintenance Power all reference the otherwise applicable rate schedules of the rate classes that currently qualify for BU. Back-Up Power is defined as “energy or capacity to replace energy generated by a customer's own equipment when that equipment is not in service.”  Supplementary Power is defined as “energy or capacity supplied by the Company to the customer in addition to that which is normally generated by the customer’s equipment.”  Maintenance Power is defined as “energy or capacity supplied by the Company when the customer’s generation is not available during the designated maintenance period.”  
For RTP customers, this is somewhat more complex in that there needs to be a separation between CBL power and applicable rates and RTP marginal rates.  I recommend that Back-Up Power and Maintenance Power be billed under the CBL rates (i.e., standard rates of the RTP customer). To bill RTP customers under CBL rates for Back-Up Power will require additional metering by Georgia Power to measure the output of the customer’s generation capacity, but will protect Georgia Power and other customers from misuse of the BU rate schedule and ensure that the CBL reduction does not result in undue access to RTP marginal prices nor create a revenue recovery risk.  This type of separate metering is common under standby tariffs designed by other utilities. Supplementary Power would be split between the CBL load shape and RTP marginal pricing no differently than the way it currently is done within the RTP rate schedules. 
is the additional metering with rtp customers reason to deny back-up service for rtp customers seeking to reduce their cbl?
No.  Although there is an added complexity in terms of metering, it is not a reason to disallow participation by RTP customers. It is common for other utilities to require additional submetering of customer-sited generation when rate design requires it, which would be the case with RTP customers on back-up service.
does the addition of firm generation by a customer bring system reliability benefits to other customers?
Yes.  Having firm generation capacity embedded with customer load adds diversity to the grid generation fleet and added reliability to the Georgia Power system.
what tariff language changes would be needed to allow firm CHP capacity to be considered for a cbl reduction?
First, the Back-Up Service Schedule applicability section would be revised to add the RTP-DA and RTP-HA rate schedules.  Second, the “Back-Up Power” rate description would need to add “RTP CBL rates.”  The “Supplementary Power” rate definition would add “RTP CBL rates” and “RTP hourly price” under the normal RTP CBL load shape.  Maintenance Power would be priced as specified within the Back-Up Service contract.  Finally, the CBL Revisions (Section C of Special Provisions) to RTP-HA and RTP-DA would be revised to include customer-sited generation in which the customer is subscribed to Georgia Power Back-Up Service as an allowance for CBL reduction consideration.
ARe you recommending these revisions to the tariff language within the back-up service schedule and rtp schedules?
Yes.
ROE EARNINGS BAND AND SHARING MECHANISM
did you testify on the earnings band in georgia power’s 2019 rate case?
Yes.
what was your recommendation in the last rate case?
At that time, I recommended the Commission reduce the width of the ROE band to 100 basis points (1.0%); that is, 50 basis points above and below the target approved ROE.  
are you revising this recommendation?
If the Commission adopts a target ROE below the 11% proposed by the Company, such as 10%, then I would alter my recommendation to 100 basis points above the target ROE before sharing with customers takes place.  In other words, 11% could serve as the top end of the earnings band.
what is georgia power proposing for the earnings band in this case?
As set forth in the Direct Testimony of Aaron P. Abramovitz, Sarah P. Adams, Adam D. Houston, and Michael B. Robinson:
The Company is proposing to continue the ROE band of 9.5%-12.0%, which was approved by the Commission in the 2019 base rate case. The earnings band defines a reasonable range of return within which the Company is allowed to earn. Earnings below this band could indicate a rate adjustment is necessary, whereas earnings above this band would trigger the sharing mechanism whereby 80% of the excess earnings above 12.0% is returned to benefit customers. The sharing mechanism provides direct financial benefits to Georgia Power customers. Since 2013, customers have received the benefit of approximately $297 million through the sharing mechanisms. 
are you providing an alternative recommendation in this case?
Yes. As an alternative consideration, I recommend the Commission eliminate the 20% retention of earnings above the band or greatly reduce this retention by the Company.  That is, given the sufficient width of the earnings band, which is beneficial to Georgia Power, the Company would now share 100% of the earnings in excess of the band.  If the Commission believes that it is important to retain some incentive to achieve earnings above the band, then the amount retained by the Company could be reduced to 5%.  If the Commission reduces the amount retained to 5% for Georgia Power, the Company would still retain $5 million for every 50 basis points above the band.  This $5 million calculation is based upon the statement of Mr. Abramovitz at hearing that 50 basis points represents about $100 million.
how much does Georgia power retain as a result of the earnings band?
For every 50 basis points (or 0.5%) above the last awarded ROE of 10.50%, Georgia Power earns about an additional $100 million.  In other words, actual earnings at 12.0% under the current earnings band will generate $200 million in additional earnings above the proposed 11.0% ROE.  Therefore, giving the full amount above the band to customers is a fair balance. As stated by the Company as quoted above, customers have only received $297 million since 2013.
if customers had received 100% of the earnings above the band since 2013, how much more would have that been?
The customers would have obtained a full amount of $380 million, or $84 million more. This additional sharing would have provided extra relief to customers, but less than the amounts retained by the Company above the target approved ROE.
please summarize your recommendations for the earnings band.
I recommend that the Commission either narrow the band around the target ROE determined in this case and/or eliminate or reduce the 20% retained by the Company for amounts above the band.
do you believe that the constructive regulatory environment in georgia is retained with the adoption of your recommendation?
Yes. I have suggested a 100 basis point band above the target ROE approved by the Commission.  This 1.0% earnings band is worth $200 million to the Company and will be viewed as very constructive while still giving opportunities to customers to receive refunds, which are helpful interim rate relief for customers during the ARP years and good for public perception.  
RATE CLASS COST OF SERVICE AND RATE DESIGN
which witness sponsors the Georgia Power class cost of service study (“CCOSS”)?
Mr. Lee Evans is sponsoring the CCOSS for Georgia Power.
do you have any recommended changes to the CCOSS?
Not at this time.  
does the CCOSS support the across-the-board increase proposed by Georgia power?
No. The evidence produced by the CCOSS suggests that the rate increases to individual rate groups should be somewhat different, as reflected in Mr. Evans’ Direct Testimony, and GPC Exhibits 27-30 (Exhibits LPE-1 through LPE-4).
what do you recommend the commission order?
I recommend the Commission order implementation of the rate adjustments based on the CCOSS produced by Georgia Power. Alternatively, the Commission should consider movement toward cost of service to avoid significant departures from cost of service that may be more difficult to address in the next rate case.
Why may the current departures from cost of service become greater in the next rate case if an equal, across-the-board increase is applied now?
Based on my experience, public utility commissions that approve across-the-board increases when there is a need for cost of service adjustments tend to find larger departures from cost-based rates that need to be contended with in the next rate case.  It would be better to address this now with movement toward cost-based rates to avoid a larger deviation in the next rate case.
are cost-based rates important to maintaining an attractive business environment within the state of georgia?
Yes. Existing and new industries, businesses, and government entities looking for locations to site their next expansion typically include an assessment of energy costs in each potential location across the nation.  Therefore, it is important that the State of Georgia remain competitive in electricity rates for commercial and industrial customers, and the proper way to do that is with consideration of the cost of serving those customers. 
PROPOSED RATES AND MINIMUM FILING REQUIREMENTS
Has the company proposed a levelized approach for the base rate increase in 2023 to cover the three-year ARP?
Yes. 
do the schedules filed to satisfy the minimum filing requirements match the levelized rate increase proposed by georgia power?
No. The MFR schedules are based on the test-year 12 months ending July 31, 2023, thereby including the incremental first year of the three-year rate plan, but do not include the full amount of the Company’s proposed one-time levelized increase for traditional base rates to be effective January 1, 2023.  For example, the proposed tariff rate schedules found in MFR Item F-1 do not include the rates that would become effective if the Commission approves the levelized traditional base rate revenue increase found in Table 1 of the Direct Testimony of Abramovitz, Adams, Houston, and Robinson.  Similarly, MFR Items F-3 and F-4, containing billing comparisons between present and proposed rates and revenue proof, do not utilize rates based on the one-time levelized increase proposed to be effective January 1, 2023.    
Does the lack of schedules using rates proposed under the levelized approach make assessment difficult?
Yes, it is difficult to fully assess the Company’s rate proposal without knowing the rates and revenue implications and bill impacts for each individual rate class.
do you have a recommendation for the commission to order schedules for the next rate case if georgia Power elects to continue or modify its alternate rate plan?
Yes.  I recommend that the Commission order in this case a requirement for Georgia Power in its next rate case to include the proposed rates, tariff rate schedules, and the schedules found within MFR Items F-3 and F-4 at the revenue levels actually proposed by the Company.  If the Company is proposing a one-time, levelized, first-year base rate adjustment, as it has in this case, then the Company should provide additional corresponding schedules that match its levelized revenue proposal.  If the Company is proposing multi-year base revenue adjustments, then it should provide in its application what those rates are, along with the corresponding schedules.  Without those matching schedules, it is not clear what the Company is asking the Commission to approve for implementation in each of the effective rate years.
are you asking the Commission to change the mfrs?
No, just to order the Company to provide schedules found within the MFRs that match the revenue proposal(s) of the Company.
does this conclude your testimony?
Yes.

II. PURPOSE AND SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATIONS
What is the purpose of your testimony in this proceeding?
The purpose of my testimony is to recommend, for ratemaking purposes in this case, an overall ROR and a fair ROE for Georgia Power. I accept the Company’s capital structure for ratemaking purposes. My recommendation for the ROE is set forth according to the standards in Bluefield Water Works v. Public Service Commission, 262 U.S. 679, 692-93 (1923) and Federal Power Commission v. Hope Natural Gas Co., 320 U.S. 591, 605 (1944). In Bluefield and Hope, the U.S. Supreme Court established the principle that a public utility may be allowed to earn a return comparable to a return on investments in other enterprises having similar risks that allow the utility, under efficient management, to maintain financial integrity, the opportunity to attract capital on reasonable terms, and to maintain a satisfactory credit rating.
What are the ROE and ROR that the company is requesting in its filing?
The Company’s cost of capital witness, Mr. Coyne, is recommending that the Commission allow an opportunity for Georgia Power to earn an ROR of 7.70%. This ROR is based on a regulated capital structure of 56% equity and 44% long-term debt, with a cost of debt of 3.53% and an ROE of 11.0%. Although he is not recommending a ROE range, Mr. Coyne’s recommended ROE of 11.0% is within his models’ range of 8.99% to 13.55%. This proposed overall cost of capital is based on the average of Georgia Power’s estimated capitalization for the 13 months ending July 31, 2023. These recommendations are shown in Table 1.

should the Commission REJECT MR. Coyne’s roe recommendation?
Yes. Mr. Coyne’s ROE recommendation does not comport with his own data. An objective analysis of his data would yield a lower ROE. Mr. Coyne skews his analysis through the over-emphasis and overreliance on inputs with an upward bias. For example, he relies exclusively on earnings growth estimates, which has the effect of inflating his Discounted Cash Flow (“DCF”) model results as well as his Capital Asset Pricing Model (“CAPM”) results. Relying exclusively on earnings growth estimates also inflates his estimated total market risk premium, a key input in his DCF-based CAPM. IV. RATE OF RETURN AND CAPITAL STRUCTURE
Please summarize the process of estimating the Company’s COSt of capital.
The overall cost of capital is comprised of the costs of long-term debt and equity capital. The first step in estimating the cost of capital is to determine the appropriate capital structure. Long-term debt costs are computed using the Company’s actual embedded costs for a certain time period (e.g., the test year ending July 31, 2023). Unlike the debt component of the capital structure, the equity cost rate must be estimated. The overall WACC is computed by weighting individual costs of debt and equity capital by their respective proportions of total capitalization and summing the result.
The capital structure is important because investors may view a high reliance on debt as risky (referred to as financial or leverage risk). Investors may view a high reliance on debt as risky because it can contribute to earnings volatility. This can thereby lead to a higher required ROE relative to similar investment opportunities. However, excessive equity, while reducing financial risk, may increase the overall cost of capital (and therefore return on rate base) for customers.

Please discuss the different types of risk that a regulated monopoly, such as an electric utility, may face.
An investor’s expected return on an investment is composed of the risk-free rate and different types of risk, to include inflation risk, interest rate risk, business risk, financial risk, and regulatory risk. 
The risk-free rate is the level of return investors can achieve without assuming any risk. In general, most investors agree that a U.S. Treasury bond is an asset perceived by the market as having relatively less risk than other market instruments because the federal government’s access to tax proceeds to fulfill its debt obligations and strong credit rating makes Treasury securities practically default-free. However, Treasury bonds are not absolutely risk-free because they incorporate a risk premium associated with interest rate risk, which is the premium investors require to compensate them for the forgone opportunity cost of an alternative, higher interest rate later. 
Inflation risk, also called purchasing power risk, is the chance that the cash flows from an investment will not be worth as much in the future because of changes in purchasing power due to inflation.
Interest rate risk is the risk that arises for investors from the variability in returns caused by fluctuating interest rates, which depends on how sensitive an asset’s price is to interest rate changes in the market. For bonds, for example, its sensitivity depends on the bond’s time to maturity and the coupon rate of the bond. 
Business risk, as perceived by investors, includes all the operating factors that increase the probability that expected future cash flows accruing to investors may not be realized. Business risk would include such factors as sales volatility and operating leverage. A utility’s business risk is a function of factors such as customer base diversity, necessary capital expenditures, the regional and national economy, and the regulatory environment in which the utility operates. 
Financial risk relates to the capital structure of a company, including its fixed contractual obligations and ability to pay interest on its debt and refinance that debt when it is due. Credit-rating agencies assess the financial health of a company through the use of key financial ratios that measure the extent to which a company can pay its debt, including principal and interest. WHAT IS THE PREDOMINANT ROE MODEL UTILIZED BY REGULATORY BODIES IN THE UNITED STATES?

Although I use variants of the Constant-Growth DCF model, the CAPM and Empirical CAPM (“ECAPM”), and the Comparable Earnings Model (“CEM”) to provide an average ROE of 9.38%, I rely on my Constant-Growth DCF to form the basis of my recommendation of a 9.47% ROE for Georgia Power. 
A. Constant-Growth Discounted Cash Flow Model
Please describe the Constant-Growth DCF model.
The Constant-Growth DCF model is based on the dividend discount model first proposed by J.B. Williams in 1938. The model is based on the premise that since cash dividends are the only income from a share of stock held to infinity, the value of that stock will be the present value of its stream of dividends, where the discount rate is the market’s required return. The model can be modified to take into account the (more common) situation whereby shares of stock are bought and sold, producing capital gains income in addition to dividend income. In order to simplify the mathematics of the model, expected future dividends are represented by applying a constant growth rate to the current observable dividend. It recognizes that common equity capital is riskier than debt from an investor’s perspective because debt holders are typically paid before shareholders, and as such, investors require higher returns on stocks than on bonds to be compensated for the additional risk. E.Comparable Earnings Model
DO YOU EMPLOY OTHER METHODOLOGIES FOR YOUR ROE ESTIMATE FOR THE COMPANY? 
Yes. I use the Comparable Earnings Model (“CEM”). A CEM estimate is derived from the “corresponding risk” standard of the Hope and Bluefield cases and is based on the economic concept of opportunity cost. The cost of capital is an opportunity cost whereby a company’s return represents a return available from alternative investments of similar risk. I use the CEM by examining realized ROEs for my proxy group and comparing investor acceptance of these returns via corresponding marketto-book (“M/B”) ratios. The M/B ratio is the stock price divided by the BVPS and shows the degree to which a given level of ROE equals the cost of capital. An M/B of greater than one (1) shows that a company can attract new equity capital without dilution. 
Using market-based information via the M/B ratios, I show that historical ROEs have attracted investors to purchase shares of utility stock. I calculate historical M/B ratios using average annual stock prices and the Value Line reported BVPS data from 2012 through 2021. My results show that the companies in my proxy group were successful in attracting investors, given reported historical, book value-derived ROEs. Even in cases where a company’s ROEs were as low as 7.23% (as in the case of Duke Energy Corp.), that company’s stock was valued higher than book value, as demonstrated by M/B ratios of greater than one (1) at 1.35. For my proxy group, the median M/B ratio is 1.74 and the median historical ROE is 9.85%. By way of comparison, the median Value Line forecasted ROE (2025-2027) for my sample is 10.50%.

what are flotation costs?
Flotation costs are the costs associated with the sale of common stock issuances that include out-of-pocket expenditures for preparation, filing, underwriting, and other issuance costs.
why does mr. coyne believe that an adjustment to his roe recommendation to account for flotation costs is necessary?
Mr. Coyne states that the allowed ROE is the only ratemaking mechanism through which it is appropriate to recover these necessary costs. Flotation costs are reflected on a utility’s balance sheet as “paid in capital” and are not expensed on the utility’s income statement. When a company issues common stock, flotation costs are incurred and netted against the proceeds from the issuance, reducing the amount available for investment in rate base by the amount of the flotation costs. If Georgia Power is denied the opportunity to recover its prudently incurred flotation costs through its allowed ROE, actual returns will fall short, and equity share value will be diluted.
Do you agree with mr. coyne’s adjustment for flotation costs?
No, I do not. Mr. Coyne’s estimate of a flotation cost adjustment is based on a review of other companies’ typical flotation costs and academic literature and is not based on the Company or the parent’s actual common stock issuance costs. Although proxy group data are used for estimating the ROE for Georgia Power because the Company does not issue stock, when determining flotation costs, one should look to whether Georgia Power’s parent company, Southern Company, is planning to issue new stock, and not the proxy group for the flotation costs determination. Inclusion of such flotation costs in the setting of base rates in this case should only be allowed for the public issuance of common stock by Southern Company (and therefore the incurrence of flotation expenses) in the foreseeable future. The setting of the ROE is, after all, a prospective concept.
Does southern company plan to issue new stock over the test period?

Value Line also projects no increase in Southern Company’s outstanding shares during the test period ending July 31, 2023, to remain at 1,070 million shares from 2022 to 2023. In fact, Value Line projects that the parent will not increase shares over the next five years.

I recommend that the Commission reject Mr. Coyne’s flotation cost adjustment.

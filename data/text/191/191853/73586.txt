 1   Q:   Do Georgia Power’s proposals allow for site hosts to choose the charging equipment

 2        installed on their property?

 3   A:   Yes. Based on discussions with Georgia Power, it is my understanding that site hosts will

 4        continue to have the ability to choose their charging equipment. I use the term “site host”

 5        to refer to the owner or lessor of the property on which an EV charging station is located.

 6        Site hosts include residential customers; owners of multi-family housing (MFH);

 7        commercial customers that offer charging to the public, their customers, and/or their

 8        employees; fleet owners; and government entities.

 9   Q:   Why is site host choice important?

10   A:   Site host choice is the critical program design element that allows customers to enjoy the

11        benefits of competition, including innovation, cost-competitiveness, and a variety of

12        products and services to satisfy a variety of needs and preferences. In the competitive

13        marketplace for EV charging services, the ability of site hosts to select the technologies

14        they prefer in an open market, invest their own capital, seek any incentives available

15        through public agencies or utilities, and, in the case of commercial stations, offer

16        competitive charging services to attract drivers and recoup necessary expenses, is

17        paramount. Ensuring that site hosts can choose the charging solution that works best for

18        them ensures that the competitive dynamics that exist in the absence of a utility program

19        will function within the confines of the utility program, to the benefit of customers.

20   Q:   Has Georgia Power proposed to increase investments in community electric vehicle

21        charging facilities?

 1        IV.       Enable Site Hosts to Price EV Charging Services on a $/kWh basis.

 2   Q:         What will you address in this section of your testimony?

 3   A:         In this section of my testimony, I will address the ability of EV charging station operators

 4              (site hosts) to sell EV charging services by the kilowatt-hour in Georgia. As I will discuss,

 5              providing regulatory certainty to non-utilities that they can charge a $/kWh price for EV

 6              charging is crucial to encouraging EV charging station deployment and EV adoption.

 7   Q:         Can non-utility site hosts currently sell EV charging services on a $/kWh basis in

 8              Georgia Power’s Service Territory?

 9   A:         No. A non-utility site host would risk violating Georgia Power’s prohibition on resale of

10              electricity, which appears in Georgia Power’s tariff.4 Because historically the only

11              commodity or service priced on a $/kWh basis was electricity, any person or entity using

12              a $/kWh pricing arrangement is generally considered to be reselling electricity. The current

13              uncertainty and risk that a site host would violate Georgia Power’s tariff by charging

14              drivers to charge their EVs on a $/kWh basis effectively prevents site hosts from doing so

15              in Georgia. For the reasons I will discuss, ChargePoint recommends that the Commission

16              clarify that EV charging station site hosts do not resell electricity.

17   Q:         Do EV charging stations resell electricity?

18   A:         No. However, as discussed above, any $/kWh pricing arrangement is generally deemed to

19              be a resale of electricity, which is why EV charging station site hosts risk violating Georgia

20              Power’s tariff if they sell EV charging services on a $/kWh basis. In reality, EV charging

 1        stations use electricity to provide a service; specifically, the service of charging the battery

 2        of an EV. While the amount of energy stored in a battery is measured in kilowatt-hours,

 3        the EV charging station is not reselling electricity but rather using electricity to perform

 4        the value-added service of charging a battery (in which energy is stored).

 5                The nature of this service is entirely distinct from the way that a utility sells

 6        electricity. A utility sells electricity as a commodity that a customer can use for a wide-

 7        variety of purposes, such as powering an air conditioner, cooking, or charging their smart

 8        phone. By contrast, an EV charging station uses electricity for a single purpose – charging

 9        the battery of an EV – and the EV driver can only use the energy stored in the EV’s battery

10        for a single purpose – operating the EV.

11                Like EV charging station site hosts, many businesses use electricity to provide

12        services to the public. For example, a laundromat uses electricity to run its business, but it

13        would be inaccurate to say that the laundromat resells electricity to its customers that the

14        customers use to wash and dry their clothes. Rather, the more accurate description of the

15        laundromat’s business is that it uses electricity to provide the services of washing and

16        drying customers’ clothes. By the same token, it is inaccurate to say that an EV charging

17        station site host is reselling electricity to EV drivers. Rather, the accurate way to describe

18        the transaction between a site host and an EV driver is that the site host uses electricity to

19        provide the service of charging the battery of an EV.

20   Q:   If site hosts cannot charge drivers on a $/kWh basis, how do they price charging

21        services?

22   A:   The most common alternative pricing arrangement non-utility site hosts use is to charge

23        drivers by the minute ($/minute). Other site hosts provide charging for free as an amenity

 1        to support their primary business, such as a retail store or a restaurant, but free charging is

 2        typically limited to Level 2 chargers and is not a sustainable business model for deploying

 3        DC fast chargers.

 4   Q:   Why does it make sense to allow EV charging station site hosts to charge EV drivers

 5        on a $/kWh basis?

 6   A:   EV charging station site hosts provide a service: namely, the service of charging the

 7        batteries of EVs. The amount of energy that is stored in a battery is measured in kilowatt-

 8        hours. Allowing EV charging station site hosts to price charging services on a $/kWh basis

 9        will allow site hosts to charge drivers accurately and fairly for the battery charging service

10        they provide.

11   Q:   What are the challenges that arise from charging EV drivers by the minute?

12   A:   A per-minute pricing arrangement does not account for the amount of energy that is

13        delivered to an EV’s battery. Because different EV models have different maximum

14        charging speeds, billing on a per-minute basis leads EV drivers to pay varying costs for the

15        same amount of energy consumed. For example, it may take only 30 minutes for a newer

16        model EV with a high maximum charging speed to add 30 kWh of charge at a DC fast

17        charger. However, at that same fast charger, it could take 90 minutes for an older EV with

18        a lower maximum charging speed to add the same 30 kWh of charge. If the charger used a

19        $/minute pricing scheme, the older EV would pay three times as much to add the exact

20        same amount of charge to its battery. In addition to being disconnected from the amount

21        of energy delivered to the battery the per-minute pricing scheme has the effect of negatively

22        impacting those EV drivers with older vehicle models.

 1   Q:      Have other states addressed this issue?

 2   A:      Yes. EV charging station site hosts may charge EV drivers on a $/kWh basis in each of the

 3           43 states that have addressed this issue, as well as in the District of Columbia and Puerto

 4           Rico. Please see ChargePoint Exhibit 1.2.

 5                   It is worth noting that the uncertainty and risk faced by site hosts preventing them

 6           from charging on a $/kWh basis has arisen in different ways in different states. In some

 7           states this issue has been resolved through a statutory change, in other states through a

 8           statewide regulatory commission proceeding, and other states through a utility-specific

 9           proceeding (such as this docket). Nevertheless, in each of the 43 states and the District of

10           Columbia the result is the same: EV charging station site hosts may charge EV drivers

11           using their preferred pricing arrangement, including on a $/kWh basis.

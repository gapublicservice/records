 1   II.   BACKGROUND ON ELECTRIC VEHICLE CHARGING

 2   Q.    What is the purpose of this section of your testimony?

 3   A.    In this section of my testimony, I provide a brief overview of the EV charging landscape

 4         across the nation and in Georgia. More specifically, I summarize the different types of EV

 5         charging infrastructure, explain why it is important to broadly deploy public DCFC

 6         infrastructure, and describe the state of the nascent charging market in Georgia.

 7   Q.    Please describe the different types of EV charging infrastructure.

 8   A.    There are three main types of EV charging infrastructure, each with distinct capabilities:

 9         •   “Level 1” charging delivers electricity though a 120 volt plug and is most often used in

10             homes through an existing conventional outlet;

11         •   “Level 2” charging typically provides a full charge in 4 to 8 hours and is used in

12             locations with longer dwell times, such as workplaces, homes, parks, or other

13             destinations where drivers may spend several hours;

14         •   Public or commercial Direct Current Fast Charging or “DCFC” is suited for quick

15             charge needs in and around cities, towns, and suburbs and along high-traffic travel

16             corridors. DCFC stations are located at or near places where drivers live, drive, and

17             shop, including retail locations, restaurants, grocery stores, and other locations where

18             an EV driver will spend usually under an hour of time. EVgo’s primary interest in this

19             case is the discussion of DCFC in Georgia Power’s service territory.

20   Q.    What types of needs does DCFC serve, and how does that impact station siting?

21   A.    DCFC serves a variety of needs for EV drivers. In addition to providing a quick charge to

22         EV drivers on longer road trips, public DCFC also plays an important role in serving EV

23         drivers’ primary charging needs in dense urban and suburban areas, where not every home


                                                    6

 1            has dedicated parking. In fact, according to the International Council on Clean

 2            Transportation, apartment-dwelling EV drivers living in multi-unit dwellings (MUDs) rely

 3            on public charging stations to serve 50-80% of their charging needs.4 Further, a study of

 4            EV charging data from the University of California – Los Angeles concluded that

 5            “[i]nfrastructure planners should also note MUD users’ stronger preferences for DCFC

 6            locations near home” and “MUD residents who drive EVs are, on average, heavier users

 7            of DCFC than non-MUD residents.”5

 8                     To effectively serve the different needs of EV drivers, therefore, it is important that

 9            DCFC be sited not only along high-traffic transit corridors, at service plazas, and at rest

10            areas along interstates and along major highways (traditional DCFC deployments), but also

11            in and close to areas with high concentrations of MUD residents, among other locations.

12            Importantly, whether a DCFC is located along a transit corridor or in a dense urban area,

13            siting DCFC in and around existing amenities (e.g., retail, grocery, restaurants) allows for

14            charging to be integrated into everyday activities, which enhances the customer experience

15            and facilitates EV adoption and use.

 9   III.    MAKE-READY PROGRAM

10   Q.      What is “make-ready” infrastructure?

11   A.      “Make-ready” infrastructure refers to the electrical equipment necessary to operate a

12           charging station. This can include sub-panels, main-panels, conductors, wiring,

13           transformers, and other equipment on both the customer- and utility-side of the meter.13

14           Utility investments, or incentives for private investment, in make-ready infrastructure

15           bolster market deployment of charging stations. Through make-ready programs, utilities

16           might, for instance, invest in rate-based distribution upgrades and branch line extensions,

17           while leaving investments in chargers, charger ownership, operation and maintenance,

18           marketing, customer service, and network operation to experienced private sector

19           providers. By approving make-ready programs, utility commissions can attract and

 7   IV.        CHARGE IT – ELECTRIC VEHICLE RIDER

 8   Q.         Please describe Georgia Power’s proposed Charge It – Electric Vehicle rate rider.

 9   A.         As Company witness Legg explains, the Charge It – Electric Vehicle (CIEV) rider will:

10                        “modify how the Power & Light tariffs price commercial EV
11                       charging loads. Specifically, the new Charge It rider will modify the
12                       monthly billing demands by using Billing Demand Adjustment
13                       Factors as specified in the tariff. Charge It is a temporary rider that
14                       will expire either at the end of 48 months or when the rolling 12-
15                       month load factor for the account reaches 15% or greater –
16                       whichever comes first. Participation in Charge It is available to new,
17                       separately metered charging loads that are served on Georgia
18                       Power’s system after January 1, 2023.”44
19
20   Q.         How can the CIEV rider complement the make-ready program to enable a

21              competitive charging market?

22   A.         The CIEV rider is a well-structured rate that offers an excellent opportunity to encourage

23              additional investment in EV charging infrastructure if implemented appropriately, and

24              EVgo commends Georgia Power for this aspect of its proposal. The procurement of power

25              represents a significant portion of operating costs borne by EV charging operators, so the

26              availability of Commercial EV rates – rates that account for the uniqueness of DCFC loads

27              – factors into the decision making for developing new charging stations. In particular, the

28              demand charge component of traditional commercial rates can present a barrier to

29              increased deployment of public DCFC infrastructure. The CIEV rider lowers that barrier


     44
          Direct Testimony of Larry T. Legg at 17.


                                                           22

 1           by reducing the demand charge borne by public DCFC operators for a limited period of

 2           time. The CIEV rider can therefore be a valuable catalyst for greater investments in EV

 3           charging infrastructure.

 4                   Typically, in well designed and comprehensive utility EV charging programs, both

 5           EV rates and make-ready are complementary programs. They work hand in hand because

 6           each program addresses different aspects of the economics of public charging. In the

 7           examples of successful make-ready programs discussed previously, many of these utilities

 8           have both a make-ready program and EV rates.45 Therefore, it is essential that both the

 9           Make-Ready program and CIEV rider work in parallel and complement each other. This is

10           easily achieved when they have consistent eligibility requirements. Both programs are

11           essential and complementary pieces of a comprehensive transportation electrification

12           program and are not interchangeable. Unfortunately, as proposed, the CIEV rider will be

13           broadly available to new, separately-metered charging stations, whereas the make-ready

14           program will be limited to a subset of charging station deployments (as discussed above).

15           This misalignment risks compromising the value of both program offerings; the CIEV rate

16           is unlikely to realize its full potential unless Georgia Power makes its make-ready program

17           more broadly available, and vice-versa.

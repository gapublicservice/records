Q.WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF YOUR TESTIMONY?
A.The purpose of the panel’s testimony is to provide recommendations to the Commission in the interest of the City governments included in the Coalition and the approximately 1 million GPC ratepayers we represent. These measured recommendations can help increase the impact of Georgia Power’s programs and support our communities’ transitions to a clean energy future. 
Specifically, our testimony provides recommendations on four topics included in the Company’s Rate Case filing. The first is to support the company’s expansion of its electric transportation programs and propose changes to the “Make Ready” program to make smaller sites eligible for work. The second is to propose changes to the Community Solar program to increase participation and provide a viable option for those who cannot benefit from rooftop solar due to the ownership, condition, or shading of their home. The third is to ensure that all commercial customers have access to hourly interval data free of charge, as approved in the 2019 rate case. The final recommendation is to reject the Smart Usage tariff, which includes a confusing and punitive demand charge, as the default rate structure for new premises. 
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COALITION’S RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING THE EV MAKE READY PROGRAM.
A.The Coalition supports the Company’s request for $81 million over the next three years for the EV Make Ready Program. This program has been a tremendous benefit to our member governments as we seek to transition our fleets, and it has also supported our efforts to provide public charging for our citizens. 
Q.WHICH OF THE COALITION MEMBER GOVERNMENTS HAVE RECEIVED MAKE READY FUNDING?
A.The City of Savannah and DeKalb County have received Make Ready Funding. The City of Decatur and City of Atlanta have applied for Make Ready funding at one site and three sites, respectively, but have not yet received funds. 
Q.MR. DEFFLEY, GIVEN SAVANNAH’S EXPERIENCE WITH THE MAKE READY PROGRAM, WOULD YOU RECOMMEND ANY CHANGES? 
A.Yes. The program currently prioritizes locations that seek to install 5 or more chargers at the time the Make Ready work is completed. This makes it hard for smaller sites to compete for and receive funding, which in turn makes it less likely that those locations will be able to provide charging. 
This has implications for equitable access to electric vehicles. As an example, the City of Savannah is applying for a grant to establish EV carshares at community centers in USDA defined food deserts. Because there will only be one car per community center, and because many of the community centers have limited parking, this project is not a good candidate for the Make Ready program as it is currently operated. However, this project should be supported because it will provide critical transportation support to communities in need and increase community comfort with EVs. Both academic and market research, studies have demonstrated that customers who ride in an EV are more likely to purchase one than those who have not.
Q.IS THE REQUESTED AMOUNT OF FUNDING APPROPRIATE GIVEN CURRENT RATES ADOPTION OF ELECTRIC VEHICLES?
A.Yes. The requested amount is appropriate given 1) the over-subscription of the program for the 2019 rate case cycle 2) the influx of federal dollars for charging through the NEVI program, Clean School Bus Program, and other programs, which will likely bring additional competitive pressure to the Make Ready program 3) the positive financial impact that this program is having on non-participating customers, which will only grow as charger utilization increases and  4) increasing requirements from vehicle manufacturers for dealerships to install charging infrastructure in order to continue selling electric vehicles. 
Q.HOW DOES THE INFLUX OF FEDERAL FUNDING FOR EV CHARGING AFFECT THE MAKE READY PROGRAM?
A.Several federal agencies are in the process of implementing provisions from the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law that create funding streams for electric charging in Georgia. Examples include 1) DOT’s National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure “NEVI” program, which is providing $135 million in formula funding to Georgia over the next five years for fast charging stations along major highways; 2) DOT’s Charging and Fueling Infrastructure program, a $2.5 billion competitive funding opportunity for rural and disadvantaged communities that might be missed by the NEVI program; 3) EPA’s Clean School Bus program, which provides funds for electric school bus acquisition; and 4) the Congestion Mitigation Air Quality Program, which provides funds for public transit buses and other medium and heavy duty vehicles. Each of these funding streams is expressly intended to be leveraged with other funding streams, including utility funds. 
Together, these programs will require a tremendous amount of Make Ready work. For example, the NEVI program for highway fast charging mandates that each charging station have at least four charging ports that can each deliver up to 150 kW simultaneously, or 600 kW total. That is a tremendous amount of power demand that few sites are prepared to meet in their current state. These Make Ready costs will only grow if infrastructure is “future proofed,” or sized in anticipation of the EVs of the future. As an example, while the NEVI program requires chargers to provide 150 kW of power, vehicles and chargers are already being mass marketed for charging at 300 kW, which can provide 62 miles of range in as little as four minutes.
To summarize, the anticipated pace and scale of charging buildout, as well as the need to make prudent, future-proofed investments, necessitates the significant increase in funding for the Make Ready program that the Company requested. 

Q.ARE THERE ANY OTHER FACTORS THAT THE COMMISSION AND THE COMPANY SHOULD CONSIDER IN LIGHT OF THE PREVIOUSLY DISCUSSED FEDERAL FUNDING OPPORTUNITIES?

A.Yes. As discussed previously, federal funding will enable charging deployments at a rate and scale that we have never seen. As this occurs, Georgia Power will likely be required to make investments in its transmission and distribution systems to accommodate the additional load. These “major grid upgrades,” as the Federal Highway Administration calls them, are explicitly not eligible for NEVI funding. The same is true for the Clean School Bus Program, which requires that funds be spent entirely behind the meter. In other words, unless paid for with state matching funds, these investments will need to be made by Georgia Power and approved by the Commission. If a different approach is taken, and the Commission decides that grant recipients like school systems and transit systems should pay for all or part of the needed grid upgrades, some grant recipients may be forced to decline their awards. 

As with the Make Ready funding, it is the Coalition’s recommendation that these investments not only be made by Georgia Power, but also be “future proofed.” That is, the system should be built out to not only serve four 150 kW fast chargers per 50 miles or the maximum 25 school buses that can be received through the Clean School Bus Program, but to serve the expected number and capacity of chargers that will be needed in 2030 or 2035.

In some ways, the NEVI requirements are already somewhat obsolete. According to Bloomberg New Energy Finance (“BNEF”), national EV sales are expected to hit 52% by 2030, due to the incentives in the Inflation Reduction Act. If sales reach or exceed that projection, four chargers per 50 miles won’t even begin to meet the demand for fast charging. 
The Commission should also consider another form of “future proofing,” preparing for and supporting vehicles and chargers with vehicle to grid (“V2G”) capability. While not strictly necessary for charging, V2G capability could provide significant benefits to the utility system and non-participating ratepayers. School buses are particularly well suited to serve as distributed energy storage resources because they are largely idle in the hot summer months when the grid is most stressed. A good model is Dominion Energy’s V2G program in Virginia, which currently has 50 buses enrolled. This program is designed to scale to 1000 buses to ultimately provide 105 MWh of storage, or enough to power 10,000 homes. 

Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COALITION’S RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING THE COMMUNITY SOLAR PROGRAM.
A.The Coalition recommends that the Commission reject Georgia Power’s request to raise the price of a 1 kW subscription block from $24.99 to $27.99. The Coalition further recommends that the Commission direct Georgia Power to file a new subscription price that is based on the cost of the program, rather than avoidance of lost revenues. There are four key reasons to support this change. 
1) Participation has been underwhelming, at best, and will continue to be underwhelming until the program provides some potential upside to customers. As of February of 2022, only 1954 out of 8000 blocks had been subscribed, a decrease from a high of 2013 subscribers in December of 2021. The Company’s proposed solutions—opening up the program to General Service Customers and seeking subsidies from corporations and non-profits for income-qualified customers—do little to address the core issue of poor value proposition and as such make it unlikely that the program will be fully subscribed, much less grow the program as proposed in the Company’s 2022 IRP filing. 
2) Community solar provides benefits to all ratepayers. Until the 2022 IRP, all solar resources had to demonstrate that they would produce energy at a rate below the Company’s long-term avoided cost to be approved. In other words, those resources had to put downward pressure on rates for all customers, or they would not be built. This means that the solar resources that are used for the community solar program were deemed to provide benefits to all customers. 3) As the primary alternative to rooftop solar, community solar should have at least as much upside as rooftop solar, if not more, because it can take advantage of economies of scale and because it can be strategically located by the utility to meet specific system needs. However, while rooftop solar customers pay an average cost per kWh that is significantly less than the average cost of grid energy, community solar participants have paid an average per kWh premium of [X]% over grid energy under the R tariff over the past 12 months. Notably, this premium is higher for subscribers who use less energy than those who use more, because higher users can offset higher cost kilowatt hours under the Company’s inclining block rates. 
4) The lost revenues approach to pricing defeats the purpose of participating. By enrolling in a community solar program, a customer is effectively contracting for a dedicated renewable energy supply for a portion of his or her consumption. In doing so, and assuming all the accompanying risks, the customer is also effectively opting out of the generic mix of energy for the covered consumption. As such, it is unreasonable to charge the participant for fuel, environmental compliance, or nuclear cost recovery, since those relate to generation resources that the solar is meant to replace. 
Further, because the community solar subscription fee is based upon an expected amount of production, there is a very real risk that customers could pay for fuel, environmental compliance, and the like twice if production is less than projected. 
Q.WHY IS THE COMMUNITY SOLAR PROGRAM IMPORTANT TO THE COALITION?
A.As local governments who have adopted aggressive clean energy goals, we are very concerned about our citizens’ access to robust, financially feasible clean energy programs. While rooftop solar receives a lot of attention, rooftop solar is difficult or impossible for certain groups, including occupants of multi-family housing, renters, and households who live in shaded or structurally deficient homes. 
We are also interested in community solar as a potential means of reducing energy burden. In other parts of the country, community solar is one of the primary means of making solar more accessible for low-income families. However, that has not been the case with Georgia Power’s program, which GPC openly describes as a premium program. The Company seeks to address this criticism through a program that subsidizes a majority of the cost for low-income customers through corporate sponsorship and external philanthropic donations. However, this does not address the core issue of poor value proposition. Asking companies to pay $127/REC as a form of corporate good citizenship is simply diverting much-needed funding away from causes that provide more assistance to low-income customers or more impact to the climate. 
Q.WHAT SPECIFIC RECOMMENDATIONS DOES THE COALITION HAVE FOR THE PRICING AND STRUCTURE OF THE PROGRAM?

A.The Coalition is open to several options, so long as customer costs are more proportionate to the program and resource costs. One option is to maintain the existing structure where customers pay a fixed price per kW block and receive credits through virtual net metering, but reduce the fixed price to exclude “lost revenues” related to fuel, environmental compliance cost recovery (ECCR), and nuclear construction cost recovery (NCCR). 

To understand what this option would look like in practice, it is helpful to first understand how the Company calculates a customer’s bill. First, the customer is billed for his or her energy use on either an inclining block rate (in the summer) or a declining block rate (in the winter). An inclining block scale means that customers pay more per kWh as they use more energy, and a declining block rate means that they pay less as they use more. Across both seasons, Georgia Power uses blocks that go from 0-650 kWh, 651 kWh – 1000 kWh, and 1000+ kWh. The usage within each block is multiplied times the rate for that block, and the results are summed to calculate a customer’s “base bill.”

Once the base bill is calculated, a percentage adjustment is applied to the base bill for Environmental Cost Recovery (ECCR), Nuclear Construction Cost Recovery (NCCR), and DSM. Then, the customer is charged for fuel at a cents/kWh rate. Finally, the sum of the adjusted base bill and the fuel bill is subjected to a percentage adjustment for the municipal franchise fee and sales taxes, if applicable. 
 
To justify its proposed price of $27.99 per 1 kW block of community solar, Georgia Power calculated the delta between the annual bill of a typical customer without a 1 kW community solar subscription and the typical bill of a customer with a 1 kW subscription for every year between 2023 and 2025, inclusive. This delta averaged REDACTED. Excluding the municipal franchise fee, this delta falls to an average of REDACTED per month. The Company then calculates the delta for fuel, which averages to REDACTED per month. Adjusting the remaining REDACTED to exclude ECCR and NCCR, costs that are driven by fuels unrelated to solar, results in a delta of REDACTED. Thus, the Commission should consider adopting a per kW subscription price of REDACTED. 

Another option would be to maintain the existing structure, but “lock in” a customer’s subscription price at the current rate of $24.99/kW for the duration of his or her participation. While this would not provide immediate financial benefits like the first option, it would provide a valuable hedge against future rate increases, particularly those tied to fuel, nuclear, and environmental compliance costs. 

Q.OTHER THAN FINANCIAL BARRIERS, ARE THERE ANY OTHER BARRIERS TO PARTICIPATION IN THE COMMUNITY SOLAR PROGRAM?

A. Yes. The current Community Solar tariff specifies that the program is only available to customers on the R tariff. If that tariff is being closed to new premises, customers in new premises will be left out. While these homes should be more structurally sufficient, they could have the same ownership issues that many existing premises have.

Possible solutions include 1) developing a customer-specific allocator for time of use (TOU) customers to allocate their virtual net metering credits between the peak and off-peak periods or 2) expanding eligibility for the Community Solar tariff to Pre-Pay customers. 

Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COALITION’S RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING HOURLY USAGE DATA.
A.Georgia Power is seeking $786,000 in cost recovery for expenses incurred in providing hourly usage data to all residential and commercial customers, as required in the 2019 rate case. While no Georgia Power witness seemed to be fully prepared to speak on this issue, Witness Legg suggested that this information is currently available to all residential customers and certain commercial customers through the My Power Usage platform.  
Over the past couple of years, our governments have sought this information for a variety of reasons, including rate selection, energy load forecasting, informing more energy efficient behaviors, solar sizing, and Energy Savings Performance Contracting (ESPC) projects. When we have inquired about getting this information, the answers have been convoluted and inconsistent. Sometimes, we have been told that the data simply isn’t collected, either because the meter has not been programmed to do so or is not capable of doing so. Other times, we have been told that the data is collected, but is only stored for a certain period of time, generally a few months. Still other times, we have been told that it is collected and stored indefinitely, but that we have to pay for a premium subscription of Energy Direct to access it.
Our best assessment of the situation is that some meters are not automatically configured to collect interval data, while others are. Savannah, for example, has been told that no interval data exists for certain meters receiving service under PLS-C and PLM-C tariffs. Follow up conversations with Company representatives have suggested that this is due to a decision by the Company to install the cheapest meter that can capture the necessary readings for the rate. 
Given this assessment, the Coalition has four recommendations: 
1) GPC should ensure that all commercial customers, regardless of their rate schedule, have free access to hourly usage data through My Power Usage or Energy Direct. If this requires the replacement or reprogramming of existing meters, this should be done promptly at the Company’s cost, and all future meters should have the capability and programming that is required to collect this data. 
2) Customers should have the ability to export a full year of hourly usage data as a .csv file for analysis. This is a shortfall of the My Power Usage platform, which currently limits exports to 33 days of data at a time, as shown in the screen capture below.


3) GPC should provide clear messaging to account managers and other key contacts in the organization to ensure that questions about data access are answered consistently and correctly. 
4) GPC should make it simple to share hourly usage information with third parties as needed. 
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COALITION’S RECOMMENDATIONS REGARDING THE DEFAULT RESIDENTIAL TARIFF.
A.The Company is proposing to close the R tariff to new residential customers and to place those customers on a deceptively named “Smart Usage” tariff instead. This tariff is described as a three-part rate, which includes a fixed customer charge, a time-varying energy rate, and a demand charge. The Company spins this as providing customers three ways to save, when the reality is that even the most sophisticated customers struggle to comprehend demand.
A large body of academic research has demonstrated that customers struggle to understand which appliances use the most energy and which use the least.  This pattern holds true for both representative samples of customers and more motivated or sophisticated samples of customers. 
The Coalition recommends that the Commission reject the Smart Usage tariff as the default tariff and either maintain the R tariff as the default or replace it with a rate that is more understandable to customers and more aligned with system needs, like an energy-only time of use rate. 
Q.DOES THE COALITION BELIEVE THAT RATES LIKE THE SMART USAGE RATE WILL BE A POSITIVE FOR THE CLIMATE AND DECARBONIZATION? 
A.No. Each of the five governments in the Coalition has a community-wide clean energy goal that will depend on electrification of many end-uses, including space heating, water heating, cooking, and transportation.  Residential demand charges encourage the opposite—the continued use of natural gas. 
 Q.DOES THE COALITION HAVE ANY OTHER CONCERNS WITH THE DECISION TO MAKE THE SMART USAGE RATE THE DEFAULT RESIDENTIAL RATE?

Q.IS THE SMART USAGE RATE ACTUALLY BETTER AT ASSIGNING COSTS AND SENDING PRICE SIGNALS THAN ENERGY ONLY RATES?
A.No. Consider the scenario that Witness Legg developed: 
“To the extent that you would have two customers who use the exact amount of energy – same amount of energy – but one of them peaks, their peak demand for the month is 5 kW and the other one is 10 kW. The one that’s 10 kW is causing more cost because we have to build more infrastructure because they’re causing a higher peak demand. But on the R rate, if they use the same energy and have different demands they pay the exact same amount. That’s a bad price signal.”
There are a couple problems with this example. First, it assumes that individual customer demands generally coincide with the system peak, which isn’t necessarily the case. As shown in the illustrative figure below, entire class demands can vary from the system peak.


Further, within a given class, individual customer demands can vary from the aggregated demand that drives investment in infrastructure. Consider, for example, the figure below, which uses actual data from 10 residential customers on a single transformer.


As shown above, only three of the ten customers have an individual peak demand that is coincident with the peak demand on the transformer. The surrounding report goes on to reveal that the coincident peak is only 86% of the individual customer peaks. In other words, assessing demand charges on the individual peak demand of each customer would over-recover the aggregated demand-driven cost.

Q.WILL THE SMART USAGE RATE AFFECT ALL CUSTOMERS EQUALLY?
A.No. Customers with better access to technologies like smart thermostats, time delay appliances, and water heater controls will be much better prepared to control their costs on the Smart Usage rate than those who don’t have those technologies. Further, customers with flexible schedules will be better able to stagger loads than somebody who works multiple jobs, for example.
Q.DOES THE COALITION HAVE ANY OTHER RECOMMENDATIONS RELATED TO RATE DESIGN?
A.Yes. The Coalition further recommends that the remaining energy-only time of use rate— the Time of Use Plug-In Electric Vehicle (TOU-PEV) rate—be renamed to avoid confusion that it is only available to EV owners.  
Q.DOES THE COALITION HAVE ANY FINAL THOUGHTS?
A.Yes. Our Coalition is deeply concerned about energy affordability and the effects that energy prices can have on community health and economic development. The cumulative impact of this base rate case, the upcoming fuel docket, and the hopeful completion of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 could add an additional $55-60 to the average residential bill. Because of this, we encourage the Commission to examine this request with a heightened level of scrutiny. Some particular questions that we believe ought to be asked include:
Should the 2022 IRP modeling be re-run to account for the tax benefits and other funding opportunities in the Inflation Reduction Act?
Are the expected reliability improvements from proposed T&D investments significant enough to warrant the high cost?
Does Georgia Power truly need the requested ROE to maintain its credit rating? 
What can the Company to do to promote deeper awareness of and easier access to programs like the Income Qualified Senior Citizen Discount and the HEEAP Program?

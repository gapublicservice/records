I.
PROVISION OF EV CHARGING
Q.  What are the business goals of AACE members related to EV charging?
A.  (Mr. Akers) The future is clear:  electricity will be an important source of transportation fuel in the future.  AACE members (along with other gas stations, convenience stores, truck stops, grocery stores and retailers) already offer fuel (e.g., diesel, gasoline, etc.) for internal combustion engines now used in commerce by the traveling public.   We can provide electricity to EVs in the exact same way we provide gasoline and diesel to most vehicles on the road today.  EV charging simply expands the services we offer to the traveling public. 
 
Q.   Why should AACE members, rather than the Company alone, provide EV charging to the public?
A.  We hold a key asset that will be required to deploy reliable EV charging in Georgia:  existing business locations that already dispense transportation fuel and that the traveling public deems safe and convenient.  We also believe that private investment can deploy EV charging stations faster and with better amenities for customers than a regulated utility dependent on long-term ratepayer subsidy.  The faster the EV charging market can grow through private investment, rather than ratepayer funds, the better.

Q.  Why are your existing business locations a key asset for EV charging?
A.  We already provide fuel to the traveling public at numerous business locations along every thoroughfare and rural byway in Georgia.  Our locations are conveniently located for urban, suburban, rural and traveling customers.  Our locations are found in virtually every town in Georgia and in some of the most rural areas of the state.  We provide motorists with convenience, offering fuel, amenities and the security of on-site personnel and good lighting on a 24/7 basis.  We provide products and services that the traveling public wants and needs at locations with which they are already familiar.    

Q.  Does Georgia Department of Transportation (“GDOT”) recognize the importance of our existing business locations to provide EV charging?
A.   Yes.  In August 2022, GDOT released its “Georgia Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Deployment Plan” which it submitted to secure funding through the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Program (“NEVI”) to deploy EV charging infrastructure and stations.  Therein, GDOT found:
Convenience store and gas station operators are logical candidates to offer charging services for EVs given that they currently provide fueling services to the nation’s drivers. 

GDOT recognized the value of placing EV charging stations at our existing business locations where the traveling public already purchases gasoline and diesel.  GDOT’s plan was approved on September 14, 2022.  

Q.  Will our customers and the traveling public benefit by having access to EV chargers at our existing business locations?
A.  Yes.  The traveling public already obtains transportation fuel and other services at our locations. Providing EV charging at the same place gasoline and diesel are already purchased makes sense, particularly since electricity is just another form of transportation fuel.   Commerce and travel require a reliable EV charging infrastructure so that EVs can travel long distances with assurance that fast charging is available.  Drivers will not experience “range anxiety” -- concern about: “Where am I going to charge?”  or “Will the charger work when I get there?”  While there is no difference between EV range anxiety and a driver’s concern about running out of gas, EV chargers are not yet as commonplace as fuel pumps, and drivers’ range anxiety is greater for EVs.  Our members’ locations are already etched in the minds of the traveling public and knowing that EV charging is available at our locations will assure drivers that their EV will reach the intended destination because an EV charging station can be found in time to recharge.  As EVs proliferate, our locations can provide drivers with access to an EV charging network that will be as familiar, reliable and ubiquitous as the gasoline and diesel we provide now. 
  
Q.  But, can’t EV owners charge their vehicles at home?
A.  Many EV drivers will be able to charge at home.  The availability of home charging is important, and EV owners can charge at home using relatively inexpensive, slower chargers and pay lower off-peak electric rates.  However, those who live in multi-unit dwellings (“MUD”) or do not have a detached garage where home charging can be installed will most likely need to rely on public chargers to fuel their EVs.  For those with home charging, it can take 8-10 hours to charge up an EV at home, and the ability to charge an EV at home at night does not eliminate the driver’s range anxiety when the EV must travel long distances the next day.  Home charging may be sufficient for daily commuters, but it does not cure long distance range anxiety.  

Q.  What type of charging will be required for long-distance travel?
A.  High speed chargers, commonly referred to as Direct Current Fast Chargers (“DCFC”), are required for business and long-distance travel.  The time to recharge must be as comparable as possible to the amount of time required to fill a gas tank and pay.  Currently, Level 2 chargers cannot satisfy that need because they take too long to charge while traveling on the road.  Over time, chargers will become more powerful, and the time to recharge will be reduced.  
  
Q.  Are AACE members willing to invest funds to provide EV charging services?
A.  Yes.  We are prepared to make significant capital investment to develop EV charging services supported by a reliable EV charging infrastructure.  However, our capital investment cannot succeed unless our members can deploy EV chargers and provide EV charging service to customers on a level playing field with those deployed by the Company. 

Q.  Does the current market structure deter AACE members’ investment to provide EV charging?
A.  Yes.  We face two primary barriers to our ability to provide EV charging:
1.  We will provide EV charging in direct competition with monopoly electric suppliers, like Georgia Power, whose costs to provide the same EV charging equipment and the electricity required to provide electric service will be paid by ratepayers; and
2.  We will obtain electricity to provide EV charging pursuant to tariffs, riders, terms and conditions that the Company will not charge or impute to itself to provide its own EV charging stations with which we must compete.  
As a result, our costs to provide EV charging will always exceed those of Georgia Power, and our EV charging service will always be provided at competitive disadvantage.  As retailers in private enterprise, we do not earn a higher return because we incur more costs.  Instead, we have to cover increased costs by charging our customer a higher rate for EV charging than the Company, and we risk losing customers as a result.

Q.  How should EV charging services be defined?
A.  The Commission has already determined that the owner of the EV charging station is Georgia Power’s customer and that the retail sale of electricity occurs between Georgia Power and the EV charging provider.  Before the Joint Study Committee on the Electrification of Transportation (“Joint Committee”), Madame Chair Pridemore’s presentation noted that:
… because EV charging service is not provided to a Premises, but rather, to a transient motor vehicle, EV charging service does not constitute the provision of retail electric service.”  (Emphasis in original)

She also noted that no court has ever found that EV charging constitutes retail electric service.  We agree with both the Commission’s decision and Madame Chair’s comments before the Joint Committee.  Not being a retail electric service, EV charging does not fall within the Georgia Territorial Electric Service Act, O.C.G.A. § 46-3-1, et seq.  (the “Territorial Act”).  To date, we know that our members, as EV charging providers, are the Company’s customers and that providing our EV charging service does not constitute a retail sale of electricity. 

Q.   How should our EV charging service be defined?
A.  (Mr. Nelson, Mr. Selvidge) The provision of EV charging to the public is probably most closely associated with the term “resale.”  This is consistent with the Commission’s telecommunications precedent and the exclusion of “resale” from the Territorial Act.  Though the Territorial Act does not apply to “resale,” “resale” is prohibited by Georgia Power’s rules and regulations.

Q.  How are EV charging providers currently compensated for electricity they provide EVs?
A.  Currently in Georgia, most EV charging providers charge for the amount of time by the hour or minute the EV charger is used.  If an EV charging provider wants to continue to charge on that basis (or some other) they should have the right to do so, so long as the EV charging customer is made aware of the fee they are paying.

Q.  Does AACE recommend a better means of compensation?
A.  (Mr. Akers, Mr. Nelson) We believe that a volumetric charge to the EV owner based on the amount of electricity in kWh being provided to the EV by the EV charger is the best and most appropriate way to compensate the EV charging provider.   This is permitted in over twenty (20) states, and it is more transparent to the EV owner.  The average EV owner that pulls into a parking space to charge expects to get electricity from the charger, not rent the charger over time or rent the parking space in which the EV is located.  Customers buy gas and diesel by the gallon, and electricity from the charger should be provided by kWh.  There is no substantive difference between how they are dispensed. 
The EV charging provider pays for the electricity on a per kWh basis.  EV drivers obtain a commodity – electricity – whose units are measured in kWh, and the EV charging provider should be compensated on the same basis.  Per-kWh pricing allows for that granular price signal.   It is also simpler to set per kWh costs for drivers.  Finally, it is easy for drivers to estimate the cost of charging based on $/kWh pricing, particularly when they travel across state lines.  They simply need to know the total kWh of their EV battery and multiply it by the $/kWh pricing to find the total cost of charging. 

Q.What is your concern with pricing EV charging service on a per minute or hour basis?
A.(Mr. Nelson) Dollar-per-minute or hour pricing does not account for the different power levels of EV chargers.  A 50-kW station does not provide the kWh delivered as a 100-kW station in the same amount of time.  Under dollar-per-minute or hour pricing, the EV owner must know the charging power of the station (in kW), the total kWh of their EV battery, the charging rate of the vehicle’s battery at different states of charges and the dollar-per-minute pricing, then (using that information) calculate the total cost to charge up their EV. This is much more complex, and it makes it very difficult to compare the cost of charging between different stations.  For example: is a 150-kW station priced at $3/minute more or less expensive than a 75-kW station priced at $2/minute?  $/kWh pricing is not dependent upon the charging power of the EV charging station.  With $/kWh pricing,  EV chargers with higher charging power can charge the EV faster but the total kWh delivered – and therefore the total cost -- would be the same for the EV.

Q.   Is the Territorial Act implicated at all if EV charging providers charge by the kWh?
A.  (Mr. Nelson, Mr. Selvidge) It does not appear that a kWh charge conflicts with the Territorial Act.  

Q.  Has Federal Highway Administration (“FHWA”) proposed regulations that suggest per kWh pricing is preferred?
A.  FHWA’s proposed regulations implementing NEVI include a requirement that chargers “display and base the price of electrical charge in $/kWh,” signaling that this is the most driver-friendly pricing structure and should be the standard around the country.  FHWA acknowledged that “several states restrict the ability to display charge in $/kWh.”

Q.  Is AACE familiar with Georgia Power’s Make Ready program?
A.  Generally, yes.  The Commission approved Georgia Power’s Make Ready program to fund EV infrastructure improvements in the Company’s 2019 rate case.  It authorized $6 million per year for three (3) years to install EV infrastructure upon application by a commercial or industrial customer that wants an EV charging station installed on their property.  The Commission approved the program as a policy decision, advancing public policy goals that extend beyond cost considerations.  The Make Ready and Community Charging programs fall within this authority, and the costs for both are paid by ratepayers.  

Q.  Does Georgia Power seek to enlarge the infrastructure program in this rate case?
A.  Though the Company’s direct pre-filed testimony did not request an extension of the Make Ready and Community Charging programs, its response to STF PIA 3-5 identified a requirement of $90 million in EV infrastructure upgrades from 2023-2025.  The Abramowitz panel and Mr. Legg testified in the Company’s direct case that Georgia Power is requesting $90 million for EV infrastructure deployment, with 90% of the funds to go to the Make Ready program. The remaining 10% is proposed for the Community Charging program.

Q.  How does the Make Ready program work?
A.  Based on public information, a commercial or industrial customer applies to the Company to install an EV charger on the customer’s property.    Georgia Power reviews the application, notifies the customer that their site has been selected and then installs the infrastructure from the customer’s meter to the EV charger.  The customer selects the EV charger, pays for it, installs it and collects revenue from charging.  Georgia Power maintains the infrastructure for the life of the EV charger.  The customer owns, operates and maintains the charger. 

Q.  Are there guidelines for participation in the Make Ready program?
A.  The Company’s rules and regulations for the Make Ready program are publicly available.  (Exhibit 4).  The Make Ready program standard contract between the Company and the customer is not publicly available to our knowledge.  Pursuant to a number of Georgia Open Records Act requests, we obtained the Make Ready contract between Georgia Power and the City of Augusta, Georgia to install an EV charger at 535 Telfair Street.  (Exhibit 5).  
We do not believe that the Make Ready program guidelines, criteria and standard contracts were reviewed or approved by the Commission.   It appears that participation in the Make Ready program rests largely in Georgia Power’s discretion.  Also, the Make Ready program’s terms and conditions do not set priorities based on a strategic plan to deploy EV charging stations.

Q.   How many EV chargers have been deployed in the Make Ready program?
A.  Based on its public disclosure response to STF PIA 1-1, the Company received approximately 616 location applications to deploy approximately 3100 Level 2 chargers and 100 DCFCs.  Some were not installed because the program funds ran out.  Georgia Power reports that there is a waiting list for the Make Ready program.

Q.  Does AACE support continuation of a Make Ready program?
A.  Provided the goals and requirements of the program are clearly defined, there is a role for a program like Make Ready until the EV market is more developed.  We support it and would participate in it.  But, the Commission should require clearly stated priorities for the use of ratepayer funds to deploy the infrastructure from the customer’s meter to the EV charging station, and continue the customer’s obligation to pay for the charger, its installation and maintenance. All EV commercial and industrial customers will be eligible to apply and qualify, as the Abramowitz panel testified.  

Q.  What priorities should the Commission set for the Make Ready program? 
A. Priority for deployment in the Make Ready program should be based on the proposed location and the nature of the site, as follows:
1.  Charging infrastructure located at public locations (gas stations, downtown areas, shopping malls, community centers, gas stations, grocery stores, etc.) which will provide EV charging service to the public, including those without access to home charging, for both long-distance and daily travels.  Sites that offer24/7 amenities (employees on site, restrooms, food etc.) should be given the same priority as in the NEVI grant program administered by GDOT.
2.  Charging infrastructure serving public transit fleets which provide the benefits of EVs to all residents, including low-income residents and residents who do not own EVs.  Electrifying Medium- and heavy-duty vehicles (“MHDV”). used by public transit fleets also reduces air pollution. 
3.  Charging infrastructure at workplaces and multi-unit dwellings (“MUD”) which are shared among many EV drivers but may have limited accessibility to the public. 
4.  Charging infrastructure serving other non-transit fleets which primarily serve private companies and not the general public but help electrify MHDVs; and 
5.  Within these categories, charging infrastructure sites located in rural and economically challenged communities which are expected to have lower EV adoption rates in the near term.  

Q.  Based on these priorities, what do you recommend?
A.  To support these priorities, we recommend that the Company through the Make Ready program offer to cover a percentage of the total make-ready costs as follows:
Public transit fleets and public charging sites: 70% of make-ready costs
Workplace and MUD sites: 50% of make-ready costs
Other fleets: 30% of make-ready costs
Bonus 20% of make-ready costs if the EV charging site is located in a rural or economically distressed community.
Q.   Do you have additional recommendations for the Make Ready program?
A.   We also recommend that the Commission:
1.  review and approve requirements, terms, conditions and contracts used by the Company in the Make Ready program; and 
2.  review the benefits and costs of the Make Ready program triennially in the Company’s Integrated Resources Plan.
3.  require that the property owner own the EV charging station and charge for the service provided.
    
Q.  What is the difference between the Company’s Make Ready program and its Community Charging Program?
A.  In the Community Charging program, the Company incurs the cost to install the infrastructure from the customer’s meter to the EV charger, and it also owns, operates and maintains the EV charger and gets the revenue generated from its use over the life of the charger.  All of the costs of the Community Charging program are borne by ratepayers.

Q.  How many EV chargers have been deployed in the Community Charging program? 
A.  Based on the Company’s response to STF PIA 1-8, the Company deployed approximately 50 chargers at 25 Community Charging sites as of July 2021.  Of these, approximately 90% are DCFCs (45) and 10% (5) are Level 2.  The Company contends that Community Charging chargers serve charging deserts – locations at which private investment will not deploy an EV charger.

Q.   Do you agree with the Company’s contention?
A.   No.  Investigation of the Community Charging locations identified in response to STF PIA 1-8 showed that most EV chargers in the Community Charging program are actually located along commercial strip thoroughfares, near interstate interchanges, in central downtowns and near college campuses.  In these areas, it is likely that private investment would support EV charging without ratepayer subsidy.  

Q.   Should DCFCs be deployed in the Community Charging program?
A.  No.   EV chargers deployed with ratepayer funds in the Community Charging program should provide basic, Level 2 charging service.  The costs to deploy Level 2 charging in rural locations is substantially less than that required for DCFCs.  

Q.  Are there other issues with the Community Charging program?
A.  Yes.  Like its Make Ready program, the Company has not (to our knowledge) developed objective or identifiable criteria to determine the need for the charger at any proposed Community Charging location.  Again, deployment rests largely within the discretion of the Company.  

Q.   What does AACE recommend?
A.  Over a period of time as the EV market develops, a community charger may be needed to serve a specific location that private enterprise cannot.  To serve those locations, Community Charging guidelines should be developed that include specific objective criteria by which the need for a community charger (placed in service and operated at ratepayer expense) can be identified.  Before the costs of the community charger are imposed on ratepayers, however, private enterprise should be given the opportunity to serve the proposed EV charge location through private investment.  But, these locations will prove to be few and far between.

Q.  Is there a telecommunications precedent applicable to the deployment of EV chargers to serve charging deserts, particularly in truly rural locations?
A.  (Mr. Selvidge) Yes.  The FCC found that payphones “unquestionably serve critical public interests in health, safety and welfare” and that a competitive market may fail to adequately provide payphones in locations serving important public needs because the payphones “may not be economically self-supporting.”  The FCC cited “payphones…along deserted stretches of highway [that] can be essential for public safety but fail to be [sufficiently] revenue-generating for various reasons, including lack of sufficient traffic, damage from extreme weather conditions, or high maintenance costs.”  The FCC established criteria by which individual states could maintain and fund “public interest payphones in locations serving health, safety, and welfare goals, where they would not otherwise exist as a result of the operation of the market.”  
The FCC defined a "public interest payphone" as a payphone which (1) fulfills a public policy objective in health, safety, or public welfare, (2) is not provided for a location provider with an existing contract for the provision of a payphone, and (3) would not otherwise exist as a result of the operation of the competitive marketplace.  However, the FCC added a caveat that “while we grant the states broad discretion in administering and funding public interest payphone programs, we also require that they do so in a manner which does not upset the competitive balance of the payphone market (i.e., competitively neutral), and that fairly and equitably compensates those entities providing public interest payphones.”

Q.  Should the Commission apply this precedent to Georgia Power’s Community Charging program?
A.   Yes.   The Commission should recognize that an EV charger deployed in the Community Charging program is the equivalent of a public interest phone and develop equivalent rules governing their deployment.  This Commission should establish criteria for the deployment of EV charging stations that are truly required in the public interest, while being cognizant of the FCC’s caveat that the Community Charging program not upset the competitive balance in the provision of EV charging service.

II.
EV CHARGING TARIFFS

Georgia Power’s Proposed Rider CIEV-1 (Charge It – Electric Vehicle)

Q.Is the Company proposing any new riders in this proceeding?
A.(Mr. Nelson)  Yes, the Company is proposing a new rider, Charge It – Electric Vehicle - 1 (“CIEV”), to support public commercial EV charging.

Q.Please describe Rider CIEV-1.
A.CIEV-1 is a rider to the Power and Light (PL) Small (PLS-14), Medium (PLM-14), and Large (PLL-14) tariffs, available to new premises dedicated to EV charging, served behind a dedicated meter, and starting service on or after January 1, 2023.  Under Rider CIEV-1, the customer’s billing demand under tariff PLS/PLM/PLL is adjusted by the Billing Demand Adjustment Factor (BDAF) following this schedule:
Year 1 (bills 1-12) BDAF = 0.20
Year 2 (bills 13-24) BDAF = 0.40
Year 3 (bills 25-36) BDAF = 0.60
Year 4 (bills 37-48) BDAF = 0.80

The adjustment amounts to a billing demand discount of 80% in Year 1, 60% in Year 2, 40% in Year 3, and 20% in Year 4. The reduction in a customer’s billing demand ultimately reduces the customer’s minimum monthly bill. This adjustment is only available for customers with a 12-month load factor of 15% or less, which the Company measures by dividing that month’s total energy by that month’s peak demand divided by the hours in the billing month.

Q.What are the underlying tariffs to which Rider CIEV-1 is attached?
A.PLS, PLM, and PLL are three similar tariffs that consist of three key elements.  Each tariff has a basic serve charge, an energy charge ($/kWh), and a demand charge.  The energy charge decreases as consumption increases. The demand charge is calculated using a billing demand based on the highest 30-minute kW measurement during the current month and the preceding 11 months.  This is also referred to as a demand “ratchet”.

B.  Analysis of Georgia Power’s Proposed CIEV -1 Tariff
Q.Provide an overview of your analysis and recommendations regarding the Company’s rate design.
A.The Company’s proposed CIEV-1 rider and the existing tariffs that accompany it do not reflect the importance of rate modernization in the context of an evolving power grid. Specifically, the Company’s proposed tariff does not encourage load flexibility, even though EV demand can be aligned with changing grid conditions for cost-effective EV integration, if given the right price signals. To better reflect cost causation, I recommend an alternative rate design that encourages EV adoption while providing meaningful price signals for grid-efficient charging.

1.  Background: Impact of an Evolving Power Grid for Rate Design Processes
Q.How is the power grid evolving in Georgia?
A.It is important to provide context about Georgia’s evolving power grid and how it impacts the rate design process, including EV rates.  The penetration of variable renewable energy in Georgia Power’s resource mix has increased and is projected to accelerate in the future. While renewables comprised only 3% of the Company’s generation in 2020, the Company has plans to add 6,000 MW of renewable generation by 2035, doubling its renewable generation. Renewable resources are cost effective, and consumers are demanding them.  

Q.Do changes in the power system impact the rate design process, generally?
A.Yes.  The evolution of the power grid toward increasing renewable resources is beneficial from a public interest perspective and requires cost-effectively integrating those resources.  The increase in variable renewable resources makes the timing of grid services and the availability of grid flexibility (i.e., the ability to shape demand side load, such as EV charging load) increasingly important.  From a ratemaking perspective, flexible demand can be treated like a system resource.  The ability to shape demand makes it important to recover generation and transmission costs through dynamic, time-varying volumetric rates and to closely examine distribution-related demand charges. 

Q.How are changes on the power grid relevant to EV rate design?
A.EV rates are no exception to the rate design evolution—and are arguably more important to treat dynamically due to EVs’ high potential for flexibility and equally high potential to charge out-of-sync with variable supply and incur high grid costs in the absence of proper price signals and program options. Time-varying volumetric cost recovery that incentivizes load flexibility will become increasingly important, such as from EV users.  The Company’s rate design should reflect the actual value of load flexibility by creating and improving temporal cost allocations that can be used to inform more granular, dynamic rate designs and demand response programs. For example, supply and transmission costs can be analyzed and translated into more granular TOU rates.  It is important that a utility have a suite of rate options that incent multiple types of load flexibility. EV load can be throttled, curtailed, increased to build load during low net load, scheduled, and managed in numerous other ways.

2.  Concerns with the CIEV-1 Rider
Q.Describe your concerns with the CIEV-1 Rider
A.I have several. My primary concern is that the rider does not improve upon the underlying tariffs, which are not conducive to EV adoption or cost-effective EV charging. My secondary concern is that the rider itself may not efficiently achieve the intended goal of EV adoption. 

Q.Explain how the CIEV-1 Rider fails to improve upon the underlying tariffs.
A.Rather than proposing a holistic EV rate, the Company proposed a rider that layers on top of existing PL rates – rates that themselves present significant barriers to EV adoption and grid-efficient EV charging.  Even when combined, the CIEV-1 rider and underlying tariffs do not reflect the importance of rate modernization and the evolving power grid that I highlighted above. Specifically, the proposed rate structure maintains a ratcheted demand charge and does not time-differentiate energy costs.

Q.How does the ratcheted demand charge on tariffs PLS, PLM, and PLL hinder EV adoption?
A.The ratchet locks in a higher bill for charging stations by setting monthly billing demand at the greatest of: 
1) current actual demand, 
2) 95% of the highest actual previous summer demand, or 
3) 60% of the highest actual previous winter demand. 

Thus, rather than paying monthly demand charges based on individual monthly demand, an EV charging station would likely pay monthly demand charges based on the highest demand over the entire previous year. For example, a station with multiple charging ports might usually only have one customer at a time, but one day, it has three simultaneous charging customers.  Under CIEV-1, it may be billed based on the abnormally higher demand level resulting from this single event for the following year. 
High demand charges make the economics of EV station ownership particularly challenging because at low charger utilization, EV stations are usually unable to collect the amount of revenue necessary from drivers to cover the high demand charges. Indeed, the demand ratchet is the reason the Company’s CIEV-1 discount on billed demand is necessary in the first place.

Q.Why is a ratcheted demand charge inappropriate for EV customers?
A.Although the CIEV-1 rider attempts to address the issue that high demand charges pose to low load factor EV station hosts, the ratchet itself is inappropriate for EV customers. The ratchet is an outdated rate design intended for high load factor customers that does not reflect the costs caused by EV charging load.  Specifically, at low load factors, EV charging stations do not use generation and transmission capacity the same way that a high load factor customer does.  Their spiky load is less likely to occur during the system peak than is a high load factor customers’ load. EV charging stations’ load diversity means that they can more efficiently share system capacity, unlike customers whose load is more consistent across all hours – including during the coincident system peak.  But under GP’s proposal, low load factor charging stations would be charged as though they cause generation capacity costs the same way as high load factor customers.  If they have abnormally high charging for 30 minutes out of a full year, such as in the example above, it would be billed the same way as a high load factor customer.    These customers utilize the system in significantly different ways, but the Company proposes to bill them similarly.

Q.Have other jurisdictions started to retire the concept of ratcheted demand charges to move toward more modernized rate designs?
A.Yes.  In January 2020, Xcel Energy filed for a new general time-of-use service tariff in Minnesota.  This new rate consists of three time periods and includes new seasonally differentiated demand charges that bill based on the highest monthly 15-minute load during the mid and on-peak times during any given month. Duke Energy has also investigated rate design alternatives to ratcheted demand charges.  It is clear that Duke Energy has determined that demand ratchets are not an appropriate rate design mechanism.  Duke has proposed to transition away from their current two-part demand structure to a three-part demand charge consisting of a base, mid-peak, and peak period.  The structure aligns to system costs and includes lower overall $/kW prices that are applied to different determinants.  This proposed restructuring is indicative of Duke Energy recognizing that ratcheted demand charges are outdated and a rate structure that is not a good fit for many customers.

Q.How do the non-time-differentiated energy charges on tariffs PLS, PLM, and PLL hinder cost-effective EV charging?
A.As described above, EV charging has the potential to provide demand flexibility – in other words, to avoid charging during times of day that are costliest on the power system, and to instead target charging to low-cost hours.  On the other hand, EVs could significantly increase system costs by charging during times of system stress. Whether or not EV charging causes system costs or works efficiently with the grid depends on whether EVs receive dynamic price signals via time-varying volumetric rates. The rates that Georgia Power proposes to underlie the CIEV-1 charging service (PLS/PLM/PLL) are not time-differentiated and therefore do not provide sufficiently granular price signals to encourage charging during system surplus and discourage charging during system stress. The PLS/PLM/PLL energy rates do not promote load flexibility.
Q.Regarding your secondary concern about the CIEV-1 Rider, explain how the rider structure may not efficiently achieve the intended goal of EV adoption.
A.Under the Company’s proposal, a customer’s demand charge discount decreases annually. Annual decreases would result in customers who need the discount (i.e., those with low load factors) not receiving it.  A demand charge discount should decrease based on load factor, not arbitrary time increments. Lowering a demand charge discount on a sliding scale as load factor increases (up to, say, 15%) would target the discounts to low load factor EV chargers who need it the most and ensure that potentially profitable chargers (i.e., those with high load factors) do not continue to receive a discount that they don’t need.

3.  Alternative EV Rate Recommendation
Q.Do you recommend an alternative to Georgia Power’s CIEV-1 Rider?
A.Yes.  Instead of the CIEV-1 rider, Georgia Power should develop a distinct and default rate (not a rider) available for EV charging with the following features:
A time-of-use energy rate with on-peak, mid-peak, and off-peak periods. 
The on-peak period should be narrow (e.g. five hours long, such as from 3pm-8pm.)

There should be a high price differential between the on- and off-peak periods (e.g. 3:1 or 4:1 on:off peak). 

The rates should be seasonally differentiated to reflect the variations in system costs throughout the year.

A demand charge that recovers only distribution costs. All generation and transmission costs should be recovered through the volumetric TOU charges, rather than demand charges.

No demand charge ratchet
This rate should be default for separately-metered EV customers.  But, they should be able to select other rates if they choose.  It is important to make a suite of options available to customers.  If they want to meter separately under an EV rate or treat EV charging as native load under their pre-existing tariff, both should be permissible.  In the future, the Commission should also require Georgia Power to develop and offer a suite of rate designs to maximize EV load flexibility, such as TOU with critical peak pricing and active load management options that allow for capacity interruption.

Q.Why do you recommend a narrow on-peak period?
A.The objective of an on-peak period should be to capture the highest average hourly system prices, while keeping the on-peak time period short enough to allow customers to react to the elevated price signal.  Without hourly system energy and transmission data, I cannot recommend specific on- and off-peak periods for Georgia Power.  However, best practices suggest that a short peak period more easily enables customers to shift energy, while a longer peak period limits customers’ opportunities to respond to the TOU price signal, preventing behavioral change. At the same time, a shorter window allows the utility to more narrowly target the highest hours of system stress – achieving behavioral change precisely where it is needed.

Q.Why do you recommend a high on:off peak price differential?
A.Behavioral research and analysis on TOU rates demonstrate that utility customers shift their consumption when presented with highly differentiated peak and off-peak prices.

Q.Why is your EV rate recommendation superior to the Company’s CIEV-1 Rider?
A.I recommend a TOU rate structure that incentivizes managed charging and load flexibility, neither of which are incentivized by Georgia Power’s proposed CIEV-1 Rider and underlying PLS/PLM/PLL rates.  Load flexibility is critical to ensure cost-effective EV charging and avoid harmful grid impacts into the future as EV adoption increases.  Indeed, my rate recommendation also supports increased EV adoption.  My recommendation reduces the cost barrier for EV charging customers with low load factor, more effectively achieving the Company’s stated goal to “encourage the growth of EVs” in Georgia.  My recommended rate addresses the same demand charge issue that the CIEV-1 Rider addresses, without the need for a discount. Instead, my rate recommendation lowers the demand charge by recovering only distribution costs via demand charge and not generation and transmission costs.

Q.Could the Commission still apply a demand charge discount under your rate recommendation?
A.If the Commission desires to further incentivize EV adoption, a demand charge discount based on EV station load factor could be applied to the distribution portion of the bill for a four-year period. However, the structure I have proposed should already alleviate the largest financial hurdle for EV charging (i.e., collecting generation and transmission costs through a demand charge) without any explicit subsidy.

Q.Is your rate recommendation similar to EV rates offered elsewhere?
A.Yes.  Alabama Power offers Rate BEVT (Business Electric Vehicle – Time of Use), which includes seasonably differentiated, three-period TOU energy charges.  Rate BEVT also includes a $2.00/kWh demand charge that is only assessed based on the customer’s demand during each billing period and applied as part of a minimum bill provision.  Xcel Energy Minnesota will also be piloting a commercial and industrial rate, that is eligible for EV charging, with three-period TOU energy charges that collect generation and transmission costs via volumetric rates rather than demand charges.
Q.How do you recommend that the Commission oversee Georgia Power’s development and implementation of your recommended rate?
A.The Commission should ensure that there is sufficient opportunity for stakeholder review prior to adoption.


III.
APPLICATION OF THE COMMISSION’S TELECOMMUNICATIONS
PRECEDENTS TO ESTABLISH A LEVEL-PLAYING FIELD FOR EV CHARGING 
Q.  Does the panel seriously suggest that the Commission should apply precedents to EV charging deployment that were intended to address issues pertinent to a now extinct telecommunications service – payphones?  
A.  (Mr. Selvidge) Yes.  History is an excellent teacher, and policies and precedents set by this Commission should be followed when similar facts are presented as a new service develops.  To deploy a ubiquitous network of EV charging stations, Georgia needs AACE members to install public EV charging stations at their business locations.  Nearly forty (40) years ago, when regulators determined that available LEC owned public communications facilities were insufficient to serve the traveling public, they authorized private ownership and investment to deploy public payphones to serve a similar public demand and fill a service void.

Q.  From a regulatory perspective, are public EV charging stations and public payphones essentially the same?
A.  Yes, they present the same market and regulatory issues.  The only difference between them is the service that each provides.  The EV charger provides electricity to the traveling public’s vehicles while the public phone provided communication access to the traveling public to make a telephone call while away from home or office.  The similarities between public charging and public communications services are so striking that deployment of EV charging stations and development of a level-playing field between EV charging services provided by AACE members and the Company do not present novel regulatory issues of first impression.  Application of the Commission’s public communications precedent to EV charging will establish an appropriate policy for this new service.          

Q.  Elaborate on the similarities between public charging and public communications.
A.  Before there was range anxiety, there was missed communication anxiety.  Before cell phones, the traveling public needed access to communications while away from home or office at airports or on traveled highways.  Payphones provided this important mobile telecommunications access.  Payphone service was initially provided by incumbent LECs that (similarly to Georgia Power) were rate of return, regulated monopolies that furnished service in a specified geographic service territory.  
Payphone service was also a component of universal service.  The Commission’s rules required that each incumbent LEC provide a single payphone in each exchange.  All costs incurred by the LEC to provide public communications were included in the incumbent LEC’s ratebase and paid by its ratepayers.  Obviously, this level of service was not sufficient to serve the traveling public’s communications demand.  To meet that need, the FCC and this Commission allowed location owners and third parties to own and deploy payphones on their properties.  The authority was premised on the belief that private enterprise would deploy payphones to fill the unserved void in the monopoly, incumbent LECs’ public communications service. 

Q.  Continue.
A.  Unfortunately, the traditional regulatory environment was not welcoming to new IPP entrants to fill the public communications service void.  The primary roadblock was the refusal of incumbent, monopoly LECs to allow property owners to deploy the same type of public phone that LECs operated which could access essential central office functions.  As a result, a payphone manufacturing industry gradually developed that used computer technology to replicate central office functions in each individual payphone.  Manufacturing rose to the challenge, providing IPPs the ability to navigate around incumbent LEC barriers.  Today, these payphone manufacturers are personified by EV charging manufacturers, like ChargePoint, EV go and others, and the first IPPs that deployed the new payphones for public use are personified by AACE members.  

Q.  Was service provided to IPPs pursuant to incumbent LEC tariffs?
A.  Yes. Incumbent LECs crafted special public payphone tariffs for IPPs.  IPPs had to rely on the monopoly LECs to provide the basic telephone line required to provide payphone service and connect the IPP’s phone to the network.  Early LEC tariffs required the IPP to pay up to 75% of the revenue generated from the payphone for line charges.   This imposed high costs on IPPs to deploy phones, and it limited the locations at which a profitable public phone could be deployed.  While IPP’s private investment eventually and significantly increased the overall number of public payphones in the United States, the incumbent LECs’ tariffs dictated where a profitable phone could be located, and Georgia Power’s proposed EV charging tariff may produce a similar result.

Q.   Did incumbent LECs, such as BellSouth, pay for the service to their own phones under the same tariff that was required of IPPs?
A.  No.  Incumbent LECs’ payphone line costs were expensed for accounting purposes in the same manner as the cost of providing telephone service to itself – an amount that was always lower than the tariff paid by IPPs for their payphone lines.  This cost differential limited phone deployment and placed IPPs at competitive disadvantage.  
Similarly, Georgia Power testified that it will not pay the tariffed rate it will charge AACE members to deploy their chargers.  Like BellSouth, it will account for the cost of the electricity required to run its EV charger the same way it accounts for the cost of electricity that its facilities use.

Q.  What was the relationship between the IPP and the incumbent LEC?
A.  The IPP was both the incumbent LEC’s customer and its competitor.  The incumbent LEC provided the basic telephone line to the IPP pursuant to tariff, and it also owned and operated (at ratepayer expense) the payphones against which the IPPs’ phones had to compete.  As the FCC noted years later, the incumbent LECs by furnishing the telephone line to the IPP pursuant to tariff as well as competing against the IPP for locations had both the incentive and ability to “unfairly act to the detriment of their…competitors and to act in other anti-competitive ways.”
Q.  Will the relationship between AACE members and Georgia Power be the same for EV charging services?
A.  Yes.  To provide EV charging for the traveling public, AACE members must obtain electric service from Georgia Power pursuant to tariffed rates, terms and conditions.   Then, they must deploy the EV charger in competition with the Company’s own EV chargers.  Just as with payphones, AACE members will deploy EV chargers predominately through private investment, and their EV chargers will directly compete against Georgia Power’s EV chargers for which the costs will be paid by ratepayers.

Q.  Does the Company’s CIEV-1 tariff (or any other Company tariff) and its provision of electricity service thereunder establish a level-playing field between Georgia Power and ACCE members in the EV charging market?
A.  No.  Georgia Power’s tariff (CIEV-1 or other) will only apply to service provided to EV chargers owned and operated by private enterprise.  Tariff options still discriminate in favor of the Company’s EV charging stations by increasing the private EV charging station provider’s costs.  Mr. Legg’s testimony on behalf of the Company made this distinction very clear:  electricity supplied to the Company’s EV chargers will not be provided pursuant to any tariff.  Instead, Georgia Power (like BellSouth years ago) will use its internal accounting policies governing the cost to supply electricity at its properties to account for the cost of electricity provided to its EV chargers.  This will either prevent the private EV charging provider from offering EV charging as a service or it will require the EV charging provider to charge its customers a higher price than the Company for the same service from the same EV chargers.  Either result provides competitive advantage to the Company’s EV chargers.

Q.  Will the tariffed rate revisions proposed by our panel eliminate this cost differential and the competitive advantage enjoyed by the Company?
A.  No.  The revised tariff rates proposed in our panel’s testimony will still not be applied to Georgia Power’s own EV chargers.  The cost differential may be reduced somewhat by the tariff we propose, but the cost differential will continue to exist, and private EV chargers will remain at a competitive disadvantage.

Q.   What is the competitive significance of this distinction?
A.   This means that the tariffed rate for electricity provided to a privately owned EV charger will always exceed the cost that Georgia Power incurs to provide electricity to its own EV chargers.  The privately-owned EV charging provider will always be at competitive disadvantage.

Q.   Is the tariffed rate the only cost differential?
A.  No.  PLS, PLM and PLL are the tariffs onto which CIEV-1 is attached as a rider for new EV charging locations.  The PL tariffs also charge customers the following riders which private EV chargers will have to pay, but the Company’s chargers will not:   
Environmental Compliance Cost Recovery (ECCR)
Nuclear Construction Cost Recovery (NCCR)
Demand Side Management Schedule (DSM)
Fuel Cost Recovery (FCR)
Municipal Franchise Fee (MFF)
These riders (which are paid in addition to tariffed rates) increase the private EV charging provider’s costs significantly.

Q.   Can you quantify the differential increase in costs incurred by a privately-owned EV charge provider, given the Company’s failure to pay/impute the tariffed rates and charges to its own EV chargers?
A.   Yes.   The following graph quantifies the cost differential between the higher costs imposed on private EV charging providers:


The back-up data supporting this graph is provided in Exhibit 6.  As shown, the increased costs imposed on private EV charging providers exceeds $110,000.00 over the ten (10) year life of the EV charger.

Q.   Can the Company implement operational policies that will favor its EV charging business?
A.   Yes.  BellSouth created a separate internal office to address all payphone issues and provide service to its own payphones as well as service to IPPs.  As a result, BellSouth knew exactly where IPPs intended to place public phones based on receiving service orders in advance. Often after placing a telephone line order and awaiting installation, the IPP found that BellSouth had already placed its own payphone at the desired location.  This is just one example.  
In direct testimony, the Company stated that it has created an EV charging team to handle all EV issues.  If so, private EV charging providers may encounter similar risks that Company policies may similarly advance its EV charging business.  For example, Georgia Power’s standard Make Ready contract requires the program participant to provide complete EV charger usage information and data to Georgia Power after the EV charger is operable.  No other entity will have access to or be able to use that information which over a large number of deployed EV chargers could be competitively valuable in understanding the developing EV charging market.  Company policies, like tariffs, can negatively impact EV charging availability.  

Q.  Is there also risk of cross-subsidy?
A.  Yes.  Cross-subsidy occurs when the monopoly provider uses revenues from its regulated service to financially support its competitive service.  Absent appropriate accounting separations, cross-subsidies can develop to benefit the Company’s EV charging business.  At a minimum, the Commission must prohibit cross-subsidy of its EV charging business from its regulated revenues.

Q.  How can a level-playing field be implemented in a market in which both the Company and private enterprise provide EV charging services? 
A.   All EV charging providers, both Company and private enterprise, must be treated equally in application of tariffed rates, terms and conditions, as well as the Company’s operational policies. 
 
Q.   Did you work to establish a level-playing field between the incumbent LECs’ payphone business and the businesses of IPPs?
A.  Yes, for more than a decade.  Competitive issues were addressed piece meal and finally resolved shortly before the industry began to decline due to the proliferation of cell phones.  

Q.   What action did you take?  
A.   At the federal level, I was a member of the team that negotiated with most stakeholders to enact Section 276 of TA 1996 “to promote competition among payphone service providers and promote the widespread deployment of payphone services to the benefit of the general public” (Section 276 (b)(1)).  Among other directives, Section 276 directed the FCC to:
1.  discontinue all intrastate and interstate payphone subsidies from basic exchange and exchange access revenues;  and
2.  prescribe a set of nonstructural safeguards for Bell operating company payphone service…;
The legislation also directed the FCC to “… determine whether public interest payphones which are provided in the interest of public health, safety, and welfare, in locations where there would otherwise not be a payphone, should be maintained, and if so, ensure that such public interest payphones are supported fairly and equitably.”
Finding that “regulatory restrictions on the placement of payphones, and existing subsidies from other telecommunications services available to certain competitors but not others are…examples of regulatory inefficiencies affecting competition and the widespread deployment of payphones,” the FCC conditioned the competitive entry of monopoly telecommunications carriers into the unregulated activity of providing payphone services on the termination of any subsidies that were being received – essentially preventing the cross-subsidization of a monopoly’s payphone operations from its local exchange services.

Q.  What happened in Georgia?
A.  For nearly a decade, Georgia’s payphone industry addressed discriminatory tariff provisions, policies and procedures in a piece meal fashion with the Commission.  Numerous issues were presented when BellSouth provided a service to its own phones that it would not provide to IPPs.  The BellSouth tariff rate to IPPs was reduced bit by bit, and in order to achieve some rate relief, IPPs actually agreed to pay measured service whereby the IPP paid for the use of its phone on a per minute basis.  Finally, GPCA filed its Same Terms, Same Conditions petition asking the Commission to require BellSouth and other incumbent LECs to provide IPPs with the same services on the same terms they provided service to their own payphones.  As a result, BellSouth entered a Stipulation by which it consented to the relief sought by GPCA, and it agreed to provide the same service to IPPs that it provided to its own payphones.

Q.Did Section 276 and/or the Stipulation in the Same Terms, Same Conditions docket eliminate the incumbent LEC’s competitive advantage?
A.No.  Though the competitive playing field improved, the non-structural safeguards did not level it, and the incumbent LEC’s competitive advantage persisted.  It became clear that structural separation between the incumbent LEC’s regulated business and its competitive payphone business was required.  

Q.   Has this Commission imposed structural and non-structural separations requirements to prevent competitive advantage between service providers?
A. Yes.  The Commission has addressed structural and non-structural separation and accounting safeguards between the competitive services provided by an entity (such as an incumbent monopoly provider, a municipality or electric membership corporation) that possesses the ability to utilize its position to cross-subsidize its competitive affiliate or gain competitive advantage by favoring its affiliate’s competitive service.  This issue has arisen in the provision of telecommunications, natural gas and broadband deployment. 

Q.   What did BellSouth ultimately do?
 A.  In 1996, BellSouth created a separate subsidiary to own and operate its payphone business:  BellSouth Public Communications, Inc.  As a result, tariffed rates to IPPs were reduced and the market quickly equalized between BellSouth and IPPs.  Achieving these necessary changes took more than a decade, and by then, the writing was on the wall for the limited remaining viability of public wireline communications, as payphones were being replaced by cellular phone service. 
Q.   Should the Commission apply the lessons learned from these public communication precedents to EV charging?
A.  Yes.  From this history, the similarities between public communications and public EV charging should be obvious.  The Commission should carefully consider the lessons learned from years of regulatory battles fought over public phones as precedent to develop a fair, robust and competitive environment for providing an essential EV charging service to the traveling public. 
We believe that the best policy is to direct the Company to create a separate subsidiary for its competitive EV charging business.  The Company’s current locations should be transferred to that entity,  which will develop future locations and pay the same tariffed rates for electricity to the Company.

Q.Why is it important for the Commission to address these issues now as Georgia’s EV charging market is just beginning to develop?
A.The implementation of policies to ensure that the EV charging market develops on a level-playing field from the very beginning will benefit Georgia’s consumers and encourage a market driven, robust public charging market in the public interest.  By applying the countless lessons learned over many years from the development and emergence of public phone competition, the Commission can avoid the considerable issues that required more than a decade to finally achieve a non-discriminatory regulatory regime that supported fair competition in Georgia’s payphone industry.  For public phones, the FCC’s ultimate goal was to have a “competitive payphone industry that meets the needs of the public by a wide deployment of payphones…in our view, we can best facilitate this by putting in place rules and regulations that provide incentives to all the players in the industry to eliminate, as soon as possible, all of the market distorting factors that exist today.”  
In contrast to the public phone market that confronted the FCC and this Commission nearly forty (40) years ago, this Commission does not have to navigate any preexisting market conditions as it establishes the appropriate regulatory structure and policies for Georgia’s new EV charging industry.  It has a clean slate from which to work to promote robust competition for EV charging station deployment.  Ultimately, developing a level playing field now will result in robust, market driven competition to provide ubiquitous EV charging by the Company and private enterprise all across Georgia for the traveling public.

Q.Does this conclude the panel’s testimony?
A.Yes.

Question:

How long on an average does it take to reconductor a 50 miles-long 115 kV, 138 kV, and 230 kV line, which is scheduled for reconductoring, with a new conductor assuming no other items need to be replaced such as cross arms, insulators, towers / poles in the GPC’s service area? Please provide supporting information based on the Company’s experience in the recent past.

Response:

Please note that the criteria referenced in the question are not typical for a reconductor project. When performing a reconductor project, it is common to assess structures and associated hardware to ensure that strength and clearance standards, as defined by the National Electric Safety Code (“NESC”), continue to be met after replacing the conductor. The estimates provided below are based on similar scopes of work that have been performed. With two construction crews, the Company estimates that it would take approximately 100 weeks and 150 weeks to reconductor 50 miles of 115-kV line and 230-kV line, respectively. The Company does not have any 138-kV transmission line on the system, thus an estimate for this voltage cannot be provided. It should also be noted that the timeframe needed to reconductor transmission projects is dependent on many variables, including terrain, soil conditions, weather, availability of material and construction crews, railroad and highway crossings, and various environmental considerations. These variables can affect the estimated durations stated above.

STF-WG-6-4

Question:

How long on average does it take to remove the old conductor and other major components such as cross arms, insulators, etc., except for the poles/towers for rebuilding a 50 mile-long 115 kV, 138 kV, and 230 kV line, which is scheduled for replacement/rebuilding in the GPC's service area?   Please provide examples supporting information based on the Company’s experience in the recent past.

Response:

The Company’s transmission line construction organization determined that a similar scope of work for the criteria referenced in the question could not be identified. A more typical scope would be a complete line rebuild, which would include the replacement of structures. Further, during construction, removals are intertwined with the installation of facilities, thus making it difficult to determine the duration of “removal only” work. Given this context concerning the difficulties with accurately estimating durations for the criteria referenced in the question, the Company estimates that it would take approximately 150 weeks and 250 weeks to remove old conductor and associated hardware for 50 miles of 115-kV line and 230-kV line, respectively, with two construction crews. The Company does not have any 138-kV transmission line on the system, thus an estimate for this voltage cannot be provided. As also mentioned in the Company’s response to STF-WG-6-3, the timeframe needed to reconductor transmission projects is dependent on many variables, including terrain, soil conditions, weather, availability of material and construction crews, railroad and highway crossings, and various environmental considerations. These variables can affect the estimated durations stated above. 

Supporting project information for the estimated durations stated above is not available due to the atypical nature of the criteria referenced in the question.

STF-WG-6-5

Question:

Please describe the criteria the Company uses to schedule transmission facilities and associated station equipment for replacement/rebuild that can be simultaneously taken out of service without compromising the reliability of the power system for a given year, if the replacement / rebuild programs occur during spring and fall.

Response:

The Company identifies and schedules transmission facilities and associated station equipment for replacement/rebuild based upon many factors such as the condition of the equipment, performance, commitment dates, customer needs, and changes in system needs. The process is consistent with the planning guidelines provided in Section B of the 2022 Georgia Power Integrated Resource Plan Technical Appendix Volume 3 (Docket 44160). Regarding the coordination of simultaneous clearances, the Company performs operational planning throughout the year, assessing in advance the clearance requests submitted for each day to identify thermal, voltage, and stability constraints during both normal and contingency conditions (such as additional unplanned outages of transmission lines or facilities). Due to the dynamic nature of the transmission system, it is important to note that forecasting system constraints through operational planning assessments is very difficult beyond an 18-month window. Transmission clearance requests are evaluated consistent with the NERC reliability standards to provide reliable system conditions entering each operating day. When potential reliability constraints are identified, associated clearance requests are declined and rescheduled during favorable system conditions to eliminate conflicts with other scheduled work.

STF-WG-6-6

Question:

Please describe any technical studies conducted to evaluate the impact on the system operation of simultaneous long-term outages of multiple transmission facilities scheduled for replacement / rebuild each year during the 2023 – 2025 period.  Please provide the results of such studies. If no such studies are conducted, please explain why not. 

Response:

The Company performs long-term transmission planning on a ten-year horizon consistent with the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (“NERC”) Reliability standards and coordinates with the Company’s Integrated Resource Planning processes to identify transmission projects and their associated timing to meet customer needs. Long-term outages of transmission facilities are modeled in the transmission planning base cases per NERC TPL-001-5 Requirement 2.1.3. Simultaneous outages are evaluated as part of the validation phase of the annual planning process as described in Georgia Power’s 2022 Integrated Resource Plan filing (Docket 44160) in Section A of Technical Appendix Volume 3. The 2023-2025 period does not currently involve simultaneous long-term outages of multiple transmission facilities scheduled for replacement / rebuild in close physical or electrical proximity.

STF-WG-6-7

Question:

Please describe the Company’s backup plan if the transmission facilities scheduled for replacement/rebuild each during the 2023-2025 period cannot be completed as planned. 

Response:

Keeping in mind the difficulties associated with forecasting system constraints beyond an 18-month window as noted in the Company’s response to STF-WG-6-5, the Company coordinates transmission projects to achieve the need date targets with flexibility and accounts for potential delays in consideration. For example, a complex project with a need date of June may be scheduled during the preceding fall outage period to avoid potential conflicts with other projects and to provide sufficient buffer if project delays are encountered. Projects that cannot be completed by need dates may result in operating constraints during certain system conditions. Projects driven by North American Electric Reliability Corporation transmission planning standards are typically not adjusted if there is not an applicable operating guide.

STF-WG-6-9

Question:

How many transmission facilities (100 kV and above) can the Company take simultaneously out of service for replacement/rebuild each month/year for the 2023 – 2025 period without compromising the reliability of the interconnected network?

Response:

There is not a specific number of transmission facilities which can be taken simultaneously out of service during a multi-year period. System conditions could support multiple simultaneous clearances, and the Company assesses and reliably supports thousands of clearance requests each year. If operational planning assessments identify transmission constraints due to multiple simultaneous clearances during specific dates and system conditions (such as changes in load forecast, changes in generation resources and patterns, changes in loop flows with neighboring facilities, etc.), one or more of the clearances will be rescheduled. Generally, clearance conflicts can be mitigated by staggering projects throughout the year, shortening clearance durations through construction practices, using temporary reconfigurations, coordinating multiple projects under the same clearance, and other practices.

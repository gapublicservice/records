 3                    II.    OVERVIEW OF DEPRECIATION RATES AND EXPENSE

 4   Q.     WHAT IS DEPRECIATION EXPENSE?

 5   A.     Depreciation is an expense that represents recovery of the Company’s capital investment over

 6          the expected life of the investment. Depreciation expense also accounts for the expected cost of

 7          removal (COR) and salvage value over the expected life of the investment.

 8   Q.     HOW DOES RECOVERY OF INVESTMENT DIFFER FROM RETURN ON
 9          INVESTMENT?

10   A.     Recovery of investment is the recovery of the original investment, while return on investment

11          represents the financing cost on the asset. Assets are financed with a combination of debt and

12          equity.

13   Q.     HOW IS DEPRECIATION EXPENSE CALCULATED?

14   A.     Depreciation expense is calculated by multiplying the gross plant balance in each Federal

15          Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) account or subaccount by a depreciation rate

16          specific to that account or subaccount. Depreciation rates are approved by the Commission in

17          each general rate case and typically are not changed between rate cases. Depreciation rate

18          recommendations made to the Commission are usually supported by a depreciation study.

 4   Q.     WHY DO YOUR RECOMMENDATIONS FOCUS ON THE EXPECTED
 5          RETIREMENT OUTCOMES?

 6   A.     A commonly accepted premise of fairness is that those who benefit from a cost should bear the

 7          cost. This concept is applied when using cost of service studies to support the allocation of

 8          costs across customers. The same concept is used to support the recovery of capital across

 9          time. Customers benefit from utility plant only as long as the plant is in service. Thus, the

10          recovery of the investment in plant should be spread across time that plant is expected to be in

11          service. Depreciation rates calculated based on expected retirement outcomes accomplish this

12          goal.

13   Q.     IS IT SOUND POLICY TO COLLECT EXTRA EXPENSE IN ORDER TO
14          ESTABLISH A BUFFER FOR UNEXPECTED RETIREMENTS OR COST OF
15          REMOVAL?

16   A.     No, this is not sound policy for several reasons. Expected outcomes are outcomes that

17          represent what is expected on average. While retirements occasionally exceed or fall short of

18          expectations, these shortfalls and excesses are expected to balance out. One property of a

19          depreciation system is that it typically provides a mechanism for these shortfalls and excesses

20          to accumulate and offset over time. Both the Alliance Study and my analysis adopt the

21          Average Remaining Life Procedure. This procedure spreads excess and deficient depreciation

22          reserves across the remaining lives of assets. In addition to the remaining life procedure,

23          reserve transfers and regulatory asset accounting can be used to mitigate and address temporary

24          excesses or shortfalls.

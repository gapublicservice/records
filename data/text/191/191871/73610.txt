Overview of Depreciation Rates and Expense
Q.What is Depreciation Expense?
A.Depreciation is an expense that represents recovery of the Company’s capital investment over the expected life of the investment. Depreciation expense also accounts for the expected cost of removal (COR) and salvage value over the expected life of the investment.
Q.How does recovery of investment differ from return on investment?
A.Recovery of investment is the recovery of the original investment, while return on investment represents the financing cost on the asset. Assets are financed with a combination of debt and equity.
Q.How is depreciation expense calculated?
A.Depreciation expense is calculated by multiplying the gross plant balance in each Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) account or subaccount by a depreciation rate specific to that account or subaccount. Depreciation rates are approved by the Commission in each general rate case and typically are not changed between rate cases. Depreciation rate recommendations made to the Commission are usually supported by a depreciation study.
Q.What are GEORGIA POWER Company’s recommendations for depreciation rates and what are they based on?
A.Georgia Power Company’s (Company or Georgia Power) proposed depreciation rates are contained in Appendix Exhibit 2 of the Company’s filing. This appendix is a depreciation study performed on behalf of Georgia Power by the Alliance Consulting Group (“Alliance Study”). The Alliance Study includes a general discussion of depreciation at pages 6 through 20. I agree with this general discussion and refer the Commission to it for detail on how depreciation rates are calculated. However, I disagree with certain parameters and findings in the Alliance Study related to specific assets and plant accounts. In this testimony I identify the parameters that I disagree with, recommend alternate parameters, and provide the rates resulting from these alternate parameters. 
Q.What General issues do you raise in this testimony?
A.I address the terminal retirement date for Hatch plant, the decommissioning costs for coal and gas plants, and the survivor curves used to model future retirements.
Q.How should the Commission evaluate and resolve the disputed issues that you raise?
A.The Commission should weigh the evidence in the record that is specific to each plant or account and make a conclusion about the expected retirement outcomes based on that evidence. 
Q.How does chanGing a depreciation rate impact BoTH the Company and Customers?
A.The primary function of depreciation is to allocate costs to customers across time over the service life of the asset. Thus, an increase or decrease in depreciation rates does not have a direct impact on the total amount collected from customers or returned to the Company over time. The primary impact from changing a depreciation rate is the year in which the capital is fully recovered. A lower depreciation rate shifts the recovery of invested capital from the current time period to a future time period. A higher depreciation rate shifts the recovery of invested capital from a future time period to a current time period. 
Q.Why do your recommendations focus on the expected retirement outcomes?
A.A commonly accepted premise of fairness is that those who benefit from a cost should bear the cost. This concept is applied when using cost of service studies to support the allocation of costs across customers. The same concept is used to support the recovery of capital across time. Customers benefit from utility plant only as long as the plant is in service. Thus, the recovery of the investment in plant should be spread across time that plant is expected to be in service. Depreciation rates calculated based on expected retirement outcomes accomplish this goal.
Q.IS IT SOUND POLICY to collect extra expense in order to establish a buffer for unexpected retirements or cost of removal?
A.No, this is not sound policy for several reasons. Expected outcomes are outcomes that represent what is expected on average. While retirements occasionally exceed or fall short of expectations, these shortfalls and excesses are expected to balance out.  One property of a depreciation system is that it typically provides a mechanism for these shortfalls and excesses to accumulate and offset over time. Both the Alliance Study and my analysis adopt the Average Remaining Life Procedure. This procedure spreads excess and deficient depreciation reserves across the remaining lives of assets. In addition to the remaining life procedure, reserve transfers and regulatory asset accounting can be used to mitigate and address temporary excesses or shortfalls.
Because the expected outcome represents the outcome that is most likely to happen, collecting extra depreciation expense to establish a buffer beyond the expected outcome will over-allocate costs to current customers. This is unfair to current customers.
Q.The Alliance Study notes that informed judgment plays a role in studying depreciation. How do you recommend informed judgment be applied? 
A.The National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners (“NARUC”) publication “Public Utility Depreciation Practices” offers the following guidance on the use of informed judgment:
The use of informed judgment can be a major factor in forecasting. A logical process of examining and prioritizing the usefulness of information must be employed, since there are many sources of data that must be considered and weighed by importance. For example, the following forces of retirement need to be considered: Do the past and current service life dispersions represent the future? Will scrap prices rise or fall? What will be the impact of future technological obsolescence? Will the company be in existence in the future? The analyst must rank the factors and decide the relative weight to apply to each. The final estimate might not resemble any one of the specific factors; however, the result would be a decision based upon a combination of the components.

The NARUC manual highlights that informed judgment is a logical process that employs specific information and factors. This is important because it means that, when informed judgment is applied, the information considered and the logic that was applied should be identifiable. In this testimony, when I apply informed judgment, I identify the factors that I considered and the logic that I apply in order to aid the Commission in determining whether my judgment is reasonable. 
Q.What Guidance can aid the Commission in weighing the merits of the informed judgment that you apply and that of the Company?
A.I recommend the Commission apply the results of academic research into the reliability of judgmental forecasts. This research offers five principles for reliable judgmental forecasting:
Organize and present information in a form that clearly emphasizes relevant information.
Limit the amount of information used in judgmental forecasting. Use a small number of particularly important cues.
Use mechanical methods to process information.
Combine several forecasts.
Require justification of forecasts.
I applied these principles in developing the recommendations in my testimony. However, I recommend the Commission place particular emphasis on the final principle of justification of forecasts. This principle is important to follow in a regulatory setting.

Q.How do the issues you mentioned earlier regarding terminal retirement date, decommissioning costs, and Survivor Curves relate to the net salvage rate and remaining life?
A.The terminal retirement date affects the remaining life calculation for assets where an entire group of assets is scheduled to be retired collectively, such as generating units. Q.Are the facts and circumstances of Hatch simmilar to Votgle?
A.Yes.  While there are some differences between these two plants, these differences are immaterial in the context of setting depreciation rates. Q.What is the basis for your judgment that the expected outcome for the Hatch Plant is retirement in 2054 and 2058?

Remaining Lives
Q.How is an Account’s Expected Remaining Life Calculated?
A.Remaining life is determined by modeling when each asset in an account is expected to retire. Like humans, not all assets retire at the same age. The average age of human retirement in the United States is currently 61 years old, but some retire before or after that age. . Retirements of both people and assets follow a statistical distribution, and the shape of that distribution determines the average age of retirement. The figures below illustrate examples of different distributions, expressed as percent surviving. This figure expresses distributions as proportion surviving. In depreciation studies, retirement distributions are typically represented in this format and referred to as survivor curves.
Figure 1

 
Remaining life is the difference between current age and retirement age. For any given retirement distribution, remaining life is a function of current age. Different retirement distributions have different remaining life functions; however, each retirement distribution corresponds to a single remaining life function. Remaining life is thus dependent on the expected distribution of future retirements.

Where there are multiple factors, activities, actions, property characteristics, statistical inconsistencies, property mix in accounts, or a multitude of other considerations that affect the analysis (potentially in various directions), judgment is used to take all of these considerations and synthesize them into a general direction or understanding of the characteristics of the property. Easements
Q.What are the characteristics of easements that affected your judgment?
A.Easements are not physical assets that degrade and fail over time. The Company’s easements have a theoretically unlimited life, but individual easements may be vacated if the Company no longer uses the easement. Thus, if an easement is never vacated, the easement will never be retired.
Q.What is your recommendation for Account 350 Transmission Easements and how does it compare to The Company’s recommendation
A.The figure below illustrates both Georgia Power and Staff’s proposed distributions for Account 350 Transmission Easements. 
Figure 4: Account 350 Transmission Easements

The Original Curve reflects the historic data. The solid blue line reflects the Company’s proposed square (SQ) survival curve with an average life of 85 years. The blue line with x represents the Staff proposal of an S6 curve and an average life of 120 years. The Company’s recommendation provides a poor visual fit after age 85 because the original survivor curve remains at 100 percent while the Company’s proposed survivor curve drops to zero percent, resulting in 100 percent error in every age after 85 years.
The Company offers the following rationale for these parameters: “Since the longest life in this functional group is 85 years, this study recommends retaining an 85-year life and SQ dispersion for this account.” While not explicitly stated, the underlying assumption is that all transmission easements will survive for the expected average life of the longest lived transmission asset (in this case transmission structures and improvements), and that all easements will be abandoned the year following the average life. 
The unreasonableness of this assumption is demonstrated by the Company’s grid investment program. There is no technology currently being contemplated that would lead to the wholesale abandonment of the Company’s easements.  While it is possible that the Company occasionally abandons transmission easements in the future, I expect that the majority of the Company’s easements will be retained as transmission lines are rebuilt rather than retired and vacated. While technological change could bring about a systematic retirement of transmission lines, no such change is likely to occur in the near future.
The historic data indicates 100 percent survival for throughout the entire age range, which is nearly 110 years. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that the actual average life will exceed 110 years by some amount. A square curve is an unreasonable shape for this asset because it is extremely unlikely that all transmission easements will be retired at a fixed age. Every retirement curve, other than a square curve, that fits the historic data predicts life in excess of 140 years. I recommend adopting the 120-S6 curve because this curve is consistent with historical data and the Company’s reinvestment in aging transmission but does not assume substantial life beyond the observed data. This recommendation also gives some weight to the Company’s forecast of an 85-year average life by shortening the life indicated by historical data, which suggests an average life of at least 140 years.

Structures and Improvements
Q.What common factors affect your analysis of structures and improvements accounts?
A.Structures and improvements accounts include building structures, individual components of structures such as roofs, lighting, and heating, and other property improvements such as fencing. Typically, the primary building structures account for most of the total value in these accounts. Certain building types and systems can, with proper maintenance, survive for hundreds of years or more. Other types of buildings with less durable materials or more integrated materials have can have lives from 50 to 100 years, and individual building components such as roofing and lighting typically range in expected life from several years to 40 or more years. Changing land value or land use typically drives the retirement of entire building systems rather than failure of the primary building structure. For accounts with terminal retirement dates, building structures are expected to survive.

As with the other structure accounts, I recommend basing the survivor curve for this account on historic data with less weight given to the oldest assets. I recommend an R0.5 curve with a 65-year average life. Unlike my recommendations for most other asset accounts, my recommendation for this account materially exceeds the actual percent surviving for later ages. I attribute this to a single abnormal retirement that is not representative of expected outcomes. My consistent treatment of placing less weight on tail or older aged portions of the historic survival curves is consistent with the Alliance Study which, quoting Depreciation Systems, states “Points at the end of the curve are often based on fewer exposures and may be given less weight than those points based on larger samples.” My recommendation for this account applies judgment that is consistent with the judgment used for my other recommendations. I consistently recommend curves that are fit to the Company’s historic data but that have lower weight placed on the oldest portions of the survivor curves.

COMPLIANCE REPORT

On December 30, 2025, the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”) issued a final Order Adopting Stipulation and Granting Certification of Capacity from the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP and Supplemental Resources for 2028-2031 Capacity in Docket Nos. 56298 and 56310 (“Order”). In part, the Order requires Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) and the Commission’s Public Interest Advocacy Staff (“PIA Staff”) to (i) work collaboratively to investigate the potential benefits of load flexibility and (ii) report back to the Commission within 120 days of the Final Order in this proceeding regarding the potential for an additional load flexibility program and whether additional steps should be taken to create another program offering, including recommendations for the timing for consideration and approval of such offering, if determined to be beneficial. Prior to the collaboration, the Order requires the Company to engage current and potential large load customers to solicit input on large load flexibility options, including contract terms and cost saving opportunities resulting from flexibility, and to report its findings to Staff. On April 27, 2026, Georgia Power notified the Commission that the Company and PIA Staff had agreed to extend the time to meet and file the report regarding load flexibility to June 30, 2026. The extension allowed time for the parties to collaborate and provided PIA Staff with time necessary to provide feedback to the Company in advance of filing the report. This Compliance Report describes steps the Company and PIA Staff have taken to comply with these requirements of the Order.

SUMMARY

 In accordance with the Order, the Company and PIA Staff met two (2) times, once on June 3, 2026, and once on June 15, 2026, to work collaboratively to investigate the potential benefits of load flexibility. The Company and PIA Staff held robust discussions, shared data and information, and carefully assessed PIA Staff’s thoughts and feedback obtained from large load customers.

SCOPE OF DISCUSSIONS

The Company and PIA Staff addressed multiple issues related to load flexibility, focusing on the following areas:
Current Customer Programs.
Georgia Power offers a robust portfolio of demand side options, including energy efficiency programs, demand response options, programs aimed at leveraging customer-sited assets for the benefit of all customers, as well as rate options that provide dynamic pricing signals to encourage efficient grid utilization. The Company presented a broad portfolio of demand-side and distributed energy resource (“DER”) programs designed to address differing customer needs, including the: 
DER Customer Owned (“DCO”), DER Colocation (“DCL”), DER Pilot programs;
Curtailable Load (“CL”) program; and
Large Customer Owned Resiliency (“LCOR”) program.
Programs vary based on ownership structure (utility vs. customer), rate eligibility, and asset configuration, with eligibility thresholds such as minimum DER size and demand reduction requirements. The current program portfolio reflects a mix of energy efficiency, demand response, dynamic pricing, and customer-sited asset strategies. For customers below 100 megawatts (“MW”), the Company indicated that existing programs generally provide viable pathways to meet customer needs. However, some customers require customized combinations of ownership, compensation structure, and operational control that are not fully aligned with existing program constructs.
Large Load Customer Communications.
The Company shared with PIA Staff that communications with large load customers (including data centers) occur throughout the entire customer lifecycle, rather than at a single defined stage. These discussions take place during early engagement, contracting, and ongoing interactions, with a preference for initiating conversations as early as possible. The Company noted that customer discussions have evolved in recent years, including: 
Continued consideration of onsite generation as a flexibility tool;
Increasing interest in operational flexibility strategies, such as shifting workloads across locations where feasible; and
Adapting communications to reflect this evolving understanding of how flexibility can be achieved in practice.
Recently, the Company has introduced load flexibility language directly into large load customer-specific contract discussions, rather than relying solely on existing program structures, to more directly tie flexibility options into a customer’s site-specific planning processes. A sample of draft load flexibility language for a contract for electric service (“CES”) was provided to PIA Staff and is summarized in the following section. Based on the timing of these contracting cycles, the Company is still in the process of obtaining specific feedback on this language. Communications with customers have been highly project-specific, focusing on individual operational capabilities, site-specific conditions, and customer ability and willingness to provide flexibility.
While each customer is unique, a consistent theme throughout the communications with large load customers is prioritizing speed to power and reliable service, which strongly influences how customers receive flexibility proposals. As a result: 
Utility economic incentives associated with customer-provided flexibility are often viewed as secondary to reliability and interconnection timing considerations; and
Customers evaluate flexibility offers based on whether they support or hinder project timelines and operations.
The Company also shared customer feedback that not all large load customers are similarly positioned to offer flexibility, with key distinctions based on: 
Type of services provided (e.g., critical vs. non-critical);
A data center’s customer contract obligations; and
Availability of alternatives such as onsite generation or geographic load shifting.
More broadly, the discussion reflected that PIA Staff are interested in whether load flexibility could evolve into a formal program or tariff. However, the Company’s current approach focuses on individual customer negotiations, especially for large data center loads, rather than a one-size-fits-all program offering. Based on each large load customer’s unique business profile and site-specific characteristics, a one-size-fits-all strategy to flexibility is not recommended by the Company.
Draft Contract Language:
In response to PIA Staff’s question about the applicability of the load flexibility parameters provided, the Company clarified that the draft contract language could accommodate both (a) applicability during the next several years in order to promote reliability and mitigate operational risk to the grid as large construction projects and sophisticated outage coordination is underway, and (b) the continuation of flexibility throughout the full contract term to defer future generation assets and may provide a customer the associated economic benefits depending on the specific customer needs and willingness and ability to provide flexibility.
Flexibility parameters included in the contract language include: percent (%) of load available for flexibility; term length; number of consecutive days; minimum advanced notice; event triggers; number of hours per year; number of events per day; number of events per year; and maximum call duration per day (hours).
Role of Load Flexibility.
The Company and PIA Staff discussed the role of load flexibility and how it translates to planning value. Over the next several years, flexibility from the Company’s largest new customers promotes reliable growth and provides operational benefits to the transmission system. Given anticipated capacity needs in the near-future, load flexibility could allow the interconnection of some large load customers sooner, but each outcome will depend on the specifics of the project.  Over a longer time period, flexibility may be used to reduce the amount of generation the Company needs to build or procure while maintaining reliability for all customers. The Company explained that based on the agreed upon parameters (e.g., hours curtailed, timing, frequency) the long-term MW value would be evaluated through modeling (SERVM) to determine the Effective Load Carrying Capability (“ELCC”). The ELCC would be applied to the load flexibility amount before load flexibility capacity would be included in the Company’s resource plans. Planning does not rely on a single peak hour but instead relies on system-wide conditions across time. Therefore, capacity credit depends on when and how often load flexibility is available, not just the absolute load reduction.
System Planning and Generation Needs
PIA Staff provided feedback that their goal for load flexibility is to avoid or defer new generation capacity in light of significant large load growth. In the near term, the Company is focused on securing load flexibility for operational reliability (e.g., local outages, system constraints), while discussing the economic incentives for customers who can maintain load flexibility that would defer generation capacity. Recent discussions with PIA Staff have focused primarily on capacity value; however, additional evaluation of energy impacts will be warranted.
Customer Incentives for Load Flexibility.
The Company provided an overview of how it intends to reflect the economic incentive to customers. Because these customers are billed on rates that include real-time pricing (“RTP”), their tariffs already incorporate an appropriate price signal to incent customers to curtail load when pricing is high. Additionally, load flexibility parameters that result in a generation asset deferral and reduce the incremental costs to serve the customer will appropriately be reflected in the associated contract parameters (e.g., minimum bills). 
PIA Staff questioned whether the proposed incentive structure provides sufficient economic incentive for customers to accept curtailment and long-duration or frequent events. Discussions with customers regarding pricing expectations for load flexibility are ongoing. To date, those discussions have not yet progressed to the point where customers have provided specific feedback on a long-term economic proposal. Customer discussions remain focused on the technical feasibility of the load flexibility parameters.
NEXT STEPS

Georgia Power and PIA Staff plan to continue to discuss load flexibility as the parameters are negotiated through large load contracts. PIA Staff continues to have the ability to review those contracts, and the Company will work with PIA Staff to ensure questions related to load flexibility are appropriately addressed. To that end, concurrent with any new contract submitted to the Commission  for approval, the Company will provide an assessment of potential implementation of load flexibility for the project, identifying the extent to which the load is suited to participate and the basis for that determination. The Company will also continue to work with all customers regarding participation in programs for which they qualify.
CONCLUSION

The Company and PIA Staff have complied with the Order as pertaining to Paragraph 8 of the Stipulation by engaging in a substantial, productive evaluation of all relevant issues related to load flexibility.

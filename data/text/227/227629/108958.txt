II.OVERVIEW OF RTP RATES
Q.WHAT IS THE PHILOSOPHY AND INTENT OF RTP RATES?
A.RTP rates are a collection of tariffs that provide eligible customers the option to take on price risk in exchange for potential cost savings. The Company provides participating customers an hourly price that reflects its expectation of the marginal cost of electricity. Since electricity cannot be stored to a sufficient degree, during some constrained periods, prices can reach extremely high levels. Customers can make economic decisions with their business operations and choose to buy or reduce consumption during these periods. RTP is not intended to instantaneously capture the average or total cost of an RTP customer’s load in its entirety. Rather, the intent of RTP is to represent the marginal cost of making marginal changes in demand. 
Any revenue collected from incremental load in excess of the total marginal cost to serve that load will benefit the existing customer base. The total marginal cost to serve incremental load can be reliably and accurately calculated using the cost of peaking capacity, priced against demand during peak periods, and the marginal production cost of the system in all hours. This analytical approach is known as the peaker methodology, which represents the total capacity and energy cost to serve demand from an optimal resource mix and provides a proper basis for the Company’s RTP rates. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE GEORGIA POWER’S RTP RATES. 
A.Georgia Power’s RTP tariffs are two-part rate structures that blend standard embedded rates with a marginal cost-based rate. The standard embedded rate is applied to the Customer Baseline Load (“CBL”), while the RTP rate is assessed to incremental load and reflects hourly marginal costs. The standard embedded rate collects variable and fixed costs incurred by the Company while the RTP rate represents the costs that change alongside variations in load. Figure 1 below provides an illustration of the two-part RTP structure:
Figure 1: RTP Structure

Because RTP rates are derived from marginal costs, they send economically efficient price signals to participating customers—encouraging usage when the marginal unit cost is lower and discouraging usage when that cost increases. These price signals are provided to participating customers either daily under the RTP-DA tariff or hourly under the RTP-HA tariff. 
Q.WHO IS ELIGIBLE TO PARTICIPATE IN RTP?
A.The Company’s RTP tariffs are available to commercial and industrial customers with a minimum demand of 250 kilowatts (“kW”) (RTP-DA) or 5 megawatts (“MW”) (RTP-HA). Currently, there are approximately 2,300 customers on RTP-DA and 170 on RTP-HA.
Q.WHAT ARE THE COMPONENTS OF THE RTP RATE?
A.The five components of the RTP rate are: 
Lambda – the marginal cost of the last system resource used to serve the last increment of system load. Lambda is the largest component in RTP prices, amounting to 75-80% of the average annual RTP price and is found in the price every hour of the year. This is the component of RTP from which fuel revenues are extracted and thus the component most relevant to this docket. 
Losses (RTP-DA Only) – the physical electric losses incurred between the bulk transmission system and the customer.  
Recovery Factor – accounts for the Company’s forecasting risk since RTP prices are based on a forecasted marginal cost and are not trued-up. It provides a contribution to other fixed costs above marginal cost. 
Transmission Cost – reflects the marginal transmission capital costs. The transmission cost is designed to trigger in approximately 300 hours in a normal weather year and corresponds to periods when system loads approach the forecasted annual peak load. It also includes a temperature element that reflects the load carrying capability of the transmission system. 
Reliability Capacity Cost – reflects the marginal value of generation capacity. This component is designed to trigger in approximately 50 hours of a weather normal year and corresponds to periods when system load approaches the forecasted annual peak load.
Q.HOW DO THE COMPANY’S RTP RATES SEND PRICE SIGNALS TO PARTICIPATING CUSTOMERS?
A.Hourly price signals are accessible to participating customers through the Company’s EnergyDirect tool. For RTP-DA customers, RTP prices are posted by 4:00 pm every day for the next day. For RTP-HA customers, prices are posted every hour for the next hour.
Q.WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF THE COMPANY’S RTP RATES? 
A.RTP rates enable customers to use electricity efficiently and economically by providing a price signal that reflects the marginal cost of producing or reducing energy. Customers may increase usage when it is least costly to produce and respond to high prices by reducing load during peak periods. This load response is incorporated into the Company’s planning process through a combination of a reduction to forecasted load and a demand side option. To the extent customers do not adjust their usage based on the price signals communicated, they always face a price reflecting the hourly marginal cost.  
Access to marginal cost-based rates makes RTP a driver of new economic activity and has been an important tool in attracting incremental load to the state. Importantly, as new projects are evaluated, the Company ensures that the expected revenues more than cover the incremental costs to serve new load. Revenues in excess of the incremental cost put downward pressure on rates for all customers.  
III.HISTORY OF RTP AND THE REVENUE FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY
Q.WHEN WERE THE RTP RATES INTRODUCED?
A.The Company began offering RTP rates when the Commission approved Georgia Power’s experimental Real Time Pricing Tariff, Rate Schedule RTP-X, to operate as a two-year pilot program on June 19, 1992. RTP rates became a permanent rate offering in July 1994 when the Commission approved Georgia Power’s Real Time Pricing-Day Ahead Tariff, Rate Schedule RTP-DA-1.  
Q.WHEN WERE THE RTP OFFERINGS MODIFIED TO EMPLOY THE CURRENT RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY?
A.The Company’s RTP offerings were modified in the 2010 base rate case to employ the current accounting methodology for crediting the FCR account from RTP revenue. Prior to that, the standard FCR rate was used to divide RTP revenues between fuel and base. 
Q.WHAT CHANGE DID THE COMMISSION APPROVE TO THE RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY AT THAT TIME?
A.In the 2010 rate case, the Commission approved Georgia Power’s proposal to record RTP fuel revenues based on the average marginal replacement fuel costs (rather than the embedded FCR rate) on an hourly basis. This method appropriately records more RTP revenues to fuel when marginal fuel prices are high and less when they are low, which mirrors the concepts behind the RTP rates and better tracks the RTP revenues collected with the marginal fuel costs being incurred. Notably, the implementation of this revised accounting method did not affect the RTP prices paid by customers but rather more appropriately records the revenues received from RTP customers between fuel and base.
Q.WHY DID THE COMPANY PROPOSE THIS CHANGE?
A.The Company proposed this accounting change to the RTP fuel credit methodology (1) in response to fuel prices becoming increasingly volatile, growing fuel balance over/under-recoveries and extended amortization periods; (2) to better mirror the overall RTP rate; and (3) to more quickly reflect changes in fuel costs incurred by the Company. As discussed above, the intent of the RTP rate has always been to assess the marginal cost associated with marginal changes in demand. It makes sense that the amount recorded to fuel would reflect the same philosophy and intent. 
The revised methodology aligns fuel revenues and expenses especially during periods of fuel price volatility. The RTP hourly price can vary significantly depending on marginal replacement fuel costs while the FCR rate remains constant, which can create deviations between costs incurred and amounts credited to the fuel and base accounts. By relying on a fixed rate to record RTP revenues to the fuel account, the previous accounting method did not credit the proper amounts of fuel revenues in the same period in which those costs were incurred. 
Q.DID THIS CHANGE IN METHODOLOGY AFFECT RTP PRICES?
A.No. The RTP fuel credit methodology change in 2010 did not impact RTP prices. This accounting change only affected how RTP revenues were recorded as between fuel and base.
Q.DID THIS CHANGE IN FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY HAVE AN IMPACT ON BASE RATES?
A.Yes. Because the change in methodology altered how RTP revenues were divided between fuel and base, more revenues were estimated to be included in base rates in 2011. As an indicator of the impact of the accounting methodology change, the base revenue contribution forecasted in 2011 increased by 45 million dollars, which had the effect of lowering the overall revenue requirement increase by the same amount. 
Q.WOULD CHANGING THE METHODOLOGY FOLLOWING THIS INVESTIGATION CHANGE BASE RATE REVENUE?
A.Yes. 
IV.RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY
Current Methodology
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE CURRENT RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY.
A.For the incremental load priced at RTP, the revenues based on an average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost are recorded to the fuel account. This average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost is based on all Georgia Power generating units running in each hour plus Georgia Power’s share of pool sales or purchases. For the CBL portion of RTP customer load, fuel costs are billed and revenues are collected as applicable at the underlying FCR rate. 
Q.ARE ALL FUEL EXPENSES THAT ARE RECOVERED THROUGH THE FUEL CLAUSE CAPTURED IN THE RTP FUEL CREDIT CALCULATION?
A.No. The RTP fuel credit is based on marginal replacement fuel costs as the RTP rate reflects the marginal cost of serving incremental load. This does not incorporate fixed fuel costs, which do not vary with changes in demand. Fixed fuel costs are costs that the Company will incur regardless of changes in RTP incremental load and thus cannot be increased or avoided through changes in load priced at RTP. A contribution to fixed costs is made through the capital substitution component as discussed further below. 
Q.CAN YOU GIVE EXAMPLES OF THESE FIXED COSTS?
A.Yes. In the FCR-27 docket, fixed fuel costs related to non-dispatchable PPAs, existing unit FT costs, and FT costs associated with new generation being added to serve large loads and organic growth were identified as examples of fuel expenses not included in the RTP fuel credit calculation.
Q.WHAT ARE NON-DISPATCHABLE PPAS?
A.Non-dispatchable PPAs are those contracts in which the Company has an obligation to pay for the output of the resource when energy is produced regardless of the system’s need or current cost position. These have also been referred to as take-or-pay contracts. These are distinguished from dispatchable PPAs in which the Company can control the output of the resource in response to changes in the marginal costs of the resource or load on the system. The Company’s renewable PPAs including solar, wind, and biomass are currently structured as non-dispatchable. The execution and certification of non-dispatchable PPAs and their associated costs are not driven by large load growth. 
Q.WHAT COSTS ARE INCLUDED IN EXISTING FT?
A.Firm Transportation costs are monthly pipeline capacity reservation payments that ensure gas pipeline transportation availability to existing Georgia Power units during all periods. These costs do not include any variable pipeline transportation costs or commodity costs, which are dependent on delivery volume, priced on a volumetric basis, and captured in the marginal replacement fuel costs used in the RTP fuel credit. 
Q.HOW IS THAT DIFFERENT FROM NEW FT COSTS?
A.New FT costs are those payments that Georgia Power incurs to ensure gas availability for new or future generation that is in the Company’s plans. The Company is distinguishing new FT from existing FT in recognition that the growth of large load customers is a primary driver of new FT costs and they may need to be treated differently. As with the non-dispatchable PPAs, existing FT costs are not driven by large load growth.
Q.IF THESE FIXED COSTS ARE NOT INCLUDED IN THE RTP FUEL CREDIT CALCULATION, DOES THAT MEAN THAT RTP CUSTOMERS ARE NOT CONTRIBUTING TO THESE FIXED COSTS? 
A.No. The RTP price charged to incremental load in all hours has a component, lambda, which is the marginal cost of the marginal unit on the system. In nearly all hours, the marginal running cost is greater than the average running cost of the system, which means that in virtually all periods, RTP incremental load is contributing to fixed costs. The Company has referred to this as the capital substitution component. Further, the CBL portion of an RTP customer’s load captures the fully embedded base and FCR rate, which directly includes the fixed costs.  
Q.YOU PREVIOUSLY MENTIONED A CAPITAL SUBSTITUTION COMPONENT, CAN YOU ELABORATE?
A.Yes. The difference between lambda and the recorded fuel revenue covers two pieces: generation variable O&M and capital substitution. The capital substitution contribution, which is applied to base rates, covers additional fixed costs above the reliability capacity cost associated with resources that support lower fuel costs, including capital costs for baseload or intermediate generation and FT. 
Q.WHAT IS THE MAGNITUDE OF THAT CAPITAL SUBSTITUTION COMPONENT? 
A.The amount will vary from year to year based on the spread between the marginal and average running costs of the system and the volume of load that is priced at RTP. The Company performed an analysis based on 2025 actual data and the capital substitution contribution for 2025 was $264 million. 
Review and Revision to Fuel Credit Methodology
Q.WHY IS THE COMMISSION REVIEWING THE COMPANY’S RTP FUEL CREDIT AND REVENUE ALLOCATION METHODOLOGY?
A.Over the past 16 years since the current RTP fuel credit methodology was established, the system has evolved in both the types of fuel expenses incurred and the magnitude of loads served through RTP offerings. In the FCR-27 proceeding, in light of the increase in large load customers served on Georgia Power’s RTP offerings and to ensure such customers pay their share of costs, PIA Staff and intervenors questioned whether the RTP revenues were appropriately allocated between fuel and base rates and recommended the Commission undertake an additional review of the RTP fuel credit and revenue allocation methodology. As demonstrated in the Stipulation ultimately approved in Docket No. 56765, the Company supported and agreed to this subsequent review.
Q.DO LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS PAY FOR THEIR COSTS?
A.Yes. Per the Company’s revised rules and regulations approved by the Commission in 2025, Georgia Power has committed to protect existing customers from bearing any of the costs of adding these large customers. The Company’s approach ensures not only that large load customers pay for their costs but also that all customers benefit from adding them to the system. As previously noted, when revenues exceed the incremental cost to serve, it creates downward pressure on rates for all other customers. These benefits enabled the Company to freeze base rates through 2028 and ensure the incremental revenue from large load customers has downward pressure of at least $102 per year for the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month for years 2029, 2030, and 2031. 
Q.IF LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS PAY FOR THEIR COSTS, WHY IS THE RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY BEING CHALLENGED?
A.The revenues Georgia Power receives from large load customers pay for the costs the Company incurs to serve them. The question raised in FCR-27 and continued in this proceeding is whether those RTP revenues are appropriately being divided between fuel and base. 
Q.IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING ANY MODIFICATIONS TO ITS FUEL COST RECOVERY IN THIS PROCEEDING?
A.Yes. The Company proposes that the costs of new FT attributable to large load customers be fully recovered from those customers and not borne by non-large load customers in the FCR clause. Following the conclusion of the FCR-27 case, the Company reviewed its current RTP fuel credit methodology, conducted an RTP Technical Conference where it received feedback from PIA Staff and intervenors, and determined that this modification was appropriate to align with the intent of the fuel credit methodology.
 Q.WHAT MECHANISM WOULD THE COMPANY USE TO ALLOCATE THESE NEW FT COSTS?
A.In their FCR-27 testimony filed in Docket No. 56765, PIA Staff showed a 1-CP (coincident peak) allocation of FT costs. The Company agrees with this approach but proposes using a winter CP and basing the allocation on the share of organic versus large load demand growth. This approach is consistent with the capacity need that drove the incremental generation resources and the allocation in the large load cost model. 
Q.WERE THERE OTHER ITEMS RAISED DURING THE FCR PROCEEDING AND THE TECHNICAL CONFERENCE THAT WARRANT FURTHER DISCUSSION?
A.Yes. PIA Staff and intervenors have expressed concern regarding the treatment of non-dispatchable PPAs in the RTP fuel credit calculation. Such a change to the treatment of non-dispatchable PPAs will impact the fuel allocation for all RTP incremental load, including non-large load commercial and industrial customers. 
Q.DESCRIBE THE CURRENT TREATMENT OF NON-DISPATCHABLE PPAS IN THE RTP FUEL CREDIT CALCULATION. 
A.As approved, the RTP fuel credit rate is derived from the marginal replacement fuel costs of all Georgia Power units. Although there are some units, such as the non-dispatchable PPAs, which do not have marginal replacement fuel costs, the output of those units is included in the denominator of the RTP fuel credit rate calculation.  
Q.WHAT OPTIONS ARE AVAILABLE TO ADDRESS THEIR CONCERN? 
A.There are two potential options to address their concern. The first option is to exclude both the costs and the output from non-dispatchable PPAs in the RTP fuel credit calculation. This is consistent with an approach that the RTP fuel credit should be derived from marginal replacement fuel costs. Removing non-dispatchable PPA output (MWh) from the denominator of the RTP fuel credit calculation will increase the RTP fuel credit rate and thus the recording of all RTP revenues to the fuel clause. However, this approach is not based on all Georgia Power generating units running, as originally approved. The second option is to include both the costs and the MWh from non-dispatchable PPAs in the RTP fuel credit calculation. This would break from the marginal fuel cost-based approach approved and in place since 2010 but also result in a larger allocation of RTP revenues to the fuel clause. 
V.CONCLUSION
Q.DOES THIS CONCLUDE YOUR TESTIMONY? 
A.Yes.

17                          II.     OVERVIEW OF RTP RATES

18   Q.   WHAT IS THE PHILOSOPHY AND INTENT OF RTP RATES?

19   A.   RTP rates are a collection of tariffs that provide eligible customers the option to
20        take on price risk in exchange for potential cost savings. The Company provides
21        participating customers an hourly price that reflects its expectation of the marginal
22        cost of electricity. Since electricity cannot be stored to a sufficient degree, during
23        some constrained periods, prices can reach extremely high levels. Customers can
24        make economic decisions with their business operations and choose to buy or
25        reduce consumption during these periods. RTP is not intended to instantaneously
26        capture the average or total cost of an RTP customer’s load in its entirety. Rather,


                 Direct Testimony of Dr. Ross Beppler, Lee Evans, and Brandon Looney
                                On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                          Docket No. 57171
                                             Page 6 of 18

 1          the intent of RTP is to represent the marginal cost of making marginal changes in
 2          demand.

 3          Any revenue collected from incremental load in excess of the total marginal cost to
 4          serve that load will benefit the existing customer base. The total marginal cost to
 5          serve incremental load can be reliably and accurately calculated using the cost of
 6          peaking capacity, priced against demand during peak periods, and the marginal
 7          production cost of the system in all hours. This analytical approach is known as the
 8          peaker methodology, which represents the total capacity and energy cost to serve
 9          demand from an optimal resource mix and provides a proper basis for the
10          Company’s RTP rates.

15          incremental load and reflects hourly marginal costs. The standard embedded rate
16          collects variable and fixed costs incurred by the Company while the RTP rate
17          represents the costs that change alongside variations in load. Figure 1 below
18          provides an illustration of the two-part RTP structure:

12   Q.   WHAT ARE THE COMPONENTS OF THE RTP RATE?

13   A.   The five components of the RTP rate are:
14        (1) Lambda – the marginal cost of the last system resource used to serve the last
15           increment of system load. Lambda is the largest component in RTP prices,
16           amounting to 75-80% of the average annual RTP price and is found in the price
17           every hour of the year. This is the component of RTP from which fuel revenues
18           are extracted and thus the component most relevant to this docket.

                                On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                          Docket No. 57171

 1        (2) Losses (RTP-DA Only) – the physical electric losses incurred between the bulk
 2           transmission system and the customer.
 3        (3) Recovery Factor – accounts for the Company’s forecasting risk since RTP
 4           prices are based on a forecasted marginal cost and are not trued-up. It provides
 5           a contribution to other fixed costs above marginal cost.
 6        (4) Transmission Cost – reflects the marginal transmission capital costs. The
 7           transmission cost is designed to trigger in approximately 300 hours in a normal
 8           weather year and corresponds to periods when system loads approach the
 9           forecasted annual peak load. It also includes a temperature element that reflects
10           the load carrying capability of the transmission system.
11        (5) Reliability Capacity Cost – reflects the marginal value of generation capacity.
12           This component is designed to trigger in approximately 50 hours of a weather
13           normal year and corresponds to periods when system load approaches the
14           forecasted annual peak load.

20        hour for the next hour.

21   Q.   WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF THE COMPANY’S RTP RATES?

24        energy. Customers may increase usage when it is least costly to produce and
25        respond to high prices by reducing load during peak periods. This load response is
26        incorporated into the Company’s planning process through a combination of a
27        reduction to forecasted load and a demand side option. To the extent customers do

 3        Access to marginal cost-based rates makes RTP a driver of new economic activity

 8         III.     HISTORY OF RTP AND THE REVENUE FUEL CREDIT
 9                                METHODOLOGY

10   Q.   WHEN WERE THE RTP RATES INTRODUCED?

11   A.   The Company began offering RTP rates when the Commission approved Georgia
12        Power’s experimental Real Time Pricing Tariff, Rate Schedule RTP-X, to operate
13        as a two-year pilot program on June 19, 1992. RTP rates became a permanent rate
14        offering in July 1994 when the Commission approved Georgia Power’s Real Time
15        Pricing-Day Ahead Tariff, Rate Schedule RTP-DA-1.

16   Q.   WHEN WERE THE RTP OFFERINGS MODIFIED TO EMPLOY THE
17        CURRENT RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY?

18   A.   The Company’s RTP offerings were modified in the 2010 base rate case to employ
19        the current accounting methodology for crediting the FCR account from RTP
20        revenue. Prior to that, the standard FCR rate was used to divide RTP revenues
21        between fuel and base.

22   Q.   WHAT CHANGE DID THE COMMISSION APPROVE TO THE RTP
23        FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY AT THAT TIME?

24   A.   In the 2010 rate case, the Commission approved Georgia Power’s proposal to
25        record RTP fuel revenues based on the average marginal replacement fuel costs
26        (rather than the embedded FCR rate) on an hourly basis. This method appropriately


                  Direct Testimony of Dr. Ross Beppler, Lee Evans, and Brandon Looney
                                 On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                           Docket No. 57171
                                              Page 10 of 18

 1        records more RTP revenues to fuel when marginal fuel prices are high and less
 2        when they are low, which mirrors the concepts behind the RTP rates and better
 3        tracks the RTP revenues collected with the marginal fuel costs being incurred.
 4        Notably, the implementation of this revised accounting method did not affect the
 5        RTP prices paid by customers but rather more appropriately records the revenues
 6        received from RTP customers between fuel and base.

 7   Q.   WHY DID THE COMPANY PROPOSE THIS CHANGE?

 8   A.   The Company proposed this accounting change to the RTP fuel credit methodology
 9        (1) in response to fuel prices becoming increasingly volatile, growing fuel balance
10        over/under-recoveries and extended amortization periods; (2) to better mirror the
11        overall RTP rate; and (3) to more quickly reflect changes in fuel costs incurred by
12        the Company. As discussed above, the intent of the RTP rate has always been to
13        assess the marginal cost associated with marginal changes in demand. It makes
14        sense that the amount recorded to fuel would reflect the same philosophy and intent.

15        The revised methodology aligns fuel revenues and expenses especially during
16        periods of fuel price volatility. The RTP hourly price can vary significantly
17        depending on marginal replacement fuel costs while the FCR rate remains constant,
18        which can create deviations between costs incurred and amounts credited to the fuel
19        and base accounts. By relying on a fixed rate to record RTP revenues to the fuel
20        account, the previous accounting method did not credit the proper amounts of fuel
21        revenues in the same period in which those costs were incurred.

22   Q.   DID THIS CHANGE IN METHODOLOGY AFFECT RTP PRICES?

23   A.   No. The RTP fuel credit methodology change in 2010 did not impact RTP prices.
24        This accounting change only affected how RTP revenues were recorded as between
25        fuel and base.

12                    IV.      RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY

13   Current Methodology

14   Q.    PLEASE       DESCRIBE          THE       CURRENT          RTP       FUEL     CREDIT
15         METHODOLOGY.

16   A.    For the incremental load priced at RTP, the revenues based on an average hourly
17         marginal replacement fuel cost are recorded to the fuel account. This average hourly
18         marginal replacement fuel cost is based on all Georgia Power generating units
19         running in each hour plus Georgia Power’s share of pool sales or purchases. For
20         the CBL portion of RTP customer load, fuel costs are billed and revenues are
21         collected as applicable at the underlying FCR rate.

 1   Q.   ARE ALL FUEL EXPENSES THAT ARE RECOVERED THROUGH THE
 2        FUEL      CLAUSE        CAPTURED           IN     THE      RTP      FUEL     CREDIT
 3        CALCULATION?

 4   A.   No. The RTP fuel credit is based on marginal replacement fuel costs as the RTP
 5        rate reflects the marginal cost of serving incremental load. This does not incorporate
 6        fixed fuel costs, which do not vary with changes in demand. Fixed fuel costs are
 7        costs that the Company will incur regardless of changes in RTP incremental load
 8        and thus cannot be increased or avoided through changes in load priced at RTP. A
 9        contribution to fixed costs is made through the capital substitution component as
10        discussed further below.

11   Q.   CAN YOU GIVE EXAMPLES OF THESE FIXED COSTS?

12   A.   Yes. In the FCR-27 docket, fixed fuel costs related to non-dispatchable PPAs,
13        existing unit FT costs, and FT costs associated with new generation being added to
14        serve large loads and organic growth were identified as examples of fuel expenses
15        not included in the RTP fuel credit calculation.

16   Q.   WHAT ARE NON-DISPATCHABLE PPAS?

17   A.   Non-dispatchable PPAs are those contracts in which the Company has an obligation
18        to pay for the output of the resource when energy is produced regardless of the
19        system’s need or current cost position. These have also been referred to as take-or-
20        pay contracts. These are distinguished from dispatchable PPAs in which the
21        Company can control the output of the resource in response to changes in the
22        marginal costs of the resource or load on the system. The Company’s renewable
23        PPAs including solar, wind, and biomass are currently structured as non-
24        dispatchable. The execution and certification of non-dispatchable PPAs and their
25        associated costs are not driven by large load growth.

 1   Q.   WHAT COSTS ARE INCLUDED IN EXISTING FT?

 2   A.   Firm Transportation costs are monthly pipeline capacity reservation payments that
 3        ensure gas pipeline transportation availability to existing Georgia Power units
 4        during all periods. These costs do not include any variable pipeline transportation
 5        costs or commodity costs, which are dependent on delivery volume, priced on a
 6        volumetric basis, and captured in the marginal replacement fuel costs used in the
 7        RTP fuel credit.

 8   Q.   HOW IS THAT DIFFERENT FROM NEW FT COSTS?

 9   A.   New FT costs are those payments that Georgia Power incurs to ensure gas
10        availability for new or future generation that is in the Company’s plans. The
11        Company is distinguishing new FT from existing FT in recognition that the growth
12        of large load customers is a primary driver of new FT costs and they may need to
13        be treated differently. As with the non-dispatchable PPAs, existing FT costs are not
14        driven by large load growth.

15   Q.   IF THESE FIXED COSTS ARE NOT INCLUDED IN THE RTP FUEL
16        CREDIT CALCULATION, DOES THAT MEAN THAT RTP CUSTOMERS
17        ARE NOT CONTRIBUTING TO THESE FIXED COSTS?

18   A.   No. The RTP price charged to incremental load in all hours has a component,
19        lambda, which is the marginal cost of the marginal unit on the system. In nearly all
20        hours, the marginal running cost is greater than the average running cost of the
21        system, which means that in virtually all periods, RTP incremental load is
22        contributing to fixed costs. The Company has referred to this as the capital
23        substitution component. Further, the CBL portion of an RTP customer’s load
24        captures the fully embedded base and FCR rate, which directly includes the fixed
25        costs.

 3   A.    Yes. The difference between lambda and the recorded fuel revenue covers two

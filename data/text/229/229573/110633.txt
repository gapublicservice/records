I.     BACKGROUND

       A.      Overview of LTE

       LTE is a South Dakota corporation (EXHIBIT A) with principal offices at 244 Fifth Avenue,

Suite W221, Floor 2, New York, NY 10001, and registered with the Georgia Secretary of State on May

26, 2022 (EXHIBIT B). LTE also holds a Certificate of Authority from the Commission to operate as a

competitive local exchange carrier (“CLEC”) within the State (EXHIBIT C). In addition to its

operations within the State, LTE is certified as a local exchange carrier in the following states: New

York, Ohio, Indiana, Oregon, North Dakota, Iowa, Missouri, Kentucky, Wisconsin, Washington,

Vermont, and Illinois, with applications pending in Texas and Pennsylvania. LTE is also designated as

an ETC in the following states: North Dakota, Minnesota, Texas, and Missouri. No application by LTE

for local exchange carrier certification or ETC designation has ever been denied.

       LTE is financially strong, carrying zero debt or outside investment. LTE participates in the

Lifeline consortium to lobby for favorable rules and regulations that promote longevity and stability of

the program. With support from the federal Lifeline program, LTE will be able to access federal funds

to provide broadband access to low-income Georgia residents and will be able to discount its phone

plans to affordable levels for public housing residents, the homeless, students, the elderly, and other

Georgia residents who struggle to afford broadband access.

       LTE will provide affordable telecommunications services primarily to low-income consumers

throughout the United States, including those in Georgia. These services will include prepaid wireless

services, fixed-wireless home phone service, and fixed-wireless home broadband service. The service

areas in which LTE requests designation are listed in EXHIBIT F. LTE will provide these voice, SMS,

and data services using its own core network facilities together with resold last-mile facilities,

including the radio access networks of its carrier partners PWG Wireless. It will also provide handsets

to its customers. LTE will manage all parts of the customer experience, including pricing, handset

selection, marketing materials, sales, live customer service, and the underlying telecommunications

facilities.

          B.       Commission Authority to Designate ETCs

The Commission has the requisite authority to perform the limited ETC designation requested herein.

Section 214(e)(2) of the Act provides state public utility commissions with the “primary responsibility”

for the designation of ETCs.4 Under the Act, a state public utility commission with jurisdictional

authority over ETC designations must designate a common carrier as an ETC if the carrier satisfies the

requirements of Section 214(e)(1).5 The Commission exercises this authority under Section 214(e)(2)

and Commission Rule 515-12-1-.35, and has previously designated wireless carriers as Lifeline-only

ETCs in Georgia.6 In addition to its wireless operations, LTE holds a Commission Certificate of

Authority as a CLEC, which gives the Commission a clear basis on which to grant ETC status. By

proposing to offer all of the services supported by the federal universal service fund (“USF”) and

advertising the availability of such services, LTE will meet all of the requirements of Section 214 of the

Act, warranting its designation as an ETC by the Commission.

        The 1996 Act and the FCC in its enabling orders provide guidance for determining the public

interest.60 The overarching principles embodied in the 1996 Act are to “promote competition and reduce

regulation[,] . . . secure lower prices and higher quality services[,] . . . and encourage the rapid

deployment of new telecommunications technologies.”61 In its implementing orders, the FCC has ruled

that the pro-competitive and deregulatory directives from Congress required USF support mechanisms

to be competitively neutral and portable among eligible carriers.62

carriers satisfy the federal requirements for ETC designation, and has cautioned state commissions

against imposing artificial barriers to competitive entry by wireless carriers, particularly in rural areas

where wireless carriers are best positioned to compete with wireline carriers.63 The Commission has

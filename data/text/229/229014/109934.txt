              PETITIONERS’ OPPOSITION TO VOLTAGRID’S MOTION
                           FOR EXPEDITED RULING

       Petitioners object to VoltaGrid’s Motion for Expedited Ruling filed October 1. VoltaGrid’s

request to amend the deadline for filing direct testimony would harm Petitioners without good

cause. Petitioners do not object to a prompt resolution of VoltaGrid’s motion to dismiss upon the

filing of Petitioners’ response, though Petitioners do not believe it is prudent, in light of the

complexity and significance of the issues, for the Hearing Officer to preemptively constrain

himself to one week to opine on some of the case’s key legal questions. Petitioners do support

resolution of the Motion to Dismiss as soon as practicable.

    I.       VoltaGrid fails to establish good cause for this request and condensing hearing
             preparation deadlines would prejudice Petitioners.
         VoltaGrid fails to demonstrate “good cause” for its request, as required by this Hearing

Officer’s Scheduling Order. 1 In yesterday’s Motion, VoltaGrid asks the Hearing Officer to

condense Petitioners’ window to prepare rebuttal testimony from 21 days to 7 so that VoltaGrid

can avoid “prepar[ing] direct testimony which may never be presented or may prove irrelevant.” 2

(VoltaGrid has noticed a single direct witness. 3)

         At the outset of this matter, VoltaGrid had an opportunity to object, or request

modifications, to the Hearing Officer’s Scheduling Order. It did neither. VoltaGrid also had the

opportunity to seek stipulations of factual issues to winnow the issues required in direct testimony:

It did not. Though VoltaGrid belatedly responded to Petitioners’ efforts to narrow the factual

landscape, it did so by objecting to its own public statements, 4 by objecting to the authenticity of

its own produced documents, 5 and by objecting to its own language used in discovery responses. 6

         After service of the Petition in this docket, VoltaGrid waited 9 weeks and 2 days to file

motions to dismiss and to stay the proceedings. The Hearing Officer correctly ruled that a stay is

inappropriate. And now, VoltaGrid makes a second request for the Hearing Officer to amend the

scheduling order to accommodate VoltaGrid’s motion. Here, VoltaGrid asks the Hearing Officer to

     II.      A hearing remains required to develop a full factual record.

           Petitioners will address VoltaGrid’s repeated misapplication of the law in their forthcoming

response to a Motion to Dismiss. However, Petitioners agree that resolution of the Motion to

Dismiss could clarify legal questions arising in this matter, and Petitioners support the resolution

of that Motion as soon as practicable.

           As for the need for factual development, VoltaGrid suggests that the resolution of the

Motion to Dismiss is likely to dispose of the need for a hearing. It is wrong. The Scheduling Order

identifies four matters at issue that “will be decided on the basis of facts and arguments presented

at the hearing.” No stipulations of fact have been reached that would obviate the need for that

hearing. To the contrary, nearly all factual issues in this case are presently disputed. VoltaGrid’s

assertion that “no material facts are in dispute” is wrong, misleading, and it ignores the purpose of

an adversarial process, which is to allow factual development from various perspectives, in which

all parties are entitled to present evidence and develop a factual record to aid the finder of fact in

reaching the truth. 11

12
   For example, in a 2000 Commission order on a request for declaratory judgment as to the Commission’s jurisdiction

particular case and the question depends on such factors as the extent of the service, whether the operation holds
itself out as ready to serve the public generally—and whether, perhaps, in other ways it has conducted itself as a
public utility.” Id. (emphasis in original). Likewise, the Georgia Supreme Court in 1971 concluded that a gas
company’s provision of a service, which included electricity, was a regulated contract, considering factors including
the impact of the deal on the public, whether the gas company owned the buildings being served, and the fact that the
gas company had looked for other business opportunities selling similar “total energy systems” including electricity.
Atlanta Gas Light v. Georgia Public Service Commission, 228 Ga. 347 at 351-352 (1971).

discussions with Southern Company Services about potential customers’ behind-the-meter power

needs. Id. And in May 2026, VoltaGrid told EPD that its Covington power plant “may provide

capacity for other similarly qualified large load facilities,” in addition to Serverfarm. 18 Now that

litigation has been filed, VoltaGrid attests that it “is not engaged in negotiations . . . to provide

power services in Georgia” and that VoltaGrid “is not soliciting, marketing to, or otherwise

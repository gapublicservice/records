HAVE YOU ADVOCATED ON UTILITY REGULATION ISSUES BEFORE?

Yes, in 2019, as part of a project called “Solarize Newton-Morgan” I went before the

Covington City Council and Oxford City Council to ask them to reduce or get rid of

distributed generation fees that made using solar power in those utility territories cost

prohibitive. The City of Covington ended up significantly lowering those fees, and the

City of Oxford got rid of them entirely. This provided a formative example of how

advocating for utility policy changes can create opportunities for communities to generate

cost effective sustainable electricity. This experience also highlighted the importance of

public input and appropriate regulation of electric utilities that have territorial monopolies

in our state.

HOW     HAS    UTILITY      REGULATION          AND     DISTRIBUTED         GENERATION

IMPACTED YOUR HOUSEHOLD?

We have two electric vehicles and rooftop solar panels. Our home is in Georgia Power's

territory, so we are Georgia Power customers. We are on the “Overnight Advantage” rate

plan, which Georgia Power offers and which the Public Service Commission approved.

Having that plan means we pay more for electricity during peak daytime hours and less

during off-peak nighttime hours. Ours is a totally electric household, so we strive to

minimize our electricity use during the day and charge our electric vehicles at night,

when electricity is cheaper. The Overnight Advantage plan incentivizes us to reduce our

contribution to peak demand and stress on the grid and as a result makes the whole

system easier to plan, cheaper to power, and more efficient in its function. I am proud of

our ability to contribute to reducing Georgia Power’s peak demand in this way, which I

understand helps Georgia Power reduce its need to rely on fossil fuels during those times.

We also have a rooftop solar system that generates power for our home when the sun is

shining and allows us to buy less electricity from Georgia Power. Whenever we are using

less electricity than our solar panels are producing, we can sell that electricity back onto

the grid. We save money on our household energy bills both by using clean solar energy

and by selling some extra clean solar energy back to the system. In addition, we’ve taken

advantage of Georgia Power’s rebates to install a highly efficient heat pump water heater

as well as upgrade our insulation and HVAC system.

PLEASE      DESCRIBE       IN   MORE      DETAIL      HOW      YOUR      SOLAR      ENERGY

SYSTEM IMPACTS YOUR HOUSEHOLD.

We have a five-kilowatt rooftop solar array, which we installed in 2019. Our five-kilowatt

system has served us well, providing cheap and clean energy for our use, and electricity

bill credits for the excess energy that we send back onto the grid. Over the years, this has

saved us a significant amount of money. For example, in June, July, and August of this

year, we sold a total of 819 kilowatts back to the grid and paid an average electricity bill

of $123 over those months during a very hot summer. More recently, we have considered

expanding our rooftop system or installing another solar system on our 10-acre pasture

across the street. The pasture is particularly sunny and has plenty of space for a small

solar array. However, we would not be able to use all the electricity produced by a larger

rooftop system or connect to a system across the street. We also would not be able to send

or sell excess electricity from a new system to our neighbors. It’s my understanding that

Georgia’s electric-service laws prohibit anyone outside of the utility provider from acting

as a electric supplier to neighboring customers.

HOW DOES VOLTAGRID’S REGULATION UNDER THE TERRITORIAL ACT

AND COGENERATION ACT IMPACT YOU AND YOUR HOUSEHOLD?

As I mentioned, we are in Georgia Power's service territory, so like most people in

Georgia, we can either live without electricity, generate all of our own electricity off the

grid, or buy electricity from Georgia Power. It is my understanding that the Territorial Act

is the law that makes that so, and that in exchange for such a guaranteed customer base,

Georgia Power is regulated and held accountable by the Public Service Commission. If
                            Sara Vinson Direct Testimony
                Georgia Public Service Commission, Docket No. 57243
                                        Page 7

Georgia Power wants to build a power plant, run transmission lines, or raise electricity

prices, that has to be considered and approved by the Public Service Commission. The

Public Service Commission, in turn, is elected by Georgia voters, and has to approve or

deny Georgia Power's decisions only if they further the objectives of providing affordable

and reliable electricity supply and protecting public interests like health, safety, and the

environment, It is my understanding that this arrangement is mandated by law and is

designed to prevent anyone and everyone from building private power plants wherever

they please or criss-crossing every street with multiple sets of power lines. The end result

for our household is that we have to buy electricity from Georgia Power, but we can also

make our vote count and voice heard at the Public Service Commission when Georgia

Power seeks approval for a new power plant or transmission line. VoltaGrid is seeking to

avoid this whole arrangement. Even though they are in Georgia Power's territory, they are

building a power plant and planning to sell electricity, but assert that they cannot be

scrutinized and regulated by the Public Service Commission, voters, or the community.

FACILITY IN NEWTON COUNTY IMPACT YOU?

The VoltaGrid facility in Covington, Georgia currently under construction has attempted

to avoid enforcement of Georgia laws governing our electricity system and air pollution.

VoltaGrid intends to burn dirty gas, spew toxic air pollution, and skirt the rules that

ensure we have an affordable, dependable, and accountable electric grid. VoltaGrid will

also cause noise and light pollution that will disturb our family, our property, our horses,

our farm, and the rural character of Newton County.

HOW WILL AIR POLLUTION FROM VOLTAGRID IMPACT YOU?

VoltaGrid will emit a variety of air pollutants that cause health problems,       including

formaldehyde, fine particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, and volatile organic compounds.

These pollutants can make asthma worse, contribute to lung and heart diseases, and even

cause cancer. VoltaGrid is only about one and a half miles from our home, so I am deeply

concerned that the air pollutants it emits will travel to our property and make us sick. We

also often drive right past VoltaGrid, since it is on our way to town. I am aware that air

pollution can negatively impact brain health and that exposure to particulate matter is

linked to higher rates of dementia. My father passed away recently after suffering from

dementia, so I have a heightened fear I might suffer from such a disease as a result of

increased air pollution from VoltaGrid. The presence of VoltaGrid in our community also

increases my concern for the health and wellbeing of my family.       Our daughter and her

boyfriend and my sister also live in the area, and would be impacted by air pollution from

VoltaGrid. My daughter’s boyfriend has asthma, and I worry that he would suffer more

attacks and breathing trouble due to VoltaGrid’s emissions.      The nature of our work

requires both Randy and me to spend extended time outdoors. I feel a deep sense of

responsibility for making sure my family has clean and safe air to breathe.

WILL VOLTAGRID IMPACT YOUR FARM?

Our horses, our farm, and the wildlife on our property would suffer negative impacts

from VoltaGrid’s air and noise pollution. Our horses are always outside, and they breathe

deeply when they are running or carrying riders. Just like people, they would suffer

negative health consequences from increased air pollution. I grow our farm crops using

Response:

The Company has consistently stated in prior proceedings that the reliability component is intended to reflect marginal capacity costs in the price signal. Question: 

Please refer to Georgia Power response to STF-PIA-2-9 and STF-JKA-2-28 in Docket No. 56765.
Has the Company, in the past decade, had the to remarket firm transportation (“FT”)? If so:
Please explain how and why the FT capacity was re-marketed, including the specific operating conditions under which a re-marketing decision was made, how the re-marketed capacity was priced, and what the results of the re-marketing activity were from 2023-2025. Please provide supporting data in an excel spreadsheet.
How are FT re-marketing revenues recorded for cost allocation purposes (e.g., as an offset to FT fixed costs)? 
Please identify how Georgia Power’s proposed allocation of new FT would work if there is a net cost remaining for the next FCR period, due to any failure to remarket all new FT costs. Would those remaining costs be recovered from FCR customers only, under a Part B under-recovery mechanism?

Response:

Yes, Southern Company Services (“SCS”), enters into contracts for firm transportation capacity on FERC jurisdictional pipelines, as agent for the Southern Company electric operating companies including Georgia Power. In this role, SCS regularly looks for opportunities to optimize the firm transportation capacity secured on behalf of the operating companies, including re-marketing FT. 

For example, SCS’s contract with Transco for the Atlantic Sunrise project permits SCS to segment its capacity to other users while retaining certain segments for system use. In another example, SCS can optimize its FT capacity on the Gulf South pipeline based on the basis differential in index pricing, which allows the Company to use such capacity when commodity gas is available for procurement and delivery at a lower overall cost than procuring and transporting gas using rights held on other pipelines using the system. 

Under both of these structures, SCS used the Request for Proposal (“RFP”) process to receive market bids from parties interested in SCS’s capacity using a long-term asset management agreement (“AMA”) structure. SCS then used the RFP results to evaluate market interest and determine the optimal bid(s). Bid pricing can include fixed demand payments and/or profit-sharing options. SCS also leverages short term capacity release options to optimize capacity during plant maintenance or other outages that can leave capacity unutilized. A short-term capacity release opportunity is posted on a pipeline’s Electronic Bulletin Board (EBB), where interested pipeline shippers can post a bid for short-term capacity release. Optimizing both long-term and short-term capacity has resulted in positive returns for the operating companies while maintaining gas transportation requirements of SCS’s natural gas generation fleet. Please see STF-JKA-5-2 Attachment for the GPC revenues received related to remarketed FT transactions for 2023 through 2025. 
The revenues received from the re-marketing of FT are recorded as an offset to FT costs recorded in FERC accounts 501 and 547.

Similar to the Company’s proposed allocation of new FT, the Company believes using a winter coincident peak (“CP”) and basing the allocation of any net cost remaining from the remarketing efforts on the share of organic versus large load demand growth in determination of the amounts to include in RTP fuel credit would be appropriate. This approach is consistent with the capacity need that drove the incremental generation resources and the allocation in the large load cost model and ensures that Large Load customers are covering their share of any net costs associated with FT.

STF-JKA-5-11

Question: 

Refer to Georgia Power’s response to STF-JKA-2-15(a) in Docket No. 56765 stating that “when the Company dispatches resources based on must-run coal constraints, those resources are typically restricted from setting marginal price in the Aurora model.” When would must-run coal constraints set marginal price in the Aurora model?

Response:

A must-run coal unit can set marginal price in the Aurora model in hours when the unit is dispatched above that must-run floor to help serve system load — meaning system conditions (e.g., high demand, other unit outages, etc.) independently call on that unit’s incremental capacity through normal economic commitment/dispatch logic, not the must-run constraint itself. In those hours, the must-run designation is non-binding given that the unit would have run at that output level regardless, and therefore, Aurora’s default marginal-unit selection rule applies normally, and the unit's incremental cost can set the hourly price like any other economically dispatched resource.

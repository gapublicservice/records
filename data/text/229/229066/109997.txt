 1        While the basic design of those components is sound – allocating generation and

 2        transmission costs over multiple hours based on load and, in the case of transmission,

 3        temperature, since Georgia Power developed this method, there have been advances in

 4        other jurisdictions that could inform an improved RTP design.

 5        In a case involving the design of a real-time pricing rate for Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E),

 6        I represented small business interests in identifying flaws in its proposed rate design.

 7        PG&E accepted my critique, and engaged in a multi-party rate design study that resulted

 8        in an improved proposal, which was accepted by the California Public Utilities

 9        Commission.

10        Keeping it very simple, that design involved allocating capacity costs over many hours of

11        the year, but using an “S-shaped” curve that set capacity prices relatively low when net

12        demand was low, and then escalated sharply as demand began to reach high levels, only to

13        mostly level off at the highest levels.

14        Replicating the same design in Georgia may or may not be a good match for the different

15        circumstances, but some of the key lessons do apply. The RTP capacity price signal was

16        explicitly designed to be closely aligned with grid stress as experienced on the PG&E

17        system. This is very much the definition of a cost-based rate design.

 3   Q:         HOW DOES LARGE LOAD GROWTH AFFECT GEORGIA POWER’S

 4              HEDGING COSTS INCLUDED IN THE FCR RATE?

 5   A:         As noted above, as a result of the settlement in FCR-27, Georgia Power’s hedging is capped

 6              at 20% of total forecasted methane gas fuel burn. As large load growth impacts Georgia

 7              Power’s fuel use forecast, it also drives up the amount of hedging, whether physical or

 8              financial.

 9              For example, without large load customer demand, Georgia Power would hedge 20% of

10              FCR gas fuel burn. If large load customer demand is forecast to increase total gas fuel burn

11              by 50% for a given period, then hedging would increase proportionately. The result would

12              be an effective increase in the FCR gas fuel cost hedging cap from 20% of FCR gas fuel

13              burn to 30%.

14              This is not a “cost” necessarily caused by large load demand and imposed on FCR

15              customers, although it has resulted in a cost increase for non-large load customers in FCR-

16              27. Rather it is the effect of the hedging cap design which applies to all gas fuel

17              consumption rather than just consumption forecast to meet FCR customer demand.

18              While revisiting this issue may be outside the scope of this proceeding, I draw it to the

 3   Q:   YOU HAVE NOT DISCUSSED THE INCOME-QUALIFIED DISCOUNT (IQD).

 4        DO YOU HAVE A POSITION ON HOW THE COSTS OF THIS PROGRAM

 5        SHOULD BE TREATED FOR RTP CUSTOMERS?

 6   A:   It is my understanding that costs of the IQD are primarily collected in base rates, but that

 7        a fuel-related portion of the cost is collected in the FCR. It is not clear whether the

 8        Commission has considered how to fairly recover IQD costs from incremental load served

 9        by RTP customers. A customer taking service on an RTP rate would pay less than the same

10        customer taking service on a non-RTP rate. Certainly it cannot be argued that IQD costs

11        are included in lambda or any concept of capital substitution under the peaker theorem.

12        Resolving this is therefore not a matter of whether these costs should be included in the

13        RTP fuel revenue credit, since these costs are not currently accounted for anywhere in the

14        RTP rate. Given that rates have been set that do not collect IQD costs from incremental

15        RTP load, I do not take a position on this category of costs in this proceeding.

16        However, I suggest that the Commission direct or that Georgia Power voluntarily agree to

17        develop a fair and reasonable method for allocating a portion of IQD costs to RTP and/or

18        large load customers. The most straightforward method would appear to be a further

19        adjustment to the recovery factor, in addition to those suggested earlier in my testimony,

20        with appropriate allocation of those revenues to both the RTP fuel revenue credit and the

21        balance credited to base rate revenues.

MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF TESTIMONY

COMES NOW Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) and moves to strike portions of the Direct Testimony of John D. Wilson on behalf of Georgia Interfaith Power and Light (“GIPL”), and its member congregations Park Avenue Baptist Church, Temple Sinai, and Unitarian Universalist Church of Savannah (the “GIPL Testimony”). The portions of the GIPL Testimony at issue in this Motion to Strike are irrelevant, clearly outside the scope of this proceeding, and would not assist the Georgia Public Service Commission (the “Commission”) in rendering a decision on this matter. Accordingly, the portions of the GIPL Testimony identified in this Motion to Strike should be excluded from the record in this proceeding and not considered by this Commission.
BACKGROUND
On May 28, 2026, the Commission issued its Short Order Adopting Stipulation in Docket No. 56765 requiring that the “Commission shall further review the methodology by which the Company credits Real-Time Pricing (“RTP”) revenue to the FCR revenues and the allocations the Company is making under such methodology in this Docket. This proceeding shall be concluded by December 31, 2026, and the Commission may make modifications if appropriate. The implementation of such modifications, if any, shall be credited to fuel revenues as determined by the Commission, and any corresponding change to base revenues shall be recorded in a regulatory asset or liability and addressed in the next base rate case.” This language was reiterated verbatim in the Procedural and Scheduling Order (“PSO”) issued in Docket No. 57171 on July 7, 2026. 
On August 14, 2026, Georgia Power filed the Direct Testimony of the Panel of Dr. Ross Beppler, Lee Evans, and Brandon Looney. A direct hearing was held on the pre-filed testimony on September 1, 2026. The GIPL Testimony that is the subject of this Motion to Strike was filed on September 23, 2026. Specifically, the Company moves to strike the following testimony filed by Witness Wilson: 
Page 2, Line 16 through the word “Second,” on Page 3, Line 14. 
This testimony offers recommendations regarding the reliability and transmission cost components of the RTP rate.
Page 4, Line 18 through Page 5, Line 2.
This testimony recommends changes to the RTP rate design.
Page 12, Line 13 through Page 17, Line 14. 
This testimony addresses Georgia Power’s method for calculating the value of capacity for the reliability component of the RTP rate, RTP pricing, and includes commentary on RTP rate design. 
Page 17, Line 17 beginning at “Nonetheless,” through Page 19, Line 17.
This testimony makes recommendations related to the RTP rate and rate design unrelated to the fuel credit methodology.
Page 27, Lines 9-16.
This testimony proposes recommendations for the next base rate case unrelated to Georgia Power’s fuel credit methodology.
Page 30, Line 14, beginning at “However,” through Page 31, Line 11.
This testimony addresses the recovery factor, which is an RTP rate component and not related to the fuel credit methodology, and recommended changes to the same in the next rate case.
Page 33, Line 3 through Page 34, Line 2.
This Q&A discusses hedging costs, which is not within the scope of this proceeding, and was fully litigated in FCR-27 in Docket No. 56765.

Georgia Power’s Motion to Stike is timely and filed in accordance with Section 3(g) of the PSO.

ARGUMENT
I.The Portions of the GIPL Testimony Identified in this Motion to Strike are Outside the Scope of this Hearing and Irrelevant to the Decision Before the Commission.

It is clear from both the PSO in this docket and Final Order in Docket No. 56765 that the scope of the current investigation is limited to the Company’s RTP Fuel Credit Methodology by which the Company assigns revenue received from RTP customers to either fuel or base rates. This proceeding is limited to the accounting treatment of where revenues should be booked and not an investigation into the structure of the RTP rate itself. As a result, testimony regarding proposed changes to the structure of the RTP rate and how the price is set, including the calculation of the five RTP components, is beyond the scope of this limited proceeding and should be stricken from the record of this case. 
The scope of this proceeding was established in the Commission’s August 4, 2026 Order Adopting Stipulation resolving all issues in the FCR-27 proceeding. Rather than address the issues the Commission clearly identified for this proceeding in the PSO, the GIPL Testimony introduces new issues and proposes changes to the RTP rate itself. The GIPL Testimony also revisits issues appropriately raised, considered, and decided in the FCR-27 case in Docket No. 56765. In that regard, much of the GIPL Testimony is beyond the limited scope of this proceeding. 
Witness Wilson himself identifies that roughly the first half of his testimony, including recommending changes to the transmission and reliability components of the RTP rate, is outside the scope of this proceeding. (See GIPL Testimony at 17.) The GIPL Testimony includes a dedicated question and answer that explicitly states that changes to RTP rate are not within the scope of this proceeding. (GIPL Testimony at 30.) Such acknowledgement by GIPL only serves to emphasize GIPL’s attempts to leverage the current proceeding to propose changes to the RTP rate that are appropriately addressed in a rate case. The Commission should take Witness Wilson at his word and strike the GIPL Testimony readily acknowledged to be outside the scope of this proceeding.
As another example, Witness Wilson’s testimony regarding load growth and fuel hedging is wholly unrelated to whether RTP revenue is assigned to fuel or base rates. Indeed, the GIPL Testimony acknowledges that Witness Wilson’s testimony is “revisiting this issue” and “may be outside the scope of this proceeding.” (GIPL Testimony at 33.) There is no “may be” about it. The PSO was clear regarding the issues before the Commission and this portion of the GIPL Testimony is outside the scope of this proceeding and should be stricken.
Finally, not only are Witness Wilson’s first set of recommendations topically unrelated to the scope of this proceeding, but they also seek Commission action that exceeds the authority of what the stipulating parties agreed to in resolution of the FCR-27 case. The only Commission action contemplated when the stipulating parties agreed to establish this proceeding was the potential modification of the “methodology by which the Company credits Real-Time Pricing (“RTP”) revenue to the FCR revenues and the allocations the Company is making under such methodology.” (PSO at 1.) To expand the scope of Commission action at this point would undermine the settlement of FCR-27. Further, it would be inappropriate for the Commission to review how the Company calculates, modifies, and sets the RTP rate in the absence of the comprehensive review of the Company’s total revenue requirements conducted in a formal base rate case. Even the requested Commission action sought by Witness Wilson’s first set of recommendations is beyond the scope of the proceeding. As with the substantive arguments identified above, the recommendations offered also exceed the limited scope of this proceeding.
It is reasonable and appropriate for the Commission to strike the portions of the GIPL Testimony identified above. Mr. Wilson, by his own admission, submitted testimony that is beyond the scope of the issues identified for this proceeding and wholly unrelated to how RTP revenue should be recorded as between fuel and base rates. The Commission should strike such testimony to ensure consistency with its PSO and to maintain efficiency of this proceeding.  

CONCLUSION
The portions of the GIPL Testimony identified in this Motion to Strike are irrelevant, outside the scope of this proceeding as established by the PSO, and are not properly raised before this Commission in this Docket. For all the foregoing reasons, Georgia Power respectfully requests that the Commission strike the portions of the GIPL Testimony identified in this Motion to Strike.

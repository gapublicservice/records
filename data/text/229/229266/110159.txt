INTRODUCTION
VoltaGrid’s Motion raises two fundamental questions in arguing that Petitioners’ claims should be dismissed, both of which are nearly entirely legal, not factual. The subsequent responses present no viable argument on these questions for why VoltaGrid’s Motion should not be granted or for why further factual development is necessary.
The first question is whether there is any complaint before the Commission sufficient to trigger its enforcement authority. The answer is clearly no. The Territorial Act permits the Commission to enforce the Act only upon its own complaint, or the complaint of an electric supplier or “any other interested party.” Petitioners are none of these. They have demonstrated no interest in any right the Territorial Act confers. Their concerns are purely environmental, and they have an alternative forum for raising such concerns: the Georgia Environmental Protection Division. Beyond Petitioners, neither the Commission nor any electric supplier has filed a “complaint” in this docket.
The second question is whether, even if there were a valid complaint before it, the Commission has jurisdiction over such a complaint. It does not. The Commission’s authority over electric companies extends to those operating “public ... plants furnishing service to the public.” These words carry the ordinary meaning they had when the General Assembly enacted them in 1907. Georgia Power contends they should be given a “flexible” meaning that evolves with technology and “regulatory circumstances.” Georgia EMC contends the Territorial Act dispensed with the public-service requirement altogether. Petitioners contend Commission jurisdiction attaches on whether a company is “conducting itself as a public utility”—a non-statutory phrase that involves a multi-factor inquiry into its marketing, its podcasts, and the speculative possibility that it might grow in Georgia at some undetermined future point rather than whether a company currently furnishes service to the public. A thorough analysis of the relevant statutes demonstrates that VoltaGrid presents the only viable reading.
These questions should be decided now. If the Hearing Officer concludes that any factual determinations are necessary, efficiency demands the legal questions be resolved first, so that any hearing is confined to facts that actually matter under the Hearing Officer’s reading of the law.
PETITIONERS ARE NOT INTERESTED PARTIES.
The Commission’s enforcement authority under the Territorial Act is conditional. The Commission may issue orders to enforce the Territorial Act only “upon its own complaint or the complaint of any other electric supplier or any other interested party.” A “complaint” from one of those three sources is the predicate for any subsequent hearing and order. Georgia Power has not filed a complaint. Nor has the Commission filed its own complaint. Therefore, the only purported “complaint” in this docket is Petitioners’, so the threshold question is whether Petitioners are “interested parties.” If they are not, there is no complaint to invoke the Commission’s enforcement authority under O.C.G.A. §§ 46-3-13 or 46-3-8.
The phrase “interested party,” as used in any statute, appears to have been construed a single time by the Georgia Supreme Court. The Court concluded that “interested parties must, of course, mean parties having a legal, protectible interest,” i.e., a party whose “rights are in direct issue or jeopardy.” Federally, the term “interested parties,” as used in the Administrative Procedure Act, was construed the same way: “‘interest’ means a legally recognized private interest,” and a person relying on a statutory interest qualifies “only if the statute can fairly be construed as vesting him with a new private right.”
While those cases involved different statutes, they suggest the term “interested party” had an understood meaning by 1973 when the General Assembly chose the language for the Territorial Act, and Petitioners do not fit within that meaning, having identified no right conferred upon them by the Territorial Act, much less that any such right is in jeopardy. To support their standing, Petitioners cite a single reference to the environment in the Act’s policy statement; but even if a policy statement could vest a protectible interest, the Act’s environmental concerns are limited to “the extension and location of electric supplier lines in the manner most compatible with the preservation and enhancement of the state’s physical environment.” None of Petitioners’ concerns here is related to the extension and location of electric lines. 
Petitioners also assert they are interested parties because, without this Petition, there is no “public regulatory forum where Sustainable Newton can raise concerns.” This is demonstrably false, given that Petitioners raised the same concerns in another public regulatory agency: the Georgia Environmental Protection Division (“EPD”), which issued the air quality permit for VoltaGrid’s project. Not only is public comment provided for in the EPD permitting process, but Petitioners also have the right to appeal any issued permit before an administrative law judge, with judicial review to follow. 
What is more, assertions in their sworn testimony notwithstanding, Petitioners participated directly in that EPD process, submitting extensive comments to EPD and securing substantive revisions to the final air permit issued to VoltaGrid. Indeed, Petitioners have publicly touted the revisions to the final permit they obtained as a major victory ― claiming in press releases that it “incorporates a significant change requested by the Southern Environmental Law Center (SELC) [and] Sustainable Newton,” and that through their “participation [in the public] process for this permit, we have achieved meaningful reductions in the harmful pollutants Covington residents will be exposed to.” 
Ultimately, Petitioners’ reading would make the interested party requirement meaningless. If the General Assembly intended for every member of the public to be an interested party, it would have written “any person” into the statute. The Georgia Court Reporting Act, for example, was passed a year after the Territorial Act and triggered administrative action upon the “complaint of any person.” For the Territorial Act, the legislature limited complaints to an “interested party.” Here, Petitioners have no rights conferred by the Territorial Act, and their Petition must be dismissed.
THE COMMISSION LACKS JURISDICTION OVER VOLTAGRID BECAUSE VOLTAGRID DOES NOT FURNISH SERVICE TO THE PUBLIC.
In 1907, the General Assembly chose to give the Commission jurisdiction over those electric companies that operated “public … electric light and power plants furnishing service to the public.” That choice of words was deliberate, and it makes the General Assembly’s intent plain: private on-site plants, which were well-known at the time, were not intended to be subject to Commission jurisdiction.
That jurisdictional limit has not been disturbed. In March 1999, Director B.B. Knowles, who directed the Public Service Commission’s Utilities Division for nearly two decades, wrote: “The jurisdiction of the Commission is restricted to those electric ... companies that serve the public.” The question came before the Commission itself later that year. Eight parties intervened, among them Georgia Power, Savannah Electric, Flint EMC, and MEAG, and none disputed the settled premise that the Commission’s jurisdiction is limited to electric companies that serve the public. Georgia Power quoted Director Knowles’s letter, expressly agreed with it, and even underlined the statutory term “furnishing service to the public,” citing the 1907 statute. The Commission correspondingly ruled in 2000 that an entity “solely and exclusively” serving a single on-site customer under a private contract may be presumed not to be “furnishing service to the public” and thus not subject to Commission jurisdiction. 
The dispute here is about what the statutory phrase “public ... plant furnishing service to the public” means. Giving the phrase its original meaning, as Georgia law requires, there can be little doubt that VoltaGrid does not operate a “public ... plant furnishing service to the public,” and it therefore falls outside the jurisdiction of the Commission.
The phrase “public … plant furnishing service to the public,” as enacted in 1907, excludes companies that serve limited users by individual bargain.
Here, the question before the Commission is what the term “public … plant furnishing service to the public” meant in 1907, when the General Assembly wrote the words. On that question the law is clear. In the years surrounding enactment, a service was considered public when it was rendered to “all of the public fairly and without discrimination,” available “by common right or usage,” offered “to the general public on equal terms,” and open so that “everybody who has occasion to use it may lawfully and of right do so.” A service was private when it was rendered by “special invitation,” only to “certain persons or concerns, under contracts with them,” or by one who “in effect makes an individual bargain in each case.” 
Under that standard, this is not a borderline case. VoltaGrid converts one user’s gas into the same user’s electricity, for consumption solely by that user, on that user’s property, from which “the indiscriminate public is generally excluded.” VoltaGrid does not operate a “public … plant furnishing service to the public,” as the General Assembly understood those words in 1907. 
Legal dictionaries defined “public” as open to the community and not just particular individuals.
Georgia courts interpret statutory text according to “the ordinary public meaning … by considering the meaning the text had at the time it was enacted.” The phrase at issue here was enacted in 1907, and it has not been substantively amended since. 
The legal and general dictionaries of the period defined “public” as something commonly accessible to a community or a portion of it, rather than limited to particular or “few” individuals. Georgia’s Court of Appeals approvingly cited one of these digests, Words and Phrases, when it construed the meaning of “public” in a 1907 statute. In Tooke v. State, decided in July 1908, the Georgia Court of Appeals construed the term “public place” as referring “to the congregating of a number of persons by common right or usage, or by a general express or implied invitation, as furnishing the element of publicity.” On the other hand, the Court concluded a place is not public if the “indiscriminate public is generally excluded,” it is “not open to the access of the public,” or people congregate as the result of a “special invitation.” 
Georgia courts drew the same public-private distinction for public services and the Commission’s own statutes.
While Tooke interpreted “public” in reference to “a public place,” Georgia courts at the time applied the same meaning to statutes involving public services, electric power, and the Commission’s own jurisdictional statutes.
For example, the 1897 Electric Act granted the power of eminent domain to plants “supplying light, heat, or power to the public,” which the Georgia Supreme Court construed as reaching companies that “serve all of the public … without discrimination.” Similarly, in a 1914 condemnation dispute, the Court held that the “public” nature of a service turns on whether “everybody who has occasion to use it may lawfully and of right do so,” and that “the purpose of serving an individual enterprise only is not a public purpose.”
This public-private distinction was interpreted the same way with regard to the Commission’s jurisdictional statutes. In McIntyre v. Harrison, the Court analyzed whether a transportation provider was a “public carrier” subject to Commission jurisdiction, or whether it was “[a] private carrier [that] renders no public service, but private service.” It construed a private carrier to be one who hauled only “for certain persons or concerns, under contracts with them,”  and that “does not deal with the public indiscriminately … but in effect makes an individual bargain in each case.” A public (or common) carrier, on the other hand, “holds himself out as ready to receive ... for all people indifferently.” The opinion was written by Justice Hines—the same James K. Hines who, as the Commission’s Special Attorney in 1909, concluded the Commission “has no jurisdiction over an electric light and power company” that sells to a single party because such company “does not furnish power or light to the public.”
In summary, Georgia decisions on either side of 1907 used “public” consistently as applied to public service and specifically electricity service. Service to the public meant service accessible to the community by right, indiscriminately and on equal terms, to whoever sought it. It did not include service to particular persons under special arrangements and individual bargains.
The Commission has carried forward the historical definition of “public” to present-day regulation.
The Commission’s recent application of the word “public” tracks this historical definition. In 2018, for example, the Commission denied Great Walton Railroad’s petition to condemn land for a runaround track, finding the track “will not serve the public, but only a single enterprise.” Key to the Commission’s reasoning was that “the public generally … does not have a right to use the track.” The Court of Appeals affirmed. That understanding of what constitutes public is strikingly similar to the one Tooke adopted in 1908: a place is public where people may gather “as a matter of common right.”
The Territorial Act neither displaced the public-service requirement nor changed what those words mean.
Intervenors try to sidestep the historical authorities described above by asserting the Territorial Act somehow redefined or displaced the phrase “service to the public.” But the Territorial Act did not silently remove or disturb the well-established limits on the Commission’s jurisdiction; it expressly embraced those limits.
Georgia EMC’s reading would improperly expand the Commission’s jurisdiction.
Georgia EMC argues the Territorial Act did away with the public-service limitation altogether, at least for purposes of the Territorial Act. Petitioners themselves recognize that such an interpretation has “limited textual justification,” and “would be a tremendous expansion of Commission jurisdiction over the electric marketplace.”
To say Georgia EMC’s interpretation has “limited” textual support in the Territorial Act is generous. The text plainly contradicts Georgia EMC’s reading. Every prohibition in the Territorial Act runs to “electric suppliers.” In addition, the Act’s statement of policy itself says it binds “electric suppliers” and mentions no one else. Finally, the Act specially defines the term “electric supplier” as “any electric light and power company otherwise subject to regulation by the commission.” So, for a company to be covered by the Territorial Act, it must be “otherwise subject to regulation by the commission.” The statute that makes an electric light and power company “otherwise subject to regulation by the Commission” is O.C.G.A. § 46-2-21(b)(5), which only reaches companies “furnishing service to the public.” When the General Assembly intends the Commission’s authority to reach any person, it says so. By contrast, the Territorial Act is intentionally confined to companies “otherwise subject to regulation by the Commission.”
The Territorial Act did expand the Commission’s jurisdiction in one way. It expressly brought electric membership corporations and municipalities, which previously were outside the Commission’s authority, under its jurisdiction. It did so by an affirmative, express statement. Again, this provision shows the General Assembly understood the previous limits on the Commission’s jurisdiction, and where it wished to modify those limits, it did so expressly. In all other respects, the General Assembly’s intent was plainly to carry over pre-existing jurisdictional limits, undisturbed, into the Territorial Act.
Georgia EMC also makes a policy argument built around an imagined out-of-state supplier that runs lines to “cherry pick[ed]” customers and somehow evades regulation by not serving the public. But this could not happen. Under Georgia law, only electric companies that “serve the public” may get permission to run lines through public streets—tracking the standard used to assess Commission jurisdiction under O.C.G.A. § 46-2-21(b)(5). An electric company that runs lines through public streets is, necessarily, also subject to Commission jurisdiction, and therefore the Territorial Act. 
Finally, Georgia EMC asserts that the “express terms” of the Cogeneration Act grant the Commission jurisdiction over VoltaGrid. The provision it relies on, O.C.G.A. § 46-3-53(a), however, contains no affirmative grant of jurisdiction, much less an express one. It contains only a declaratory recognition that certain operators may operate “without being subject to the jurisdiction or regulation of the commission.” A clarifying recognition that certain facilities are outside the Commission’s jurisdiction is not an affirmative grant of jurisdiction over every other facility.
Georgia Power’s argument for a “flexible” definition creates a reading of “public” that is overly broad and inconsistent with Georgia statutory interpretation.
Unlike Georgia EMC, Georgia Power does not dispute that the Commission’s jurisdiction is statutorily limited to companies that furnish service to the public. But its reading of “public” is so broad as to render the word meaningless. It argues for a “flexible” definition that “must adapt” to evolving technology and “regulatory circumstances,” and that allows for private services to be deemed public “when the regulatory circumstances warrant.” Finding no Georgia authorities to support its position, Georgia Power simply declares all Georgia law on the meaning of “public” obsolete. Atlanta Gas Light, it says, construed “an electric service regime that was about to be fundamentally overhauled” and is “of limited value”; the Attorney General’s opinions allegedly “suffer the same timing flaw.”
However, the timing of the Territorial Act works against Georgia Power. The General Assembly enacted the Act in 1973, one year after the Attorney General concluded that a company furnishing electricity to three industrial neighbors was “not within the jurisdiction of the Public Service Commission.” Legislating against that backdrop, the General Assembly chose not to expand the jurisdiction of the Commission over electric companies. To the contrary, it wrote the existing jurisdictional limits into the Act’s definition of “electric supplier,” which it chose to define as an electric company “otherwise subject to regulation by the Commission.” This shows an intent to embrace prior limitations on Commission jurisdiction, not to “fundamentally overhaul” them.
Lacking any authority from Georgia to support its position, Georgia Power relies almost entirely on a divided North Carolina state appellate decision, apparently never cited outside the state, in advocating for a “flexible” definition of “public” that reaches single-user plants. Whatever North Carolina’s approach, in Georgia the construction of a statute does not adapt with time and technology. As discussed above, words in a statute keep the same meaning they had when enacted. This is especially true for statutes that limit the provision of electric service, which the Georgia Supreme Court has recognized must be “strictly construed” and “will not be construed to apply to any situation which was not clearly within the contemplation of the legislature at the time such law was enacted.”  
To the extent the Commission looks beyond Georgia, the Alabama Supreme Court has already decided this case: construing materially identical statutory language (“furnishing to or for the public”), unanimously rejecting similar policy arguments Georgia Power makes here; and holding that a company selling energy under private contracts to two large industrial customers—while soliciting six more—was not a public utility, because it had not “held itself out as ready, able and willing to serve the public” such that the public (or any portion) could receive the service “of right” rather than “by permission only.” 
All Georgia Power has left are policy arguments, which fare no better. Georgia Power says a single-customer rule would “throw the Territorial Act into a tailspin.” It would not. The Territorial Act was intended to “restrict competition among public utilities,” and the Georgia Supreme Court upheld it on that understanding. An on-site generator privately serving the owner of the site does nothing to cause competition among public utilities; it does not, for example, put one utility’s lines in another’s territory. Georgia Power remains the exclusive public utility authorized to serve Serverfarm. Georgia Power’s imagined flood of private, on-site “pop-up” plants, however speculative, is simply not what the Territorial Act was written to prevent.
The Territorial Act does not expand the Commission’s jurisdiction to companies beyond those “furnishing service to the public.” The public-service limitation was incorporated into the Act and must be applied here consistent with Georgia authorities stretching back more than a century.
Even if “conducting itself as a public utility” were a factor, as Petitioners assert, VoltaGrid has done none of the things the phrase describes.
Petitioners put great weight on “conducting itself as a public utility” as a factor in the Commission’s jurisdiction. The phrase “conducting itself as a public utility” appears nowhere in the statute. It comes instead from a 1969 Attorney General opinion, which said that whether a business is subject to regulation depends on factors including “whether, perhaps, in other ways it has conducted itself as a public utility.” From that tentative clause, Petitioners imagine a wide-reaching, non-statutory test that looks at websites, podcasts, and national growth trajectory.
Even if this were a jurisdictional standard, which it is not, Petitioners enlarge it well beyond its meaning. The sole case the Attorney General cited for this factor, Clark v. Olson, held that a water system providing service to nineteen other houses was not a public utility because “there was no dedication of his water system to a public use so as to entitle the public generally to demand water service as a matter of legal right.” The examples Clark quoted of conduct from which such a dedication had been implied were all formal acts binding the business to public obligations: (1) applying to public officials “for a public fixation of . . . rates”; (2) contracting to supply service “at such rates as may be fixed by law”; (3) exercising “the right of eminent domain”; (4) organizing “under acts of the legislature providing for the organization of corporations for supplying water for public use”; and (5) having “voluntarily submitted itself to the jurisdiction of the Railroad Commission and to orders made by it fixing the rates.” VoltaGrid has done nothing of the kind, and Petitioners’ complaint does not allege otherwise.
The other authority Petitioners rely on for the “conducting itself as a public utility” factor is the Commission’s 2000 Air Force/Enron order, which used the phrase in passing, in announcing a presumption against jurisdiction over a single-customer provider. Notable is that the prospective provider in that case was Enron, which in 1999 was an order of magnitude larger than VoltaGrid is today, and which told the Commission it was “the largest marketer of gas and electricity” in the country. If size and marketing mattered at all, certainly the Commission would have raised the issue with Enron. The Commission never suggested any of that bore on whether Enron would be subject to Commission jurisdiction, however; the only factual question it identified as relevant was who would receive the service. 

An efficient hearing requires resolving the outstanding legal questions first.
The jurisdictional dispute among the parties presents legal issues and may be resolved without any further factual development. However, if the Hearing Officer determines that a factual hearing on the jurisdiction issue is required, efficiency calls for resolving the legal standard before a hearing: deciding what the statutory phrase “public … plants furnishing service to the public” means, and identifying which facts, if any, bear on that meaning. If the hearing is conducted under a cloud of unresolved questions of law, much of the evidence presented—from emails, to websites, to podcasts—may well prove irrelevant once the legal questions are answered. Indeed, if the Commission agrees with Georgia Power’s “public service by scale” interpretation—that any plant with a nameplate capacity above an unstated threshold de facto constitutes “furnishing service to the public,” regardless of whether the plant actually furnishes service to the public—then no other facts need be developed. Either way, it would be inefficient to hold a hearing before the legal standard is decided.
When the Commission last confronted the question of whether an electric company was “furnishing service to the public” and therefore subject to Commission jurisdiction, it did exactly what VoltaGrid proposes here. The Commission, at Staff’s recommendation and without holding an evidentiary hearing, issued an order that resolved the legal disputes and identified the narrow factual question that would be relevant for a hearing. VoltaGrid respectfully requests that the Hearing Officer follow the same course here.

CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, and those stated in its Motion and opening Brief, VoltaGrid respectfully requests that the Hearing Officer grant its Motion to Dismiss. Alternatively, VoltaGrid respectfully requests that the Hearing Officer resolve the outstanding purely legal question of statutory construction before a hearing, and identifies which facts, if any, bear on that meaning.

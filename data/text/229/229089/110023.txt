the state as a whole,” and that “[t]he difference between the cancer rate in 30014 and the state is
statistically significant, meaning that the increase is not likely due to chance alone." 3 Further,
EPA has also singled out Covington as a place “where risks of cancer were potentially

II.       EPD Must Require More Stringent Control Efficiency and Monitoring
          Requirements for Formaldehyde Emissions
As discussed above, the preliminary air toxics screening modeling for formaldehyde showed
annual concentrations at exactly the AAC. Thus any increase in emissions beyond those
permitted and modeled would almost certainly cause exceedances of the AAC. As set out in our
comments on VoltaGrid’s application and below, VoltaGrid’s emissions estimates for
formaldehyde are dubious and must be accompanied by more stringent control and monitoring
requirements.
      A. VoltaGrid’s Formaldehyde Estimates Rely on Overly Optimistic Control
         Efficiencies
VoltaGrid estimates the 33 permanent engines will emit 2.6 tons of formaldehyde per year. This
estimate appears to be overly reliant on assumed control efficiencies. In particular, to estimate
formaldehyde emissions, VoltaGrid cites to a manufacturer emission rate and then assumes that
the relevant controls, oxidation catalyst, will reduce emissions by 95%. 13 To support these
assumptions, VoltaGrid further cites to testing on comparable engines in Texas. 14 That testing
showed that the oxidation catalyst was achieving an average emission reduction of 98.2%—
considerably higher than the 95% assumed in this permit application. 15
Critically, the Texas testing also included pre-control emission rates (i.e., they tested the “inlet”
of the control devices); 16 utilizing this emission rate and the 95% control efficiency claimed by
VoltaGrid for the Georgia engines results in an emission rate for the 33 engines of about 5.3 tons
per year, more than twice the rate (2.6 tons) calculated by VoltaGrid. 17

conditions adequate to ensure continuous compliance set out below.
        B. EPD Must Require More Stringent Monitoring Conditions for Formaldehyde
Despite the foregoing—the screening results showing formaldehyde at levels equal to the
maximum allowable ambient concentration and the heavy reliance on very efficient control
technology—the permit only contains one provision related to monitoring for formaldehyde
emissions: EPD has required initial (i.e., one-time-only) stack testing. 20

Although the permit does contain certain parametric monitoring provisions related to the
selective catalytic reduction (“SCR”) controls, each of these monitoring provisions arises from
ensuring the SCR achieves adequate NOx emissions reductions; none of these are related to
ensuring the oxidation catalyst achieves adequate control of organics like formaldehyde or the
surrogate carbon monoxide. 21 In short, nothing in the permit ensures that the controls are
operated in a manner to continuously achieve the rigorous control efficiencies needed to
maintain compliance with the formaldehyde emission limit (and, as set out above, this means the
oxidation catalyst must achieve around 97 to 98% destruction efficiency to keep emissions
within limits). The permit must be amended to included adequate parametric monitoring to
ensure the catalyst and the overall control technology remain effective continuously.

The best way to address these issues would be to require Continuous Emissions Monitoring
Systems (“CEMS”). We note that the relevant NESHAP standards include CEMS as one
monitoring requirement for RICE engines, in particular CEMS for CO, CO2, and O2, which the

III.    EPD Must Permit VoltaGrid and the Adjacent Serverfarm Operation as a Single
        Source.
VoltaGrid intends to sell power exclusively to the adjacent Serverfarm datacenter, and
Serverfarm will be solely reliant on VoltaGrid’s power; the two entities will likewise share the
same piece of undivided property, owned by Serverfarm. Despite the foregoing, EPD is
proposing to permit the two entities as separate sources, allowing both sources to escape more
rigorous Title V permitting requirements, amongst other issues. This appears to be improper—
especially because this arrangement would violate the Georgia Territorial Act, as discussed
below.
        A. Under EPA’s Source Aggregation Guidance, VoltaGrid and Server Farm Are a
           Single Source.
EPA’s source aggregation test—used to determine when two entities should be considered one
source for permitting—boils down to three elements, namely do the entities: (1) belong to the
same industrial grouping: (2) are located on one or more contiguous or adjacent properties: and
(3) are under the control of the same person (or persons under common control).
Under the first prong, EPD dismisses these two facilities as being one source because they do not
share the same industrial SIC code. Yet even VoltaGrid’s application acknowledges that there
are relevant exceptions to this prong, in particular when one facility serves as a “support facility”
for the primary establishment. EPA has explained that a facility may qualify as a support facility,

EPD further proposes to consider these two entities as separate sources under EPA’s 2018

factors indicating that this datacenter, powered solely by VoltaGrid’s engines, should not be

As an initial matter, VoltaGrid LLC is not currently registered to do business in Georgia, and it’s
not clear that VoltaGrid is complying with Georgia’s business laws by applying for an air permit
here without registering to do business in the state. But more critically for EPD’s analysis, it is
impossible to ascertain whether Serverfarm and VoltaGrid will actually operate the two facilities
in accordance with the descriptions provided in the application if VoltaGrid is not even a legal
entity in Georgia—and, as set out below, this arrangement would not be lawful under Georgia’s
electric utility laws.
Moreover, the Meadowbrook Letter was a non-binding, non-regulatory letter specific to single
set of facts regarding a landfill-to-gas operation in Pennsylvania. The Memo moreover explains
that it “is not a rule or regulation, and the statements herein are not binding on state or local
permitting authorities.” EPA further stressed that “consistent with EPA’s longstanding practice
and view, determinations of common control are fact-specific and should continue to be made by
permitting authorities on a case-by-case basis.” 28

Further, The Meadowbrook Letter states that it would “not be appropriate to rely on EPA’s
current approach [in the Meadowbrook Letter] to artificially separate a source into multiple
sources in order to evade major source status or otherwise circumvent title V or NSR
requirements.” 29 Finally, the Meadowbrook Letter reiterates the ultimate guiding principle for
this analysis, which is that for permitting purposes, a single “source” should comport with the
“common sense notion of a plant.” 30

Under these factors, EPD should view the VoltaGrid engines and the Serverfarm as one source
meeting the “common sense notion of a plant.” For instance, many other datacenters are being
constructed or proposed using full-time, onsite power without demarcating the two operations as
different sources; moreover, VoltaGrid’s sole purpose for seeking a permit is to power the
adjoining datacenter, and the datacenter could not operate without the power from VoltaGrid;
this in contrast to the scenario under the Meadowbrook Letter, wherein biogas processing facility
would process waste-gas from an existing and unrelated landfill.

Finally, even under the Meadowbrook Letter, where the analysis hinges primarily on whether
one entity could influence the environmental compliance of the other entity, that prong appears
to satisfied here. Because Serverfarm will rely on VoltaGrid exclusively for primary power—but
will also utilize hour-limited emergency backup engines—downtime or outages at VoltaGrid
could force Serverfarm to utilize its backup engines in ways that lead to permit violations.
Moreover, Serverfarm is taking synthetic minor limits, and additional run-time on its engines,
even if not a violation of federal hour limits, could cause exceedances of the synthetic minor
limits.

For the foregoing reasons, EPD should consider VoltaGrid and Serverfarm as a single source
either under the “common sense notion of a plant” standard or even under the non-binding
Meadowbrook guidance. We note that, as a practical matter, permitting the two sources as a
single source would ultimately necessitate the facility to obtain Title V federal operating permit,
which would implement more protective monitoring, recordkeeping, and reporting requirements.

       B. VoltaGrid and Serverfarm’s Proposed Operations Violate Georgia’s Territorial
          Act.

Under Georgia laws, in particular the Georgia Territorial Electric Service Act (“Territorial Act”)
and the Georgia Cogeneration and Small Power Production Act (“CoGen Act”), one entity may
sell electricity to another entity only in limited, highly-regulated circumstances—circumstances
not met here. Although we understand that EPD does not regulate electricity sales, the fact that
VoltaGrid and Serverfarm are attempting to be permitted as separate entities is highly relevant:
either these operations are not actually two separate legal entities (or may not intend to be
separate entities once electricity is flowing), or the arrangement violates other Georgia law. As
such, even if EPD disagrees with the analysis above showing that EPD must permit them as a
single source, EPD must further scrutinize the VoltaGrid/Serverfarm relationship to ascertain
whether they really are two distinct entities operating at arms-length and intend to remain as such
in the future.

In Georgia, an individual may not furnish retail electric service to a third party without
implicating two main statutes – the Georgia Territorial Electric Service Act (“Territorial Act”)
and the Georgia Cogeneration and Small Power Production Act (“CoGen Act”). In particular,
Under Section 46-3-3(1) of the Territorial Act, an electric supplier has “the exclusive right to
extend and continue furnishing service to [any] new premises” within its assigned area. Section
(b)(1) also states that each assigned area is “an enclosed geographic area assigned to only one

electric supplier by the [Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”).]” 31 Moreover, an
“electric supplier” is “any electric light and power company subject to regulation by the
[C]ommission, any electric membership corporation furnishing retail service in this state, and
any municipality which furnishes such service within this state.” 32
VoltaGrid does not qualify as an electric supplier because it is not an electric light and power
company subject to regulation by the Commission, an electric membership corporation, or a
municipality. On the other hand, Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power”) qualifies as an
electric supplier because it is an electric light and power company subject to regulation by the
Commission. VoltaGrid aims to sell electricity to a retail customer, Serverfarm, in Georgia
Power’s assigned area. As such, VoltaGrid’s sales of electricity to Severfarm would violate the
Territorial Act by infringing upon Georgia Power’s assigned area through selling electricity to a
retail customer, Serverfarm.
Further, under § 46-3-53(a) of the CoGen Act, a person may operate a cogeneration facility
without being subject to the Commission’s jurisdiction or regulation if that person uses all of the
electric energy produced at the facility. However, while subsection (b) provides for a limited
exception that allows excess electric energy to be sold to an electric supplier without being
subject to the Commission’s regulation or jurisdiction, 33 this exception found in (b) only allows
VoltaGrid to sell excess electricity to an electric supplier, such as Georgia Power, not an adjacent
datacenter.
In sum, if VoltaGrid’s representations in its application are correct, it appears that the sale of
electricity to Serverfarm violates the Territorial Act by infringing upon Georgia Power’s
assigned area through selling electricity to a retail customer, Serverfarm, and does not meet the
limited exception found in the CoGen Act.

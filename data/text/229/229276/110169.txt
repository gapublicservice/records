RESPONSE AND OBJECTION OF RESPONDENT, VOLTAGRID, LLC TO FIRST AMENDMENT TO NOTICE OF HEARING AND SCHEDULING ORDER
Comes now, VOLTAGRID, LLC (“VoltaGrid”), Respondent in the above-styled docket, and, without waiving its pending Motion to Dismiss or the objections stated in its Brief in Opposition to Petitioners’ trade secret motion (the “Opposition”), files this Response and Objection to the First Amendment to Notice of Hearing and Scheduling Order issued October 22, 2026 (the “Amended Order”), showing the Commission as follows.
INTRODUCTION
The Amended Order sets Petitioners’ challenge to VoltaGrid’s trade secret designations and request for access to that information (the “Trade Secret Motion”) for hearing on October 29, 2026. It asks VoltaGrid to litigate the release of its trade secrets to Petitioners before anyone decides whether Petitioners are proper parties to this docket, or whether the Commission has jurisdiction over VoltaGrid at all. That sequence must be reversed. Otherwise, VoltaGrid is at risk that its trade secrets will be disclosed to parties not entitled to receive them by a body that is without jurisdiction to order their release. 
Further, the Commission’s Trade Secret Rule expressly reserves the power to decide all issues related to the potential taking and disclosure of trade secrets to the Commission itself.  Under the Trade Secret Rule, that power belongs exclusively to the Commission and cannot be delegated to a Hearing Officer, leaving  nothing concerning trade secrets for a Hearing Officer to resolve. VoltaGrid therefore objects to any proceeding purporting to address the validity of its trade secret claims until such time as the Commission conducts the hearing and in camera review that the Commission’s Trade Secret Rule requires.
THE HEARING OFFICER SHOULD DECIDE STANDING FIRST.
Only “a party or intervenor” may petition for access to protected information. Petitioners are not intervenors. Their claim to party status rests on the Petition alone, and whether they are “interested parties” entitled to file it is the first question the Motion to Dismiss presents. The answer is no. As VoltaGrid’s Reply Brief explains, Petitioners identify no right the Territorial Act confers on them, and their concerns are environmental ones that Sustainable Newton already raised in comments to the Environmental Protection Division and received relief. If Petitioners are not interested parties, the Trade Secret Motion is moot.
The Hearing Officer holds express authority to “dispose of motions to dismiss for lack of agency jurisdiction over the subject matter or parties or for any other ground.” The Stay Order reasoned that standing would not dispose of any investigation the Commission separately referred; however, whatever its effect on that investigation, standing controls the Trade Secret Motion. Petitioners filed it to obtain access only for themselves. 
This is not an academic exercise. The Trade Secret Rule envisions agreements to protect trade secrets by “[a]ny person who is granted access to protected information pursuant to paragraph (3).” Only proper parties and intervenors may be granted access. Allowing Petitioners to execute such an agreement pursuant to the Trade Secret Rule would presume Petitioners have standing to bring this Territorial Act action and are proper parties to this proceeding — the very same questions VoltaGrid has raised in its Motion to Dismiss.
Jurisdiction presents the same problem. The Trade Secret Rule’s procedures apply to information submitted by “any party or utility subject to the jurisdiction of the Commission.” The Stay Order recognized that the Commission’s Assignment reserves the jurisdictional question to the Commission on a certified record. Ordering disclosure before the Commission answers that question and impermissibly assumes the Commission has jurisdiction. This is profoundly prejudicial to VoltaGrid and improper.
THE BASIS FOR THIS PROCEEDING MUST BE ESTABLISHED.
It has been suggested that Petitioners’ standing need not be resolved because the Commission may undertake an investigation on its own initiative.  This is wrong. 
The governing rules, and VoltaGrid’s rights, obligations, and protections, depend on the basis on which the Commission is proceeding. It is undisputed that the Commission has filed no Complaint. This contested case therefore exists only because Petitioners initiated it. If they are not interested parties, and lack standing to bring such a complaint, the entire proceeding must be dismissed. 
If the Commission wishes to proceed on its own initiative, it can issue a Notice of Inquiry pursuant to its standard investigative procedures and open a generic docket to consider and evaluate on-site generation. Such proceeding would be heard by the Commission, not a hearing officer, and it would conclude with factual findings and policy recommendations.
The basis for this proceeding thus carries direct legal consequences. This docket is proceeding solely on Petitioners’ Complaint, and their standing must be resolved.  A determination on standing cannot be further deferred without causing damage to VoltaGrid from the release of trade secret information to an entity that is not entitled to it.  If Petitioners lack standing, their Trade Secret motion must be denied.  For itself, the Commission has not authorized any action pursuant to its investigatory powers, instead allowing Petitioners’ Complaint to proceed. Absent clear direction from the Commission, the Trade Secret Motion must be denied.  
Either way, every tool the Petitioners are using — the motion for access to trade secret documents, the ability to cross-examine witnesses, and their demand for an enforcement order — is a tool of a party to an enforcement action, not to an investigation. Their standing to bring such an enforcement action is thus directly relevant and cannot be sidestepped.  
PETITIONERS WILL SUFFER NO PREJUDICE IF THEIR STANDING IS ADDRESSED FIRST.
“Once the data that constitute a trade secret are disclosed to others, or others are allowed to use those data, the holder of the trade secret has lost his property interest in the data.” If the Commission later concludes that Petitioners lack standing, or that it lacks jurisdiction over VoltaGrid, no order can restore what VoltaGrid lost. 
Petitioners, by contrast, suffer no prejudice if the Motion to Dismiss is resolved first. Being denied access to confidential documents they have no right to obtain is not prejudice. At most, their access to information VoltaGrid contends they have no right to obtain will be delayed while the process specified in the Trade Secret Rule plays out. And Petitioners have presented no evidence — none — that they would be prejudiced by any potential delay in the hearing schedule.
ONLY THE COMMISSION CAN RESOLVE TRADE SECRET CLAIMS.
Paragraph (7) of the Trade Secret Rule is explicit. A challenge proceeds by motion “with the Commission.” If, “after a hearing and an in-camera inspection, the Commission determines” that information is not a trade secret or should be disclosed, “the Commission shall issue an order to that effect, which order shall be automatically stayed for thirty (30) days from the date of the order,” to provide time for the affected party to seek judicial review of the Commission’s decision.
The Amended Order acknowledges that a movant under paragraph (7) “is entitled to a hearing and in-camera inspection by the Commission.” It then inexplicably sets the hearing before the Hearing Officer, provides no in-camera inspection, and states that “the Hearing Officer will consider a decision on this matter.” That procedure does not comply with the specific provisions of the Commission’s own rules — provisions that are specifically intended to protect VoltaGrid’s interest in its confidential business information.
VOLTAGRID DECLINES TO PRESENT ANY PROPOSED “AGREED” PROTECTIVE ORDER.
The continued refusal to address threshold legal questions that are ripe for decision, and the proposed deviation from the procedures specified in the Trade Secret Rule, places VoltaGrid in an untenable position. It denies VoltaGrid process to which it is entitled under the law; risks the disclosure of VoltaGrid’s confidential information to adversarial third parties and competitors; subjects VoltaGrid to a hearing on an undetermined set of issues, without any guidance as to the legal standards to be applied or the facts that may be relevant; and purports to order VoltaGrid to “voluntarily” agree to relinquish control of its trade secrets.
This deprives VoltaGrid of its property and due process rights without compensation and is inconsistent with the Commission’s hearing process. It also sets a dangerous precedent for any party that might come before the Commission in the future — leaving future parties (whether subject to the Commission’s jurisdiction or not) to wonder whether their rights and the confidential business information they provide to the Commission will be protected, or whether their trade secrets will instead be subject to involuntary release or compelled disclosure to groups that are ideologically opposed to both their business sector and the services they provide, as well as potential business competitors — all before the Commission even considers the question of whether such groups seeking access to their confidential information have standing.
Given this, VoltaGrid declines to provide any proposed “agreement” to make its trade secrets available to Petitioners, unless and until Petitioners’ standing is established, the Commission’s jurisdiction is confirmed, and it is afforded the process and protections to which it is entitled under the Commission’s Trade Secret Rule, including the automatic stay and opportunity for judicial review.       
CONCLUSION
VoltaGrid respectfully requests that the Hearing Officer and the Commission:
Remove the October 29, 2026 hearing from the calendar and hold the Trade Secret Motion in abeyance until the Hearing Officer decides the Motion to Dismiss;
Relieve VoltaGrid of the requirement to file a protective agreement; and
If the Trade Secret Motion survives the Motion to Dismiss, refer the Trade Secret Motion to the Commission for hearing and decision under Rule 515-3-1-.11 and (7), with an in-camera inspection, a closed session for protected content, reasonable time to prepare, and the automatic thirty-day stay of any disclosure order.

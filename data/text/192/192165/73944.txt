Introduction and Qualifications
Q1.Please state for the record your name, position, and business address.

A1.[Mr. Giese]. My name is Will Giese.  I am Southeast Regional Director for SEIA.  My business address is 1425 K St. NW #1000, Washington, DC 20005.
Q2.Did you participate in panel that submitted testimony in the first phase of this proceeding?
A2.[Mr. Lucas] Yes.
A2. [Mr. Young] Yes.
A2.[Mr. Giese] No, I did not testify in the first phase of this proceeding.
Q3.On whose behalf are you submitting testimony?
A3.Our testimony is provided on behalf of the Georgia Solar Energy Industries Association (“GASEIA”), SEIA, and Vote Solar, as joint intervenors in this proceeding (collectively “GASEIA Intervenors” or “GASEIA”).
Q4.What is the purpose of your testimony?
A4.The purpose of our testimony is to respond to the Georgia Power Company’s (“Georgia Power”, “GPC”, or “the Company”) supplemental direct testimony in this proceeding that focused on RNR customers.  
Q5.Please provide an overview of your supplemental testimony.
A5.We do not believe that GPC has made a good-faith effort to comply with the language in the Commission’s final order in the 2022 IRP to “provide, test, and challenge cost-based data” about RNR customers.  The Company has failed to provide the cost-based data and analyses directly requested by the Commission.  Its reasons for doing so – including that the COSS model is “insufficiently precise … to produce meaningful results” – are directly contradicted by its use of the COSS for other small customer groups.  GPC has repeatedly failed to provide complete and accurate responses to discovery requests, incapacitating the ability of other parties to “test and challenge” the data.  
Notwithstanding the cost-based data issue, the Company’s position regarding the “cost shift” caused by revenue erosion is stunning in its implications.  Quite simply, GPC appears to believe it is not merely allowed to keep but in fact entitled to recover all revenue shortfalls from revenue erosion from any customer for any reason.  This wildly broad reading of its rights to profits is out of step with conventional cost-based regulation applied by state commissions and should be checked.
The Company’s quantitative analysis regarding revenue erosion is methodologically flawed beyond repair, while also containing a glaring error that massively inflated its final cost shift value.  The Company’s choice to compress 1,100 RNR customers (which was already a subset and thus potentially not reflective of the total RNR population) into a mere four “representative” customers introduces errors that were only compounded when these four customers were expanded to represent 5,000.  The Company makes no effort to validate whether its approach was robust, whether the four customers represented the group they were supposed to, or how several errors and questionable choices impacted its final results.
The Company’s policy argument about states “walking away” from net metering is unsupported.  In fact, net metering remains the law of the land in most states and investing in residential and small commercial BTM solar creates jobs and brings investment to the state.  Further, states that have made changes away from traditional net metering have landed on export structures and rates that are far more favorable than GPC’s RNR Instantaneous program.  The proposal to mandate demand-based rates must be rejected as discriminatory.  The Company’s main support for this – its revenue erosion analysis – is fatally flawed and contains a major error that undermines its conclusion.  
The Company claims that the Commission should wait until it sees how the Inflation Reduction Act will play out.  While we agree that the IRA has the potential to be transformative to the solar industry, some of its policies will take time to implement.  Further, several of the expanded incentives are only available to commercial taxpayers, not residential customers.  State policy support remains critical to grow BTM markets, particularly in the short term while several headwinds persist.
We find the Company’s testimony regarding a lack of capacity value for solar to be uncompelling.  The Company core argument is a circular; since all existing capacity assets are sunk costs, and since new RNR systems cannot reduce sunk costs, there is no avoided capacity benefit.  That argument fails to acknowledge that the Company’s system has increased in magnitude every year over the past decade, with future, avoidable costs repeatedly being converted into embedded, sunk costs.  
While we were not able to produce all of the cost-based RNR analyses that we had hoped because of the Company’s failure to provide timely and usable responses to Public Interest Staff’s (“PIA Staff” or “Staff”) discovery, we were able to produce high-level metrics that supports our conclusion that the cost to serve RNR customers is lower than non-RNR customers.  We also walk through a more detailed Michigan analysis that compares the cost metrics before and after solar was installed to demonstrate the magnitude of cost reduction.  We also show that exported energy from RNR customers consistently contributes to load reductions during peak COSS hours and on-peak TOU hours.  In each of these cases, it is clear that the burden that RNR customers place on the system during peak hours that drive system costs falls after installing solar and are lower than a typical residential customer.  
Finally, we support the Commission working with all stakeholders, including the solar industry and GPC, to address the consumer protection issues that the Company has raised.  We recognize that expanding monthly netting has the potential to attract more out of state companies and with them “bad actors.”  However, this is a complex issue involving multiple organizations, jurisdictions, and state agencies and so a comprehensive and thoughtful approach is required.  We would recommend the Commission invite the Attorney General’s office to participate in an inter-agency task force to develop a comprehensive set of policy recommendations for Commissioners to consider within six months.  Such a task force should include Georgia Power, solar industry representatives, Commission Staff, representatives from the EMCs and municipal utilities, and possibly other state agencies like the Governor’s Office of Consumer Protections.
Q6.What are your overall recommendations?
A6.We respectfully make the following recommendations of the Commission:
The Commission should recognize that GPC failed to provide a cost-based analysis related to RNR customers and failed to provide responsive data to PIA Staff’s discovery requests which ultimately impaired the ability for parties to “provide, test, and challenge cost-based data”.
The Commission should require GPC to remove or modify its disclaimer on the COSS study that implies that non-Staff parties are violating Georgia law by using the model.
The Commission should reject GPC’s quantitative analysis related to revenue erosion as methodologically flawed while noting the impact of the major error related to summer bills.
The Commission should reject GPC’s policy-based arguments against extending the RNR Monthly Netting program and find instead that growing a robust BTM solar market will lead to increased investment, economic activity, and job growth.
The Commission should reject GPC’s request that new RNR customers must take service on a three-part rate design as unfairly discriminatory.
The Commission should continue to quantify capacity value that BTM solar exports produce and add this value to the Solar Avoided Cost calculation.
The Commission should accept Joint Intervenors’ analysis that shows that RNR customers have lower cost of service than non-RNR customers and that the cost to service RNR customers is lower after they install solar than before they install solar.
The Commission should extend the RNR Monthly Netting program with no cap while also implementing a $20 minimum total bill that is inclusive of the customer charge.
The Commission should convene an inter-agency task force to develop recommendations on consumer protection issues that may affect a growing RNR market.
GPC Has Not Complied with the Final IRP Order Directing GPC to Provide Cost-based RNR Data and Analyses
Q7.What is the purpose of this section of your testimony?
A7.We discuss the Company’s approach to the RNR Tariff issue in its written testimony, hearing testimony, and responses to discovery.  
Q8.Please summarize your findings.
A8.We do not believe that GPC has made a good-faith effort to comply with the language in the Commission’s final order in the 2022 IRP to “provide, test, and challenge cost-based data” about RNR customers.  The Company has failed to provide the cost-based data and analyses directly requested by the Commission.  Its reasons for doing so – including that the COSS model is “insufficiently precise … to produce meaningful results” – are directly contradicted by its use of the COSS for other small customer groups.  GPC has repeatedly failed to provide complete and accurate responses to discovery requests, incapacitating the ability of other parties to “test and challenge” the data.  
Notwithstanding the cost-based data issue, the Company’s position regarding the “cost shift” caused by revenue erosion is stunning in its implications.  Quite simply, GPC appears to believe it is not merely allowed to keep but in fact entitled to recover all revenue shortfalls from revenue erosion from any customer for any reason.  This wildly broad reading of its rights to profits is out of step with conventional cost-based regulation and should be checked.
Q9.What is your recommendation on the Company’s analysis?
A9.We implore the Commission to reject GPC’s unsupported opposition to expanded RNR monthly netting and move forward with an expansion of the pilot program until such time as GPC adopts a more rigorous, cost-based approach to this issue.  The Commission ordered that all parties – including the Company –provide data so that it can make an informed decision about RNR customers rather than debating “whether or not to continue temporarily doling out net metering to a subset of customers.”  We support the Commission’s approach and have worked as diligently as possible to provide it with the data and analysis on which it could rely to make this decision.  
But we have been unable to provide the level of analysis that we would like this Commission to have available, due to the Company’s refusal to share meaningful data or conduct its own analysis.  GPC did not produce a cost-based analysis, claiming it was not possible.  The discovery process and response timeline were not conducive to establishing a robust baseline set of information.  GPC’s repeated delays in providing accurate and usable response to PIA Staff discovery – the only vector that non-Staff intervenors had for this data – prevented us from testing and challenging the Company’s positions.  Finally, GPC’s absolutist position that it is entitled to any revenue erosion from any group of customers for any reason is a breathtakingly overreach and cannot be allowed to stand.
GPC Has Failed to Provide a Cost-Based Analysis of RNR Customers, Despite the Commission’s Direct Requirement
Q10.Please review the history of parties’ RNR testimony in the 2022 IRP and this case.
A10.Beginning in the 2022 IRP case, the Company advanced arguments claiming that RNR Monthly Netting customers were causing a “cost shift” to other customers.  GPC claimed that RNR Monthly customers caused a $382,000 and $968,000 revenue erosion in 2020 and 2021, respectively, that needed to be recaptured from other non-participating customers.  Because of this and other issues, it recommended that the 5,000 RNR Monthly limit not be expanded.
GASEIA Intervenors filed testimony in the IRP that refuted GPC’s claims.  We revealed that the Company’s analysis was rooted in revenue erosion, not in cost of service.  Further, we demonstrated that these costs are not recovered from ratepayers in the short run and that the presence of BTM solar moderates infrastructure needs in the long run.  Because the Company did not perform any short-run or long-run cost of service analysis, it had no basis to claim a cost shift exists.
In the Commission’s IRP order, it directed the Company and parties to consider the RNR issue in the then-upcoming rate case:
Rather than continuing to try to address rooftop solar with a series of temporary and partial measures where some customers receive net metering while others receive only avoided cost or, in some cases, nothing at all, I move that the Commission direct Staff and the Company to each address the appropriate structure and pricing of the RNR-10 tariff in the current rate case so that the Commission can move towards a fair and long term way of addressing the issue. 

In this IRP, we simply do not have the cost information necessary to set just and reasonable rates, terms, and conditions for rooftop solar.  In addition, we do not have the information to determine whether or how such rates, terms, and conditions would impact other customers. Under my motion, the parties in the rate case would have the opportunity to provide, test, and challenge cost-based data and the Commission would have more options than simply deciding whether or not to continue temporarily doling out net metering to a subset of customers.
Fast forwarding to the current rate case, the Company reintroduced its revenue erosion analysis in the first phase of this docket.  GPC proposed to adjust its target base revenue requirement by $1.4 million, claiming that it was necessary to recoup revenue loss from Monthly RNR customers. We also contrasted the magnitude of the purported RNR Monthly Netting revenue shift ($0.20 per year for a typical residential customer) with the Company’s requests in this case ($195 per year), the projection of Vogtle costs ($166 per year), and the excess revenue collection above its central ROE but below the ROE dead band ($46 in 2021.)
In the second phase of this case, GPC expanded on its RNR testimony but did not fundamentally shift its approach. It also submitted policy-based testimony suggesting that states were rapidly shifting away from traditional net metering.  Based on the results from its updated RNR customer and policy analysis, the Company recommends the Monthly RNR program not be expanded and that all new RNR customer starting in 2023 be required to take service on a three-part rate such as the TOU-RD tariff.

GPC’s Primary Analysis Contains a Major and Obvious Error that Should have been Avoided
Q16.While GPC did not provide a cost-based analysis, did it provide any analysis to support their RNR testimony?
A16.Yes.  The Company conducted a revenue erosion analysis that claimed to show a major cost shift from RNR customers to non-RNR customers.  The conclusion of this analysis is shown below in Figure 1.  In essence, GPC calculated that RNR customers were shifting $1,356 per year to non-participating customers, $1,093 of which was due simply from taking service on the R tariff.  By contrast, it claimed the total cost shift value fell to $291 per year if RNR customers took service on the TOU-RD rate, of which $254 was due to the TOU-RD rate structure itself.
  
Figure 1 - GPC Monthly Netting Cost Shift Analysis
Q17.What was your initial response to this analysis?
A17.Aside from the obvious – that it was not a cost-based analysis – we were struck by how much of the cost shift the Company claimed was due to self-consumption on the R tariff structure rather than the Monthly Netting program.  This was not the focus of the Company’s testimony prior this point, and the Company’s workpapers regarding its $1.4 revenue erosion / cost shift claim did not focus on self-consumption.  This opens a very fraught line of inquiry as it suggests that actions that customer take to reduce their usage is somehow a form of cost shift to other customers.  And sure enough, this is exactly what the Company argues.
Q18.Did you analyze the workpapers after they were provided?
A18.Yes.  the basic approach involves calculating what solar customers would have paid for service on the R and TOU-RD tariff before and after they installed solar.  The difference was broken into the two categories shown above – impacts from rate design and impacts from monthly netting.  The Company’s result that the R tariff was producing nearly $1,100 per year of cost shift was one of the main reasons it recommended that all future RNR customers be required to take service on a three-part rate such as the TOU-RD.
Q19.What is your impression of the Company’s analysis?
A19.We found it poorly conceived and executed, replete with inappropriate assumptions and simplifications.  While we discuss the myriad issues with analysis below, the biggest issue is one that clearly should have been avoided.
Q20.What is that issue?
A20.The workpaper has a glaring error: the bill for usage over 1,000 kWh in summer months for the R tariff is multiplied by 2.  This is due to a mistake in a formula where rather than rounding the value to 2 decimal places, the workpaper multiplies this value by 2.
Q21.Why do you believe this error should have been caught rather than just accepting it as an inadvertent error?
A21.For several reasons.  First, the magnitude of the error is immediately obvious in the results.  For the 4th quartile customer group, May usage over 1,000 kWh per month was about $100, but June usage over 1,000 kWh was $650.  The Company should be sufficiently familiar with its tariffs that it should have seen this anomalous result, particularly given usage over 1,000 kWh only went up roughly 60% rather than 300% between May and June.  
Second, the workpaper also includes a direct comparison of bills between the R and TOU-RD rate, including for pre-solar customers.  Due to the error, the 4th quartile customer shows an annual bill of $5,116 on the R tariff compared to $2,909 on the TOU-RD tariff.  Given these tariffs are intended to be revenue neutral with respect to each other, it is a major red flag for a non-solar customer to see a bill 75% higher on the R tariff. 
Third, extraordinary claims require extraordinary proof.  Claiming that BTM solar is so punitive to non-solar customers that even self-consumption drove more than a thousand dollars of cost shift on the R tariff is a bold claim.  This was the “smoking gun” of the Company’s only new quantitative analysis on the cost-shift issue in this case, and it should have been more diligent to ensure it was presenting accurate information to the Commission.
Q22.What happens when you correct this error?
A22.The Company’s cost shift argument is severely wounded.  Because the error occurred only on one component (pre-solar use on the R tariff), its impact was maximized.  Correcting only this error and rerunning the Company’s analysis produces substantially different results.  The $1,093 of “cost shift” from the R tariff falls to $560.  So instead of the R tariff causing $840 more per year in “cost shift” than the TOU-RD tariff, the value falls to $305.  Even without addressing the many other issues with the Company’s analysis, this alone slashes the impact of and justification for requiring new RNR customers to move to three-part rates.

GPC Believes It is Entitled to Recover Any Revenue Erosion Regardless of its Earnings
Q34.Please describe how GPC proposes to address the revenue erosion from RNR Monthly Netting customers.
A34.The Company proposed to make an adjustment of roughly $1.4 million to its target base revenue to “to account for revenue erosion resulting from the Renewable and Non-renewable (“RNR”) tariff monthly netting pilot.”  This adjustment would increase the Company’s overall base revenue requirement by a corresponding amount, which would then trickle through to the ECCR and NCRR riders that are calculated based on a percentage of base revenues.  All told, the Company’s analysis would result in a test year net increase of $1.8 million in revenue between base rates, riders, and RNR credit offsets.
Q35.If this revenue adjustment was not made by the Company in this rate case, would the revenue erosion from RNR Monthly Netting customers be collected from other non-participating customers?
A35.No, it would not.  As we discussed in our IRP testimony, a reduction in revenue does not automatically turn into a cost that is recovered from other customers; it simply lowers revenue, which in turn lowers Company profits.
Q36.If the proposed revenue adjustment is approved, what will happen?
A36.Based on the last eleven years of history, it will not be used to make up a revenue shortfall but rather would only enhance the Company’s profits.  
Q37.Please explain further.
A37.The Company’s earnings are governed by a ROE earnings band.  This structure, which we discussed extensively in our Phase 1 testimony, allows the Company to keep 100% of revenues above its approved ROE but under the top of the earnings dead band.  In every one of the past 11 years, the Company has collected revenues beyond its central approved ROE, resulting in profits higher than the central value approved by the Commission.  
In fact, in 2020, the Company’s actual ROE was 11.89%, above the Commission-approved ROE of 10.5% but below the upper ROE dead band of 12.0%.  In this case, the Company retained 100% of the $217 million revenue surplus.  In 2021, the Company’s performance was even better, with an actual ROE of 12.09%, substantially above the Commission-approved 10.5% and above the upper ROE dead band of 12.0%.  This resulted in a revenue surplus of $258 million, of which only $5.7 million was refunded to customers.  
In years in which the Company earns between its approved ROE and the upper ROE dead band (e.g., 2020), 100% of any revenue adjustment would be collected from customers and simply added to the Company’s revenue surplus.  In years in which the Company earns more than the upper ROE dead band (e.g., 2021), 100% of any revenue adjustment would still be collected from customers, but 40% would be directly refunded to customers, 40% would be kept to pay down future costs, and 20% would be retained by the Company.
The only way that the revenue adjustment would not simply add to the Company’s revenue surplus is if it were earning less than the Commission-approved ROE, which is currently set at 10.5%.  This has never happened since 2011.  And even if the Company is earning below its central ROE value, it does not have any recourse to seek recovery of those revenues unless the ROE falls below the bottom of the ROE dead band, currently set at 9.5%.  Given Southern Company’s recent 2022 Q3 earnings report, it appears that 2022 will be another banner year for GPC, with 2022 year-to-date net income 80% higher than in 2021.,
In light of this dynamic, revenue erosion from RNR Monthly Netting customers is not a “cost shift” to other customers.  It does not contribute to under-collection of revenues because there is no under-collection of revenues.  Simply put, an approved revenue adjustment for RNR Monthly Netting revenue erosion will produce only one outcome: enhancing the Company’s already-massive revenue surplus. 
Q38.Despite the coherent logic of your previous argument, has GPC investigated how the earnings band mechanism and revenue erosion interact?
A38.No, it has not.  When asked this specific question, it stated “the Company considers the total retail revenues and its retail cost of service as a whole in its calculation of actual earned retail ROE and the impact on the earnings band.  Therefore, the Company has not specifically evaluated the isolated impact of the revenue erosion or cost shifting caused by RNR customers within or outside of the earnings band.” 
It continued: 
Any revenue erosion not captured through retail rates, which occurs during the 3-year alternate rate plan will serve to lower the Company’s earned ROE. As a matter of appropriate ratemaking principles, to set retail rates at the correct level to recover the Company’s retail cost of service from customers, any amount of revenues lost from, or avoided by, a certain group of customers must be recovered from the remaining body of customers. This is true unless the amount of lost or avoided revenues also result in a comparable reduction in the Company’s retail cost of service/ revenue requirement.
Q39.What is your reaction to this response?
A39.It is shocking on its own, but when combined with the Company’s other responses, it is utterly stunning in its scope and implications.  In essence, the Company claims that it is owned “any amount” of revenue that is avoided by any group of customers through any action.  The Company does not qualify this statement on whether this revenue erosion comes from a reduction in usage, behavior changes, installation of DER, or anything else.  It makes this statement independent of the operation of the earnings band mechanism and whether it is earning above or below its central approved ROE or above the upper earnings dead band.  It makes it regardless of whether customer circumstances change, such as a child moving out, installing efficient appliances, or switching from electric heat to gas heat.  
The only limit to its claim is if there is a “comparable reduction in the Company’s retail cost of service / revenue requirement.”  But of course, it simultaneously claims that no action can reduce the embedded cost of service of its system, and that its tools and models do not allow it to perform said analysis about a cost of service reduction.  Put together, any time a customer reduces what they pay to GPC, GPC finds “as a matter of appropriate ratemaking principles” that it should be able to charge everyone else to make up the difference.  As we have said, stunning in its scope and implications.  If GPC’s captive customers want to pay more money to the Company’s shareholders, they can buy Southern Company stock.  But they should not be forced to contribute to this excess through their bills.
Q40.Is this interpretation consistent with the Company’s other testimony?
A40.Yes, it is.  Company witnesses Legg, Evans, and Gattie repeatedly claimed that revenue erosion was a cost shift, even while admitting they never performed any cost of service analyses.  The majority of Legg and Evans cost shift value was based not on monthly netting but rather on the structure of the R tariff.  In fact, of the $1,356 cost shift the Company calculated, it claimed that $1,093 or 81% was actually based on the R rate design, with the monthly netting pilot’s role diminished to “exacerbate[ing] this cost shift.” 
The line of thinking clearly tees up GPC’s position expressed in the discovery response: that it is entitled to any and all earnings that it can get under Commission-approved rates regardless of where it lies on the earnings band.  It does not consider the central ROE value – currently set at 10.5% – and the corresponding rates designed to collect revenue at that level to be limiting.  It clearly considers all revenue under the upper ROE dead band not as revenue that is it just able to keep, but revenue that it is entitled to.  Nor does the Company consider the upper ROE dead band – currently set at 12.0% – to be limiting.  In its arguments, it views the sharing mechanism as a sufficient guardrail.  Any revenue erosion from one group of customers, even if it is already collecting revenues at a level that exceeds the upper dead band, “must be recovered” from other customers.  
Q41.Do you agree with this interpretation?
A41.No.  While we agree that GPC is allowed to keep money above its central ROE and the upper dead band, we do not believe that the Company is entitled to this revenue.  Our view is that the revenue requirement calculated at the central ROE represents the best and most appropriate value for profits.  If the Commission truly believed that GPC was entitled to a 12.0% ROE, then it could have simply established a 12.0% ROE.  But it did not do this; it established a 10.5% ROE paired with an earnings band mechanism as part of the multi-year rate plan structure.  This is a critical distinction, and one that has major implications for both the requested $1.4 million RNR adjustment and the Company’s arguments about cost shifts in general.  Clearly, however, the Company sees this as a distinction without a difference.
Q42.Why is this distinction important?
A42.Because GPC is a regulated monopoly and for most of its customers, and all of its residential customers, there is no competitive alternative to the Company’s electric service.  The Commission is charged with establishing fair, just, and reasonable rates that serve as a substitute for competitive markets.  
A reasonable revenue requirement would include one that is calculated based on the central ROE set point. The Company’s Analysis is Flawed, Uses Dubious Assumptions, and Discards Critical Data
Q46.Please describe the GPC analysis related to RNR customers performed in this phase of the rate case.
A46.In the IRP docket earlier this year and in the first phase of this rate case, the Company produced an analysis that compared retail bills from customers on the RNR Monthly Netting and RNR Instantaneous tariffs. In the second phase of this rate case, GPC presented a modified version of this analysis.  It begins by analyzing 1,110 RNR Monthly Netting customers that had been in the pilot program for the full 12 months of 2021.  The Company “gathered the metered readings (both delivered and pushed back energy) from 2021 as well as the installed panel capacities for each customer.”  From this data, the Company estimated each customer’s “usage before solar” through a methodology that was not described in discovery nor present in the initial workpapers submitted.  These “pre-solar” usage levels were aggregated into quartiles based solely on annual energy use.
The Company then calculated the average annual usage and system size of each quartile, again discarding any information related to seasonality and timing of usage.  From here, the Company “selected four representative hourly loadshapes, ensuring that each sample closely matched the annual usage profile and did not have solar already installed behind-the-meter.”  GPC then generated an hourly solar load profile for each of these four customers using PVWatts based the average quartile size system, and netted this against the non-solar load profile to create four “post-solar” hourly load profiles.  These hourly load profiles were then run through the R and TOU-RD tariffs to calculate “before” and “after” bills for the four customers.  Using these “insights”, the Company expanded the results of the four customers to the full 5,000 RNR Monthly Netting pilot size to model the impact of the full pilot.  The Company’s analysis suggested that if all 5,000 customers were on the R tariff, there would have been $6.9 million in revenue erosion, with each customer causing a $1,377 annual “cost shift.”
Q47.What is your impression of this analysis?
A47.Setting aside that nothing in this analysis is related to cost of service, and setting further aside the major error discussed above, the Company’s methodology is fatally flawed and the entire analysis should be disregarded.  The Company makes too many simplifying assumptions that render the final results non-representative of the data set.  This applies in particular to the Company’s decision to compress the 1,110 customers into four customers before expanding the four to represent 5,000 customers. Q48.Are there particular choices that you disagree with or mistakes you found in the Company’s analysis?
A48.Yes, there are myriad, listed below.
GPC fails to conduct the analysis at an individual customer level.  This is critical as net load is a function of both solar generation and household use, and bills are a non-linear function of net load.  Averaging out 1,110 customers into four quartiles removes all the variation that is inherent in individual customer’s usages.
GPC calculation of the “pre-solar” loads overstates generation and is not representative of the RNR customer group.  The Company was asked for “all data, studies and analyses” used in its analysis.  Nowhere in the initial round of workpapers did the Company demonstrate how it calculated the pre-solar loads.  In a subsequent round of discovery, the Company provided its methodology, which was to simply assume that every PV system had a 16.3% capacity factor and a 120% AC to DC ratio.  These values are indicative of ideal, unshaded, south-facing roofs with insolation attainable in only some parts of the state.  Applying this solar production to every RNR system overestimates how much solar is produced, which overstates the “pre-solar” values used in subsequent analyses.
GPC uses four non-solar customers as the basis for its entire analysis.  There is considerable risk in selecting only four customers to represent the entire RNR pilot program.  No data about whether these customers are representative of the broader customer basis is provided.  It is unknown whether they use electric or gas heating, whether they live in a rural or urban area, whether they use air conditioning or not, whether they have electric vehicles or not, whether they work at home or not, whether they have medically necessarily equipment or not, whether they have kids or not, whether they went on an extended vacation or not.  Each of these and many other factors impacts how customers use electricity.  The Company did no analysis to show that these customers are in fact “representative” of the RNR population as a whole.
GPC did no analysis to justify how it selected the four “representative” non-participants.  GPC initially claimed that it “ensured that each sample closely matched the annual usage profile” of the quartiles of RNR Monthly Netting customers.  But no analysis was provided on how this selection was made.  Did GPC cross reference these customers against monthly peak and off-peak usage or monthly peak demands of the 1,110 RNR customers?  The Company did not provide workpapers despite being asked, so additional discovery was required.  In follow up, the Company confirmed that “there is not a formal model or worksheet that would be considered to be ‘analysis,’” and it only performed a perfunctory screen to ensure that the four customers were “in line with the annual usage targets from the RNR group.”  No analysis of load shape, monthly usage, or peak/off peak usage was considered. 
GPC incorrectly uses PVWatts data for single-year analysis.  PVWatts calculates solar generation based on a “typical meteorological year”, which is an agglomeration of weather data from different years.  GPC then applied this generation to the 2021 usage of the four customers.  There is a strong correlation between weather, solar generation, and customer usage; hot sunny summer afternoons lead to higher usage, but also produce more solar energy.  GPC mixes apples and oranges, rendering the post-solar net loads non-representative of actual 2021 usage.
GPC erroneously fails to shift PVWatts generation to Daylight Savings Time.  PVWatts hourly data is presented in Standard Time.  GPC’s load data from the four customers is presented in Daylight Prevailing Time, as evidenced by the blank value for 2-3 AM on March 14, 2021, when clocks “sprang forward”.  There is not a corresponding adjustment to PVWatts data, meaning that for most of the year between March and November, the solar generation is one hour earlier than the load data.  This is meaningful as it increases the amount of solar available during late summer afternoon hours when loads are high.
There are anomalous values in the hourly meter read data for the four customers.  Reviewing the hourly data reveals values that unexpectedly spike to extremely high levels for one hour before returning to more typical usage.  For example, the customer representing the 2nd quartile has repeated spikes from 1 PM to 2 PM on days throughout the year when load increase from 2-3 kW to over 10 kW, before falling back rapidly.  Even if these figures are accurate, it is highly unlikely that this load pattern is reflective of a broad set of customers.
GPC incorrectly scales results from four customers to all 5,000 customers.  In its workpapers, the Company expands the results from the four “representative” customers back to the 1,110 customer RNR Monthly Netting set as well as to a hypothetical set of 5,000 customers.  The Company provided no evidence that the 5,000 customers were sufficiently similar to the four representative customers to justify this expansion.  The total “cost shift” also assumes all 5,000 customers would be residential customers, which is demonstrably false.
GPC conflates avoided energy savings and fuel savings in the presentation of its results.  In its testimony, GPC reports that customers only produce $21 in annual “avoided energy savings / benefits”.  This value is calculated from the non-fuel variable O&M costs, but does not include the $254 in annual fuel savings that the Company itself identified.

Q52.Please briefly discuss the scope and purpose of a cost of service study.
A52.As described in the 1992 NARUC Electric Utility Cost Allocation Manual, “Generically, the prime purpose of cost of service studies is to aid in the design of rates.” A cost of service study (“COSS”) is a method that maps the embedded or marginal costs of a utility’s electric grid to the various customer classes based on those classes’ characteristics and usage of the system.  Assets and operating costs are functionalized based on their use (e.g., production, transmission, distribution, or customer), classified based on the utility service being provided (e.g., demand, energy, or customer), and allocated to customer groups based on usage of the system during certain key hours (e.g., coincident peak hours) or characteristics (e.g., number of customers or share of revenue).  The result of the COSS is an accounting of costs incurred by, revenue collected from, and rates of return for each customer class.  
Armed with this information, a utility may propose to increase or decrease the target revenue for classes that are under- or over-collecting relative to the Company’s overall rate of return target.  In this case, the Company presented its historic 2021 COSS showing that current rates produced an overall test year rate of return of 8.37% for the Company, with variations present in the various classes (Domestic – 7.25%, Small Business – 9.30%, Medium Business – 10.08%, etc.).  It also presented its future test year COSS showing that returns would fall under current rates, with an overall test year result of 6.27%.  This reduction in return is the basis for the Company’s request for more revenue.
Q53.Please briefly discuss the scope and purpose of rate design.
A53.Once the cost of service for each class and rate of return has been established, the revenue requirement for each class can be calculated.  This is the figure that will be collected through the retail rates if customers use electricity according to Company forecasts.  The structure of these rates should be informed by the COSS process (i.e., they should reflect cost-causation principles) but must also incorporate other non-analytical considerations (e.g., gradualism, customer acceptance, avoiding rate shock, etc.).  
Suppose for instance that a utility desires to collect $1 billion in revenue from its residential class.  A utility can design any number of rates that will collect this revenue based on the billing determinants (e.g., the kWh of usage, the ratio of peak/off peak usage, the peak demand of customers, etc.).  For instance, it can have a flat per kWh fee that divides $1 billion by the total kWh sold.  It can have a customer charge that collects $100 million and spread the rest over a volumetric rate.  It can calculate usage during different time periods and establish a time of use (“TOU”) rate where the sum of each TOU rate multiplied by the number of kWh in that TOU period equals the total revenue.  There are literally unlimited ways in which rates can be designed to mathematically obtain the revenue requirement.  
GPC has multiple residential rates, each of which has a different structure but are designed to be “revenue neutral” so that they would collect the same total revenue if every customer took service only on that rate.  For instance, the R tariff uses a seasonally-differentiated block structure, with an inclining block in the summer and a declining block during non-summer months.  The TOU-PEV rate has three volumetric time of use periods (peak, off-peak, and super-off peak). The TOU-RD rate has two volumetric time of use periods plus a demand component that charges based on the highest single hour of usage per month.  These rates were at one time calculated to attain the same revenue requirement based on the total residential class’s usage patterns.
Q54.Does the revenue collection results of a rate design change the underlying cost of service of customers taking service on those rates?
A54.No, it does not.  Cost of service is a function of a group of customer’s usage of the system.  It incorporates characteristics such as total energy use, timing of energy usage, usage during peak demand hours, and so on.  These characteristics impact how the system needs to be designed to meet customer loads; customer groups that use more power during on peak hours contribute more to production costs than groups that use less power during peak hours.  Similarly, customers that use more power overnight when there is spare capacity put less demand on the system and have a lower cost of service.
These characteristics are separate and distinct from revenue collected through different ratees.  For example, suppose a utility had a rate where every customer paid a fixed $100 fee every month regardless of how much and when they used electricity.  A customer that only uses energy during non-peak hours would pay the same as a customer that only uses energy during peak hours, even though the former has a lower cost of service than the latter.  Further, if the customers change their behavior, such that the former uses more energy on peak and the latter uses more energy off peak, the revenue collected on this rate does not change even though these usage changes impact the cost of providing service to the customers. 
Q55.Have other commissions addressed the distinction between cost of service and revenue erosion / rate design?
A55.Yes.  Michigan has a statute that requires export compensation for BTM systems to be cost-based.  DTE Energy (“DTE”), a regulated Michigan utility, proposed to implement a grid access charge for solar customers that was predicated on a supposed cost shift that led to a revenue deficiency from BTM DG customers.  This is the exact type of argument made by the Company in this case.  The Michigan Public Service Commission rejected DTE Energy’s argument, finding the grid access charge “is neither COS-based, as required by MCL 460.6a(14), nor equitable.  As ELPC noted, the utility’s method for calculating the [grid access] charge explicitly relied on the distribution revenue deficiency and not on any cost to serve.”  While Georgia does not have a similar statute regarding cost-base export rates for BTM DG customers, it is instructive that another utility commission reviewed and rejected claims that a rate design / revenue erosion argument for BTM DG customer was compatible with a statutory requirement for cost-based rate setting.

A73.We find it misleading at best.  While it is accurate that Georgia historically has had relatively low residential rates, its customers still face high bills.  Residential customers – particularly low- and moderate-income customer – don’t care about how high their rate is, they care about how high their bill in.  And Georgia residential bills are high – and going to get even higher if GPC’s proposed rate increases are approved.  
Q74.What are your conclusions regarding this element of GPC’s testimony?
A74.We believe that Dr. Gattie’s focus on rates rather than bills is inappropriate for the residential sector he analyzes.  Despite lower-than-average rates, GPC customer use more electricity than average and thus pay higher bills.  Georgia’s residential electricity bills are frequently in the top 10 in the country and even higher compared to regulated states.  And all of this is before the hundreds of dollars of requested rate increases, Plant Vogtle costs, and deferred fuel balance expenses hit their wallets.
Requiring RNR Customers to Take Service on Demand-Based Rates is Discriminatory and Must be Rejected
Q75.Are there currently any restrictions regarding the residential tariff that a customer must take service under to qualify for the RNR program?
A75.No.  A residential customer may take service under any of the residential tariffs and participate in the RNR program.  
Q76.Does the Company propose to change this?
A76.Yes.  It proposed that all new RNR customers who move to the tariff on or after January 1, 2023, must take service on a three-part rate with a demand charge.  For residential customers, the only such tariff is the TOU-RD rate.  In the Company’s view, three-part rates “better align with the Company’s underlying costs, and further mitigate the cost shift between participating and non-participating customers.”
Q77.Do you agree with the Company on the general point about three-part rates?
A77.No.  As discussed extensively in our direct testimony, demand-based rates in general and the TOU-RD rate in particular is a terrible fit for residential customers.  While we did not recommend eliminating this tariff as it was voluntary, we did recommend the Commission require a major redesign, ensure it was revenue-neutral with respect to the R tariff, and prevent it from being the default residential rate. 
Q78.Do you agree with the specific point about requiring RNR customer to take service on a three-part rate, which for residential customers would be the TOU-RD rate?
A78.Absolutely not.  Requiring a group of customers to take service on a particular rate as a condition for installing solar is discriminatory.  Georgia statue prevents all electricity suppliers from “having or applying any rate, charge, or service rule or regulation which unreasonably discriminates against or in favor of (1) any member of a class of consumers as opposed to any other consumer who is or should be in the same class of consumers for such purposes, or (2) any class of its consumers as opposed to another class of consumers for such purposes.”
While we are not attorneys and are not offering a legal opinion, imposing a mandatory rate on a member of the residential class that wishes to install solar is clearly against the plain reading of the statutory text.  Nowhere in the case has GPC argued that RNR customers should be treated as a separate customer class, and even if it did, it would still run into issues with discriminating against an entire class of RNR customers.  
As it currently exists, the TOU-RD rate is a bad fit for pretty much all customers that are not structurally benefitted by demand-based rates.  We showed that 90% of customers with usage below the median were charged more on the TOU-RD rate than the R rate.  Even if the rate were to be redesigned, it would still be improper to require RNR customers to take service on only one rate.  It runs antithetical to the notion of optionality and customer choice in addition to being discriminatory.
Q79.Has another state tested this approach of mandating BTM PV customers take service on a three-part rate?
A79.Yes.  The Kansas Corporation Commission (“KCC”) approved a mandatory demand-based rate for solar customers for Evergy.  The KCC was sued, and eventually the case was heard by the Kansas Supreme Court.  In its ruling overturning the KCC’s decision, the Kansas Supreme Court found that the mandatory demand rate was “simply price discrimination” and remanded the case back to the KCC.  The KCC then ordered that DG customers take service on rates that were identical to standard residential rates.
Q80.Is GPC aware of any utility that requires solar customers to take service on three-part rates?
A80.No, it is not.  The Company did note that its sister company Alabama Power requires residential customers to take service on one of three rate options, one of which is their newly approved demand-based rate.  But even in this example, customers are not required to take service on demand-based rates.
Q81.And how is Alabama rated in the country on per-capital residential solar capacity?
A81.It is dead last.
Q82.What is the basis for GPC’s proposal to shift customer to a demand-based tariff?
A82.It is to avoid what the Company claims is a major cost shift caused by customer self-consuming energy on the R tariff.  Separate from the question of RNR Monthly Netting, which is a post-hoc adjustment applied to customers’ bills, the Company analyzed the bill reductions that RNR customers receive from self-consuming energy generated from their solar facilities.  The Company calculated that nearly $1,100 a year per RNR customer was “shifted” to non-RNR customers due to the R rate design.
Q83.Was this the figure that you discussed earlier that had a major error in the calculation?
A83.Yes.  As it turns out, the Company’s workpapers contain a glaring error that, when fixed, reduced the $1,093 per year figure to $560 per year.  The Company’s own calculation suggests that the TOU-RD rate still has a $254 per year cost shift embedded in it, meaning that instead of being $839 higher per year, the R rate is instead only $305 higher per year.  While we do not endorse the Company’s analysis for a host of other reasons, the one line of support it offered for require three-part rates was effectively cut down.
Q84.Even considering the corrected values, do you agree that the “cost shift” from self-consumed energy exists and justifies a requirement to take service on three-part rates?
A84.No.  The mere fact that the Company claims a “cost shift” occurs from self-consumed energy is very troubling.  This argument has nothing to do with exported energy and nothing to do with monthly netting.  RNR customer pay GPC full retail for every kWh consumed from the grid.  The fact that they have an alternative source of supply for some portion of their electric load is irrelevant.  
Customers are under no obligation to purchase a certain amount of energy from the Company.  If they want to install a spa and heat it with electricity, the Company will provide that power and the customer will pay for it.  If the next owner of the house removes the spa, there is no expectation that the new owner will continue to pay for something they are not getting from GPC.  Similarly, if a customer replaces old, inefficient air conditioners with new heat pumps and reduces their summer load by 50%, there is no obligation to continue paying GPC based on the quantity of energy they used to use.  
GPC is trying to break the cardinal rule that “what happens behind the meter stays behind the meter.”  The Company has no business meddling in how much a customer consumes, whether they change their usage patterns, or what technologies a customer uses meets their loads.  Claiming that customers that reduce their usage are somehow creating a cost shift that must be recovered from other customers is an extreme position and should not be accepted by the Commission.
Q85.What do you recommend with respect to this issue?
A85.The Commission should reject the Company’s proposal to require RNR customers to take service on three-part rates as discriminatory.  They should be able to take services on whatever tariff they wish as long as it is currently eligible for the RNR program.  The Commission should also confirm that residential customers have the unilateral right to reduce their usage through any means without triggering a “cost shift” that must be collected from other customers.  
The IRA Will be Helpful to BTM Solar, but Headwinds Remain
Q86.What was another policy argument advanced by the Company against expanding the RNR Monthly Netting cap?
A86.The Company argued that the recently passed Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”) would provide substantial support to the industry and that the best course of action is to wait to see how that play out.  As expressed by Dr. Gattie, “Given the generous benefits provided by the IRA and Georgia’s proven track record in developing these resources, I conclude that further ratepayer investments are not needed to encourage this growth, and additional subsidies would likely result in nothing more than additional windfall payments to solar installers and developers on the backs of other ratepayers.
Q87.Did. Dr. Gattie provide any support whatsoever for his conclusions?
A87.No, he did not.  There was nothing in his testimony that justified his statement that further investments were not needed or, that if provided, they would result in “nothing more than additional windfall payments to solar installers.”  These claims are made with no support, no market analysis, no cost trend analysis, and no investigation of solar installer business models.  It is pure speculation and should given no weight by the Commission.
Q88.Was there some confusion in the prior hearing about new provisions in the IRA?
A88.Yes, the appeared to be some confusion about the eligibility of the new credits for different groups of customers.  We offer some clarification below:  
Residential customers are not eligible for the Production Tax Credit (“PTC”).  The PTC, which offers a $0.015/kWh inflation-adjusted credit (currently set to $0.026/kWh) for renewable energy production, was expanded to make solar an eligible technology, but it is only applicable to commercial taxpayers under Section 48.  Residential customers do not qualify.  SEIA anticipates that while some utility-scale solar developers may opt for the PTC in certain parts of the country, for higher-cost BTM systems, the investment tax credit (“ITC”) will remain substantially more lucrative. 
The residential ITC is not refundable.  Residential customers do qualify for a 30% ITC under Section 25D, but they must have taxable income to offset as the tax credit is not refundable.  Non-profit entities are now eligible for direct pay, so this provision might assist non-residential entities such as churches seeking to install BTM solar.
The residential ITC was restored to 30%, but it never went below 26% before.  Prior to the passage of the IRA, the Section 25D residential ITC was scheduled to step down from 30% in 2021, to 26% in 2022, to 22% in 2023, and to 0% in 2024 and beyond.  However, with the passage of the IRA in 2022, the credit never dropped below 26%.  Restoring this credit to 30% is certainly beneficial but it will not have a major impact on the short-term market conditions.
Domestic Content and Energy Community Bonus Credits are only for commercial customers.  As with the PTC, the bonus adders for domestic content and energy community locations are only applicable to commercial entities under Section 48.  Residential owners will not qualify for these bonus credits.
Commercial projects over 1 MW face new conditions on the full ITC.  Unlike before, when all projects received a 30% ITC, commercial projects that exceed 1 MW will now have to comply with prevailing wage and apprenticeship program requirements in order to receive the full 30% ITC.  If they meet none of the new requirements, the projects will only receive a 6% ITC.
Q89.Do you feel that the IRA will be helpful for the BTM solar and solar plus storage market?
A89.Yes.  Restoring the ITC from 26% to 30% is helpful, as is securing it for ten years.  Many of the IRA provisions are applicable only to commercial projects, such as the bonus tax credits for domestic content and energy community projects.  SEIA fully supports these qualifications and anticipates that the new adders will help develop and support a robust domestic manufacturing industry and increase access to solar to more low- and moderate-income customers through programs such as community solar.  
Unfortunately, at the same time, other factors impacting the market have driven prices up by well more than the 4% of reestablished ITC.  Tariffs continue to be placed on solar cells and modules, and COVID-induced supply chain pinches have impacted the solar market as with many other industries.  Likewise, solar projects are not immune to inflation, which has pushed up commodity prices such as steel and aluminum as well as wages.  At the same time, the sharp rise in interest rates has made consumer loans and other financing more expensive, further reducing demand.  Together, these trends have contributed to push national average prices up from $3.90/w to $4.10/w in the first half of 2022.  This price jump effectively negates the benefit of the higher ITC.  Along with higher inflation, interest rates have also increased.  Many customers finance their solar systems with loans, and the jump in personal loan rates is another near-term headwind.
SEIA certainly anticipates that the market dynamics will improve, and that the IRA will unleash domestic manufacturing and support the creation of well-paying jobs in the industry.  But these benefits will take some time to implement and internalize.  In the meantime, support from state policies such as net metering will be critical to help the industry through as a new equilibrium is reached.
GPC’s Position that Exported Energy Provides No Capacity Value is Untenable
Q90.What is the purpose of this section of your testimony?
A90.We discuss the Company’s testimony and discovery responses applicable to whether exported energy from RNR systems has more value than just avoided energy.  
Q91.Please summarize your findings.
A91.We find the Company’s testimony regarding a lack of capacity value for solar to be uncompelling.  The Company core argument is a circular; since all existing capacity assets are sunk costs, and since new RNR systems cannot reduce sunk costs, there is no avoided capacity benefit.  That argument fails to acknowledge that the Company’s system has increased in magnitude every year over the past decade, with future, avoidable costs repeatedly being converted into embedded, sunk costs.  
Q92.What is your recommendation on the Company’s analysis?
A92.We recommend the Commission continue the line of inquiry it began in the Avoided Cost proceeding and develop new analytical methods for valuing the capacity benefit of renewable resources such as solar.  Once this value is established, it should be applied to the export credit that RNR customers receive.
GPC’s Arguments Regarding Avoided Capacity Costs are Circular
Q93.How does the Company support is claim that exported energy from RNR customers does not provide any capacity benefit?
A93.The Company advances a circular argument related to RNR customers.  It starts with the premise that its COSS relies on embedded costs, which by definition are based on assets already in rate base.  It continues that because these costs are already incurred, they are not avoidable by RNR customers.  As such, it ascribes “no avoided cost benefits to the Company’s customers for demand- or customer-related services associated with RNR resources’ delivery of energy to the grid.”  In other words, because exported energy from RNR customers does not change the assets already placed into service, there can never be any avoided capacity cost associated with exported energy and paying RNR customers for this service is inappropriate.
Q94.What is the logical flaw in this argument?
A94.It is the Company’s overly narrow view that only historic costs should be considered.  It is correct that GPC does not pull up poles and wires or retire operating power plants when a RNR customer installs a new BTM solar installation.  Based on this interpretation, no action that any customer takes – whether installing BTM solar, energy storage, energy efficiency, demand response, or any other load-modifying technologies – would ever be able to change the embedded cost of service, and thus avoiding capacity costs is impossible.
But it is clearly true that the presence of that PV system, battery, or efficient heat pump on the Company’s grid will impact what assets it will need to serve load in the future.  As such, incremental RNR capacity reduces demand that will need to be met by the Company’s assets, which in turn has an avoided capacity cost benefit.  
Q95.Has GPC’s system grown over the past decade?
A95.Yes, it has grown substantially.  Figure 8 below shows the net plant in service (“PIS”) for the Company by function.  The net PIS has climbed from $15.8 billion in 2011 to $26.9 billion in 2021, a compound average growth rate of 5.5% per year.  Every rate base component increased in value in every year except for one (production between 2019 and 2020), with the rate base growing by an average of $1.1 billion per year.

Figure 8 - GPC Net Plant in Service
Despite this, the Company argues that each rate case should only consider the then-existing assets when determining whether BTM solar has capacity value.  Unfortunately for GPC’s customers, the “then-existing assets” increase every year.  The Commission has responsibility to ensure that rates are reasonable and just; this obligation is not limited to current rates, but extends to future rates as well.  As it is painfully obvious to all in this proceeding, and has happened every year in the past decade, future costs eventually become current costs which drives up customer’s bills.  The Commission must see through the Company’s circular arguments and recognize and properly compensate capacity benefits associated with exported RNR energy.
Despite Evidence to the Contrary, GPC Maintains Exported RNR Energy Has No Capacity Value
Q96.Does the Company agree with your premise that exported RNR energy has capacity value?
A96.No, it does not.  The Company makes several arguments against this claim:
The approved PURPA Avoided Cost methodology in assigns no value to capacity.
Exported energy is not considered in the Company’s long-term generation or transmission planning process.
The Company completely discounts distributed generation when planning its distribution system.
Q97.What is your response to the first point about PURPA avoided costs?
A97.It is correct that the Commission removed capacity value from the PURPA avoided costs in its most recent order on this issue.  However, at the same time it required the Company to “conduct an analysis of the capacity value of different renewable technologies using the effective load carrying capability method and include the results of this analysis in its 2022 IRP” and directed GPC to meet with Staff to “discuss how location value of renewable resources might be accounted for in the Company’s payments to QFs.”  
The only reason for the Commission to order a study on renewable capacity values and on locational benefits (which are typically associated with the distribution system) is if it wanted to have a more robust basis for determining capacity value for intermittent renewables.  Thus, the removal of the old capacity value methodology in the RBC framework is not indicative that the capacity value is and will henceforth be zero.  In fact, it is more likely that an updated capacity value will be determined based on new methodologies.
Q98.What is your response to the second point about exported energy not being considered in the Company’s long-term planning frameworks?
A98.A89.We do not dispute that the Company does not currently include exported energy in its long-term generation and transmission planning frameworks.  However, this is distinct from whether exported energy should be evaluated in these frameworks.  
The Company expands on this point in its discovery responses.  First, it states that because RNR customers have “no long-term, fixed commitment to the Georgia Power system, it is not appropriate to quantify any such medium or long-term demand-related benefits, as they are not relied upon in the Company’s long-term planning.”  It also states “The Company plans to meet future customers’ needs for generation capacity without taking into consideration the generation exported from RNR resources. Currently, a small amount of MW is produced and consumed onsite from RNR customers relative to the planning process.”   Put together, the Company’s argument is two-fold: RNR customers have not signed a long-term, fixed commitment contract with the Company for exported power; therefore, it is inappropriate to include in the Company’s planning process.
Q99.What is your impression of this argument?
A99.It is another example of the Company using its current protocols as a justification of its actions rather than addressing the underlying issue.  The Company’s choice to exclude exported energy from RNR customers is just that – a choice.  It could easily account for exported energy in its planning process, just as it does for self-consumed energy.  In fact, the distinction that the Company makes between self-consumed energy and exported energy is completely arbitrary when it comes to the overall operation of the grid.  
When viewed from the perspective of the bulk power grid – to which its generation and transmission planning processes are applicable – whether 5 kW of BTM generation is self-consumed at 1 Main Street or exported to 2 Main Street is irrelevant.  All that matters is that 5 kW was generated.  The timing and nature of that generation is important, but these are not the arguments the Company is making.  
There was debate in the IRP about the capacity accreditation of solar in different seasons of the year, but these discussions implicate the capacity value – not existence – of PV based on methodologies such as ELCC.  The Company is not arguing that the capacity value of exported energy should be lower because of winter generation; it is arguing there is no capacity value for exported energy because it is not included in the planning process.
Q100.Is a long-term, fixed commitment contract a necessary prerequisite for BTM energy to have capacity value?
A100.No.  The Company’s argument appears to mirror its approach to only include IPP contracts that have a long-term, fixed commitment in its IRP list of resources available to meet demand.  Clearly, the Company is not going to pursue similar QF-style long-term contracts with thousands of residential customers for exported energy, nor should it.  At the same time, PV generation from BTM solar will happen with or without a contract, and whether or not it is self-consumed or exported to a neighbor.  The Company’s decision to exclude this resource from planning is a choice, not an emergent property of BTM solar.  It is simply one more choice that GPC makes to devalue BTM solar and ultimately discourage its broader deployment.
Q101.Are there other resources included in the Company’s long-term planning process that do not require long-term, fixed commitment contracts but instead rely on statistical assumptions?
A101.Yes.  The Company’s load forecasts are based on projections of usage from different sectors.  The residential sector forecast includes assumptions about appliance usage and housing stock.  GPC, as is typical in the industry, made assumptions about the adoption of efficiency standards such as ENERGY STAR.  These factors impact how much energy the Company projects will be used by air conditioners, water heaters, televisions, and other categories.  Nowhere in this process does the Company require long-term, fixed commitment contracts to install certain types of air conditioners.  Instead, it simply assumes an evolution in the marketplace and incorporates a statistical analysis of air conditioning usage.  GPC can and should adopt a similar protocol for exported BTM solar.
Q102.What is your response to the third point about the Company completely discounting distributed generation on the distribution system?
A102.The Company’s position is unfortunately neither surprising nor inconsistent with its approach to infrastructure planning, but it requires considerable clarification and correction.  The Company’s full response follows:
Based on current IEEE standards, there is no offset to any future distribution costs. Consistent with IEEE-1547, the IEEE Standard for Interconnecting Distributed Resources with Electric Power Systems, Southern Company requires all distributed resources (including solar generation facilities) to cease energizing the distribution system upon occurrence of a fault on the distribution system. These resources are required to stay off-line for up to five minutes after the distribution system voltage and frequency are restored to ranges specified in ANSI standard C84.1. Therefore, the distribution system must be capable of serving 100 percent of the customer’s load during this timeframe. Further, the distribution system must be capable of serving 100 percent of the load when behind-the-meter generation resources are unavailable for any reason. As a result, planning of the distribution system must assume that the feeders containing such generation are still required to carry the full load connected to these circuits, thereby negating any potential deferment of distribution investment, as referenced in the Renewable Cost Benefits Framework filed in the Company’s 2016 and 2019 IRPs in Docket Nos. 40161 and 42310, respectively.
First, the Company conflates the IEEE-1547 standards and distribution system planning.  In fact, IEEE-1547 clearly states “This standard does not address planning, designing, operating, or maintaining the Area [electric power system] with DER.”  Based on this alone, the Company’s claim that IEEE standards preclude the offset of any future distribution cost is simply inaccurate. 
Additionally, it is unclear whether the Companies are referring to the IEEE 1547-2003 standard, or the updated IEEE 1547-2018 standard which was designed with the expectation that DERs installed under this standard will “play and larger role and, overall, improve power quality” in distribution and transmission networks broadly depending on penetration level.
GPC also states that resources are “required to stay off-line for up to five minutes” after a trip.  In fact, the “delay entering service” parameter is adjustable between 0 seconds and 10 minutes, with a default of 5 minutes.  In other words, the Company has the ability to select how long a DER must remain offline after voltage and frequency has returned to normal levels and could set this value as low as 0 seconds.  This fact renders moot the Company’s next argument that “the distribution system must be capable of serving 100 percent of the customer’s load during this timeframe” since the Company has the ability to set this timeframe to 0 seconds.
Q103.What is your response to the claim that a distribution system must be able to serve 100 percent of the load of the customers?
A103.This statement is contradicted by well-established distribution planning procedures.  The Company, like all other utilities, does not plan its distribution system to handle the maximum potential demand of all customers on its system.  Instead, it plans its system to serve the maximum diversified demand of all customers on its system.  This distinction captures the fact that customers do not place their maximum loads on the system simultaneously.  One customer may reach her peaks in the morning before leaving for work, while another might reach her peak when charging an EV overnight.  
The load diversification factor quickly scales as a function of customers, as seen below in Figure 9.  For equipment close to customers such as line transformers, the diversified demand may be 60% lower than the sum of the maximum individual customer demands with as few as 15 customers.  

Figure 9 - Residential Load Diversity and Transformer Sizing
For larger assets shared by hundreds of thousands of customers such as feeders or substations, the planned loads experience even higher load diversity.  GPC’s claim that it must discount all BTM solar generation in distribution planning conveniently ignores that fact that it already builds the system to meet substantially less than 100 percent of potential load of connected customers.  
Further, distribution deferrals through DERs such as BTM solar and storage have been demonstrated in other utilities.  In practice, the state of Hawaii’s Scheduled Dispatch Program (“SDP”) and its recently ordered Bring Your Own Device (“BYOD”) tariff utilize both advanced inverter functionalities and BTM storage dispatch schedules that produce various benefits to all customers on the electric grid, whether they have installed DERs or not.  These functions produce a variety of benefits such as fast frequency response (“FFR”), capacity load reduction, load shifting at peak, and scheduled dispatch capabilities.  All of these functions are provided to the grid by the consumer at distinct time periods and are compensated based on values derived using RESOLVE, a resource investment modeling software utilized by many large investor-owned utilities.  Notably, IEEE 1547-2018-compliant smart inverters and modeling software used in Hawaii for this purpose can or already have been installed in Georgia today. 
Also notable among these non-wires alternatives is the Brooklyn Queens Demand Management (“BWDM”) project at ConEdison in New York.  Facing the need for a new $1.2 billion substation, ConEdison worked with customers and businesses to instead develop a suite of demand response, energy efficiency, and DER projects that could defer the need for the new substation.  While the project had its challenges, it has also met its peak reduction goal and ConEdison is planning for even more customer-sited load reductions. 
Q104.Please summarize your findings and recommendations.
A104.The Company’s arguments that exported energy provides no capacity benefit is unsupported.  GPC spends billions of dollars each year on new grid assets, some portion of which can be avoided by reducing demand placed on the system.  We recommend the Commission continue its progress towards an analytical framework that establishes this capacity benefit across the various functions of the grid (e.g., production, transmission, and distribution) and incorporates this value into the exported energy credit.

While Georgia Power Did Not Produce a Cost-of-Service Analysis of RNR Customers, Such an Analysis Is Possible
Q108.How could one demonstrate the difference in cost of service for RNR customers as compared to non-RNR customers?
A108.One could do this in a few ways, in increasing level of complexity and sophistication.  The most straightforward and simplest would be to analyze the usage characteristics of RNR and non-RNR customers that drive costs on the system.  For instance, one could compare total usage, TOU period usage, demand during system coincident peak hours, and demand during class peak hours for these customer groups.  Given these values drive cost allocation in the COSS, if one were to demonstrate that RNR customers have meaningfully lower loads during high-use times, it would follow that RNR customers would have a lower cost of service.
While the above analysis is simple and straightforward, it does not account for the fact that customers who install solar might look different than the average residential class.  An analysis that would account for this would investigate the usage of RNR customers before and after they installed solar.  Since of the main concerns expressed in this case is whether the reduction in revenue associated with the installation of BTM solar is offset by the reduction in costs caused on the system, comparing RNR customers before and after solar could help illuminate this issue.  If the reduction in key metrics such as demand during COSS hours and on-peak energy usage fall more than revenue collection falls, then one can reasonably conclude there is not a cost shift.
A final, more complex way to do this analysis would be to create a new customer class within the COSS model that represents RNR customers, which, despite the Company’s claims to the contrary, is sufficiently precise to manage such a calculation.  This would require all corresponding analyses that feed into the COSS to be updated as well.  While this approach would produce an analytical result that could compare the cost to serve RNR customers as a discrete customer group, we do not recommend its use for anything other than this limited analytical scope.  To be explicit, we do not recommend that RNR customers be a separate COSS class in the way that different rate groups are COSS classes, nor that RNR customers be prevented from taking service under any currently-available tariff.  Rather, an alternative COSS model could be developed simply to analyze how cost of service for RNR customers compares to non-RNR customer to help inform the conversation around the continuance of the Monthly Netting pilot.

A109.No, it did not.
Q110.Were you able to perform any of these analyses?
A110.We were able to complete the first analysis (RNR customers vs. non-RNR customers) using the partial data set provided from the Company.  We were not able to produce the second analysis (RNR customers before and after solar) because the Company did not provide data that Staff requested in a timeline manner.  Had that data been available and usable, it may have been possible to perform the second analysis.  We were not able to do the more complex analysis (COSS model) for the reasons discussed previously.
Cost-Based Analysis #1 – RNR vs. Non-RNR Customers
Q111.Please discuss your first analysis comparing usage of RNR customers and non-RNR customers.
A111.This analysis used hourly load data by tariff and hourly inflow (power consumed from GPC’s grid) and outflow (power exported to GPC’s grid) data provided for 1,016 RNR customers in 2021.  The non-RNR customer data set was developed from hourly load data by tariff provided by the Company that was first converted to a per capital usage by dividing total tariff usage by the count of customers.  Inflow and outflow data for the RNR customers was averaged for each hour of the year.  Once these initial steps were done, data could be compared between the two sets.  As we discussed in the first phase of the hearing, since 2021, the TOU-RD tariff has been greatly expanded with defaulted-in customers that have lower-than-average usage.
Q112.How is the information presented, and how are the COSS metrics best interpreted?
A112.In each of the following graphs, RNR customers’ data is presented in two ways.  The first is the “RNR Inflow” which represents the amount of energy consumed by these customers from the grid.  The second is “RNR Net”, which nets outflow energy with inflow on a customer-by-customer, hour-by-hour basis to produce a “net” inflow from the Company.
The COSS demand metrics of 4CP, 12CP, and Class NCP are also used below.  In the Company’s COSS model, the 4CP, 12CP, and Class NCP metrics most directly correlate to transmission, production, and distribution system cost causation, respectively.  That said, we and other parties have argued that in a summer peaking utility such as GPC, the 4CP allocator is more appropriate for production costs. 
All of the values below are based on customer meter reads.  This means that they are not grossed up for line losses.  Given that marginal line losses can be substantially higher than average line losses, and that line losses during hot summer days are higher than mild spring days, the COSS demand figures below actually understand the magnitude of system-wide demand reductions.   
Q113.How do the RNR customers and non-RNR customers compare in terms of basic usage?
A113.After installing solar, RNR customers appear to be slightly larger than average users of energy.  Figure 10 shows how the monthly usage profile tracks other residential tariffs other than TOU-PEV, which consistently has the highest usage of all residential tariffs.  
 
Figure 10 - Monthly Usage by Tariff
When one analyzes hourly usage, the clear pattern of solar generation emerges.  In Figure 11 showing summer months, RNR Inflow customers use more energy during the morning and overnight periods than the other residential tariffs, and less energy during midday.  When outflows are considered, the RNR Net values fall substantially during midday.  RNR Inflow values largely remain below the other tariffs for most of the TOU peak hours.

Figure 11 - Summer Hourly Usage by Tariff
Figure 12 below shows the hourly usage in non-summer months.  Once again, the RNR Inflow customer use more energy than other tariffs except TOU-PEV in the evening, overnight, and morning hours, and less during midday.  RNR Net customers realize more outflow during non-summer months, pushing midday net usage negative. 

Figure 12 - Non-Summer Hourly Usage
Q114.How did load during key COSS hours vary between the two customer sets?
A114.BTM solar meaningfully reduced the RNR customer’s load during the 4CP hours and all but a few winter 12CP hours, as seen in Figure 13 below.  Where the preceding charts showed that total consumption was slightly higher for RNR Inflow customers, the demand during key COSS hours is comfortably lower than the other tariffs in all but the core winter months.  And as storage attachment rates increase, even those winter morning peaks could be reduced.   

Figure 13 - 12CP Demand
Figure 14 shows the average loads on the 4CP, 12CP, and Class NCP hours.  RNR Inflow customers’ 4CP is comfortably below the R, TOU-PEV, and TOU-REO, and is even lower than the small customer TOU-RD group.  RNR Inflow 12CP is slightly lower than the other tariffs, but falls more when outflow is included.  Class NCP occurred on July 29, 2021 between 6 and 7 PM, and while usage for the RNR Inflow group did not peak during this hour, it was higher than other tariff groups.

Figure 14 - COSS Demand Metrics
Q115.How did energy consumption fall within the TOU periods across the tariffs?
A115.While analyzing COSS demand metrics is an important consideration for COSS, it is somewhat limited in this analysis given we only had complete data for a single year.  If one of the key 12CP, 4CP, or Class NCP hours was partly cloudy in 2021, it could impact the above metrics in a way that may not reflect the long-term averages during peak hours.  As such, investigating how energy consumption falls across the TOU periods provides a bit more latitude in the event only a single year of data is available.
Subtle but clear trends exist in the TOU energy use of these customers.  As could be anticipated from Figure 11 above, RNR Inflow customers use less energy during peak TOU hours than other customers and more energy during the super-off-peak (“SOP”) period.  When outflow is factored in, RNR Net customers’ peak usage falls even further.  Figure 15 and Figure 16 show these relationships both in absolute and normalized terms. 

Figure 15 - TOU Energy Use by Period

Figure 16 - TOU Energy Usage Percentage by Period
When compared to the R class, RNR Inflow customers use meaningfully less energy on peak (772 kWh vs. 935 kWh) even though their overall usage is higher.  Off-peak usage is nearly identical between the groups (7,754 kWh for RNR Inflow vs. 7,623 kWh for R), meaning that the increased usage occurs during the low-cost, overnight SOP period.  This is readily visible in the percentage graph, showing lower peak usage (6.0% vs. 8.0%) and higher SOP usage (33.9% vs. 26.3%).  These are the exact metrics that one would want in a customer; use less energy during high-cost peak hours and more energy during low-cost SOP hours.
Q116.What is the final chart you have prepared?
A116.The final chart in Figure 17 below shows the load factors using the COSS demand metrics.  Load factor relates the demand of a customer or customer group to its energy usage and can be viewed as a measure of the efficiency of system usage.  The higher the load factor, the more consistently or “efficiently” a customer used the system.
As expected, the lower 4CP demand values coupled with higher energy consumption causes the 4CP RNR Inflow and RNR Net load factors to climb substantially above all other tariffs.  The RNR customers approach a 4CP load factor of 80%, while the remaining tariffs are in the 50-55% range.  The increase on the 12CP is not quite as large, but RNR Inflow customers still attain a 68% load factor, roughly 10% higher than the R group.  The Class NCP load factor is marginally lower for RNR Inflow customers, reflecting the higher demand metric used in that calculation.

Figure 17 - Load Factors
Q117.What are your overall conclusions based on this first analysis?
A117.The data above strongly support GASEIA’s position that RNR customers have a lower cost of service than do non-RNR customers.  RNR Inflow customers have lower 4CP and 12CP demand allocators, use less energy during on-peak periods, and have higher 4CP and 12CP load factors.  These are all hallmarks of putting less stress on the system during hours that drive system costs.  When outflow energy is considered, the RNR Net data exhibits even greater cost reductions, with further falls in COSS metrics and even lower peak energy usage.
The demand reductions are most strongly concentrated in the 4CP (transmission) and 12CP (production) metrics.  But as discussed, GPC’s allocation methodology is unusually for a summer-peaking utility, and it is our experience that similar utilities use a 4CP allocator for production and a 12CP allocator for transmission.  If this more standard convention was used by the Company, the demand reduction from RNR customers would be better reflected in cost reductions.
Cost-Based Analysis #2 – BTM Customers Before and After Solar Installation
Q118.Were you able to perform the second analysis discussing changes that occurred after customers installed solar in another case?
A118.[Mr. Lucas] Yes, I was.  In another DTE rate case, the utility provided 2018 and 2020 hourly usage for 342 residential customers who installed BTM PV in 2019.  It also provided hourly data for non-PV residential customers over this time period.  I was able to use this data to analyze the usage pattern changes for customers before they installed solar and after they installed solar, including the timing and magnitude of both inflows from DTE and outflows to DTE.
Figure 18 below shows the key results from this analysis.  Customers that installed BTM solar substantially reduced their usage during critical COSS hours (e.g., 4CP, 12CP, Class NCP).  After considering both inflow reduction and outflow during the same hour, BTM DG customers nearly eliminated their 4CP load (-91%), substantially reduced their 12CP load (-62%), and meaningfully reduced their Class NCP (-41%)  

Figure 18 - DTE BTM PV COSS Analysis
Q119.While it is clear the peak demand metrics fell substantially, did the energy consumption which drives bills fall by the same amount?
A119.No, it did not.  DG customer inflows fell from about 912 kWh a month before PV to about 684 kWh a month after DG, a 25% reduction.  So, while these customers cut their demand on the system by 91%, 62%, and 41% for these key metrics, they only reduced their inflow energy purchased from DTE at full retail rates by 25%.  Further, these DG customers – like GPC’s RNR customers – still used more inflow energy even after installing PV (684 kWh/month) than the typical residential customer (632 kWh/month) while simultaneously using less inflow energy during on-peak hours.
Q120.What is your takeaway from this analysis?
A120.The key metrics that drive cost of service – 4CP, 12CP, Class NCP, total energy, on-peak energy – all improved after installing solar.  But they did not fall equivalently.  Usage during the critical 4CP and 12CP hours and on-peak TOU periods fell substantially more than did overall energy.  Thus, while the revenue that these customers paid through their inflow fell, load during cost-causing hours fell substantially more.  The result is that there is no cost shift as the cost to serve these customers fell more than the revenue reduction.
Q121.Is there a reason to think that GPC’s RNR customers would perform similarly if you were able to analyze them?
A121.Yes.  While GPC’s and DTE’s systems are different, some usage patterns such as high demand in the summer, medium demand in the winter, and low demand during shoulder months is common across both territories.  Georgia customers use more energy overall than DTE customers, but Georgia also has higher insolation than DTE customers.  We would expect directionally similar results had we been able to perform the analysis we had hoped to. 
RNR Outflow Meaningfully Contributes to COSS Demand and Peak TOU Energy Reductions
Q122.Did you analyze the generation profile of RNR outflow energy?
A122.Yes.  Outflow energy is produced when there is solar generation in excess of a customer’s instantaneous household load.  For instance, if a PV generator is producing 5 kW of power and household load is 4 kW, then 1 kW of power will flow to the system and most likely be consumed by the customer’s neighbor.  
We aggregated data from the hourly outflows from 2019, 2020, and 2021 and broke the data into summer and non-summer months.  Even though outflow is a function of both solar generation and customer load, as seen below in Figure 19, the outflow shape closely mirrors solar production.  More than a third of outflow energy in the summer occurs during the 2 PM to 7 PM hours that comprise the TOU peak period.  Further, the average outflow during these peak periods is 0.5 kW per residential customer.  Multiplied by roughly 4,200 residential customers in the full RNR program and this is a non-trivial 2.1 MW resource.

Figure 19 - RNR Outflow Profile
Q123.Did you look at outflow during the specific 12CP hours?
A123.Yes.  As expected, solar outflow contributed during 12CP hours from April to October and did not contribute substantially during November to March as seen in Figure 20 below.  The Company’s system is still a summer-peaking system, so these outflows remain valuable to the transmission and production systems.  The outflow is also relatively consistent during summer months, reducing concerns that the resource will be broadly unavailable during those hours.  If scaled to all residential RNR customers, these outflows would reduce demand during the 4CP hours by about 1.5 MW to 1.8 MW.  

Figure 20 - Outflow 12CP Contribution
Q124.What is your conclusion regarding outflow from RNR customers?
A124.Outflow is a meaningful contributor to reducing peak energy use and contributing to 7 of the 12 CP hours, including all of the 4CP hours.  Outflow does vary depending on generation and self-consumption, but when averaged across the entire population of RNR customers, outflow was reasonably consistent.  These facts counter GPC’s argument that only resources with long-term, fixed commitments can be relied upon to produce demand reductions.
GASEIA’s Analyses Supports and Extension of the RNR Monthly Netting Program with a Minimum Bill
Q125.Based on your analyses above, what do you recommend with respect to the RNR Monthly Netting program?
A125.We reiterate our position that the RNR Monthly Netting program should be extended without limitation with a full retail rate offset.  We continue to support the implementation of a $20 per month minimum bill that we introduced in the 2022 IRP as a mechanism to prevent customers from reducing their bill below a reasonable threshold.  This would ensure that RNR customers contributed to the cost of the distribution system each month. 
Q126.Why do you believe the RNR Monthly Netting program should extended without a cap?
A126.Because we have reasonably demonstrated that there is no cost shift from RNR customers to non-RNR customers through monthly netting.  The reduction in revenue that results from providing retail-rate credits is offset by a concurrent reduction in cost of service.  The only justification for limiting the scope of the RNR Monthly Netting program is out of concerns of a cost shift.  But the Company repeatedly failed to produce any evidence of an actual cost shift, going so far as to claim that its COSS model was not precise enough to produce meaningful results.
Into that gaping vacuum, GASEIA Intervenors have introduced our own cost-based analysis.  In total, the analyses above show that RNR customers 1) have lower cost of service than non-RNR customers due to the significantly lower loads during hours that contribute to production and transmission costs and 2) reduce their cost of service after installing solar as compared to before installing solar.  Further, we have shown that exported outflow energy is reasonably consistent across the years and contributes to further reductions in peak usage by meeting portions of the RNR customer’s neighbor’s load.  So, while the revenue that these customers pay to the Company is reduced after installing solar, there is a concurrent reduction in costs.  And, in Company’s own words, there is no justification to claim a cost shift if “the amount of lost or avoided revenues also result in a comparable reduction in the Company’s retail cost of service/ revenue requirement.”
While this analysis was not as complete as we would have liked given the Company’s inability to furnish accurate data that was responsive to discovery, it still demonstrates the lower cost of service of RNR customers.  We also feel that this analysis is robust enough to demonstrate a lack of cost shift and for the Commission to expand the RNR Monthly Netting program.  And with this expansion, we expect that Georgia will see increased investment, economic activity, and job creation that comes with a robust BTM solar market. 
Q127.Does this conclude your testimony?
A127.Yes, it does.

14   Q53.       PLEASE BRIEFLY DISCUSS THE SCOPE AND PURPOSE OF RATE DESIGN.

15   A53.       Once the cost of service for each class and rate of return has been established, the revenue

16              requirement for each class can be calculated. This is the figure that will be collected through

17              the retail rates if customers use electricity according to Company forecasts. The structure of

18              these rates should be informed by the COSS process (i.e., they should reflect cost-causation

19              principles) but must also incorporate other non-analytical considerations (e.g., gradualism,

20              customer acceptance, avoiding rate shock, etc.).

22              residential class. A utility can design any number of rates that will collect this revenue based

24              demand of customers, etc.). For instance, it can have a flat per kWh fee that divides $1

25              billion by the total kWh sold. It can have a customer charge that collects $100 million and

22            demand on the system and have a lower cost of service.

12          under Section 25D, but they must have taxable income to offset as the tax credit is not
13          refundable. Non-profit entities are now eligible for direct pay, so this provision might assist
14          non-residential entities such as churches seeking to install BTM solar.

20          market conditions.

22          customers. As with the PTC, the bonus adders for domestic content and energy community
23          locations are only applicable to commercial entities under Section 48. Residential owners

13              instantaneous household load. For instance, if a PV generator is producing 5 kW of power

14              and household load is 4 kW, then 1 kW of power will flow to the system and most likely be

15              consumed by the customer’s neighbor.

16                       We aggregated data from the hourly outflows from 2019, 2020, and 2021 and broke

17              the data into summer and non-summer months.134 Even though outflow is a function of both

18              solar generation and customer load, as seen below in Figure 19, the outflow shape closely

19              mirrors solar production. More than a third of outflow energy in the summer occurs during

20              the 2 PM to 7 PM hours that comprise the TOU peak period. Further, the average outflow

21              during these peak periods is 0.5 kW per residential customer. Multiplied by roughly 4,200

22              residential customers in the full RNR program and this is a non-trivial 2.1 MW resource.

 3   Q126. WHY DO YOU BELIEVE THE RNR MONTHLY NETTING PROGRAM SHOULD EXTENDED

 4              WITHOUT A CAP?

 5   A126. Because we have reasonably demonstrated that there is no cost shift from RNR customers to

 6              non-RNR customers through monthly netting. The reduction in revenue that results from

 7              providing retail-rate credits is offset by a concurrent reduction in cost of service. The only

 8              justification for limiting the scope of the RNR Monthly Netting program is out of concerns of

 9              a cost shift. But the Company repeatedly failed to produce any evidence of an actual cost

10              shift, going so far as to claim that its COSS model was not precise enough to produce

11              meaningful results.

12                      Into that gaping vacuum, GASEIA Intervenors have introduced our own cost-based

13              analysis. In total, the analyses above show that RNR customers 1) have lower cost of service

14              than non-RNR customers due to the significantly lower loads during hours that contribute to

15              production and transmission costs and 2) reduce their cost of service after installing solar as

16              compared to before installing solar. Further, we have shown that exported outflow energy is

17              reasonably consistent across the years and contributes to further reductions in peak usage by

18              meeting portions of the RNR customer’s neighbor’s load. So, while the revenue that these

19              customers pay to the Company is reduced after installing solar, there is a concurrent

21              shift if “the amount of lost or avoided revenues also result in a comparable reduction in the

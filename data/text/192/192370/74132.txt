HEARING BRIEF OF
AMERICANS FOR AFFORDABLE CLEAN ENERGY

Comes now, AMERCANS FOR AFFORDABLE CLEAN ENERGY (“AACE”), an Intervenor in the above styled docket and pursuant to the Georgia Public Service Commission’s (“Commission”) Procedural and Scheduling Order, as amended, files this Hearing Brief on the evidence presented during the hearings conducted by the Commission in this docket.
AACE’S GOAL TO PROVIDE ELECTRIC VEHICLE (EV)
CHARGING TO THE PUBLIC

AACE members own and operate 784 convenience stores, public travel facilities and truck stops in Georgia at which they provide goods and services, primarily consisting of retail consumer goods, vehicle repair and service products, food, and fuel to the traveling public.  AACE members provide fuel for all shapes, forms and types of vehicles found on the nation’s roads today. Electricity provided to operate an electric vehicle (“EV”) is just another type of transportation fuel.  As AACE witness, Mr. Joe Akers, testified, electricity will be an important source of transportation fuel in the future, and AACE members can provide electricity to EVs in the exact same way they already provide gasoline and diesel to most vehicles on the road today.  To expand the services they can offer to the traveling public, Mr. Akers testified that AACE members are prepared to make significant capital investment to develop EV charging services supported by a reliable EV charging infrastructure, contingent upon their ability to deploy EV chargers and provide EV charging service to customers on a level playing field with those EV chargers deployed by the Company. 
Mr. Akers noted that AACE members’ business locations will play a critical role in advancing electricity fueled transportation through private investment.  He cited the Georgia Department of Transportation’s (“GDOT”) “Georgia Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Deployment Plan,” in which convenience store and gas station operators are identified as:
… logical candidates to offer charging services for EVs given that they currently provide fueling services to the nation’s drivers. 

GDOT recognized the value of placing EV charging stations at AACE members’ existing business locations where the traveling public already purchases gasoline and diesel fuel.  GDOT’s plan was approved on September 14, 2022.  
The key asset that AACE members bring to development of a reliable EV charging infrastructure in Georgia is their existing business locations that the traveling public deems safe and convenient.  Mr. Akers testified that AACE members’ business locations are found in virtually every town in Georgia and in some of the most rural areas of the state at which motorists already obtain convenience, fuel, amenities, the security of on-site personnel and good lighting on a 24/7 basis.   Mr. Akers testified that private enterprise can deploy EV charging stations faster and with better amenities for customers than a regulated utility using ratepayer subsidy. Mr. Akers concluded that the faster the EV charging market can grow through private investment, rather than ratepayer funds, the better.  His testimony on these points was undisputed.
It is also clear that AACE members can provide EV charging services and charge a volumetric rate for the service based on Commission precedent and plain statutory language in the Georgia Territorial Electric Service Act, O.C.G.A. § 46-3-1, et seq.  (the “Territorial Act”).  The  AACE panel (the “Panel”), confirmed that the provision of EV charging services by AACE members does not implicate the Territorial Act based on the Commission’s ruling that the owner of the EV charging station is Georgia Power’s customer and that the retail sale of electricity occurs between Georgia Power and the EV charging provider.  The Panel also cited Madame Chair Pridemore’s presentation before the Joint Study Committee on the Electrification of Transportation (“Joint Committee”) which noted that:
… because EV charging service is not provided to a Premises, but rather, to a transient motor vehicle, EV charging service does not constitute the provision of retail electric service.”  (Emphasis in original)

Not being a retail electric service, EV charging does not fall within the Territorial Act, and ACCE members can provide EV charging services directly to EV drivers. 
The Panel also testified that a volumetric charge from the EV charging provider to the EV based on the kWh of electricity provided by the EV charger to the EV is the best and most appropriate way to compensate the EV charging provider, as is already permitted in over twenty (20) states.  Additionally, the Panel cited the Federal Highway Administration’s (“FHWA”) proposed regulations that require chargers “display and base the price of electrical charge in $/kWh.”
REGULATORY IMPEDIMENTS TO PRIVATE ENTERPRISE EV CHARGING 
 However, there are regulatory impediments impacting AACE members’ ability to provide EV charging services that this Commission can and should address in this docket, among them:
Provision of electricity to EV charging providers pursuant to Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) tariffs that impose unreasonable charges on AACE members to deploy EV chargers and that will give the Company’s EV charging service competitive advantage all while being subsidized by ratepayers; and
Provision of EV charging in competition with the Company on terms and conditions implemented by the Company, that the Company as an incumbent, monopoly electric supplier will not impose on its own EV charging service, giving its EV charging business significant competitive and economic advantages.
The Panel’s testimony addressed both issues, as detailed below: 
A. The CIEV-1 Tariff Rider
Mr. Nelson testified that the Company’s proposed CIEV-1 Rider to its PL tariffs will not encourage EV charging deployment.  The CIEV-1 rider to the Power and Light (PL) tariffs is only available to new premises dedicated to EV charging, served behind a dedicated meter, for service starting on or after January 1, 2023.  To qualify for the Billing Demand Adjustment Factor (BDAF)  set forth in CIEV-1, the customer must have a 12-month load factor of 15% or less, measured by dividing that month’s total energy by that month’s peak demand divided by the hours in the billing month.
PL tariffs include a basic service charge, an energy charge ($/kWh), and a demand charge.  They are applicable to traditional businesses.  The energy charge decreases as consumption increases. The demand charge is calculated using a billing demand based on the highest 30-minute kW measurement during the current month and the preceding 11 months -- referred to as a demand “ratchet.”
Mr. Nelson’s primary concern was that the CIEV-1 rider does not improve upon the underlying PL tariffs, which are themselves not conducive to EV adoption or cost-effective EV charging.  His secondary concern was that the CIEV-1 rider itself will not achieve the intended goal of EV adoption.  The Company’s CIEV-1 rider will be layered on top of existing PL rates which present significant barriers to grid-efficient EV charging.  The combination of CIEV-1 and PL tariffs maintains a ratcheted demand charge that does not time-differentiate energy costs.
Mr. Nelson explained that the Company’s proposed ratcheted demand charge actually hinders EV adoption by locking in a higher bill for charging stations by setting monthly billing demand at the greatest of: 
1) current actual demand, 
2) 95% of the highest actual previous summer demand, or 
3) 60% of the highest actual previous winter demand. 

As a result, an EV charging station under CIEV-1 and PL will likely pay monthly demand charges based on the highest demand over the entire previous year. Highest demand could be set by a rare event occurring when a station with multiple charging ports that usually serve only one customer at a time, unexpectedly must serve three simultaneous charging customers for thirty (30) minutes out of a full year.  Under CIEV-1/PL, the charging station may be billed demand charges based on the abnormally higher demand level resulting from a single, thirty (30) minute event for the following year.  High demand charges make the economics of EV station ownership particularly challenging. At low charger utilization, EV stations are usually unable to collect the amount of revenue necessary from drivers to cover the high demand charges.  Indeed, the demand ratchet is the reason the Company’s CIEV-1 discount on billed demand is necessary in the first place.
Mr. Nelson testified that the demand ratchet itself is inappropriate for EV customers. Its outdated rate design is intended for high load factor customers and does not reflect the costs caused by EV charging load.  At low load factors, EV charging stations do not use generation and transmission capacity the same way that a high load factor customer does, and their spiky load is less likely to occur during the system peak than a high load factor customer’s load.  EV charging stations’ load diversity means that they can more efficiently share system capacity, unlike customers whose load is more consistent across all hours – including during the coincident system peak.  In the above example, an EV charger that experiences abnormally high charging for only 30 minutes out of a full year will be billed the same way as a high load factor customer.    Mr. Nelson testified that many electric suppliers, such as Xcel and Duke have determined that demand ratchets are not an appropriate rate design mechanism and are moving away from them.  
Finally, Mr. Nelson testified that EV charging can provide demand flexibility, avoiding charging during times of day that are costliest on the power system and targeting charging to low-cost hours.  However, the underlying PL rates that the Company proposes to apply to EV charging are not time-differentiated and do not provide sufficiently granular price signals to encourage charging during system surplus and discourage charging during system stress. Because they do not promote load flexibility, the PL tariffs will not encourage EV adoption.
Mr. Nelson’s testimony was largely confirmed by Company witness, Mr. Legg.  On cross-examination, Mr. Legg confirmed: 
peak usage for thirty (30) minute interval could affect the demand charge for the next year;  
PL tariffs are not a time of use rate, and the Company will not charge EV charging providers different energy prices at different times of the day – as is done for residential EV charging;
Tariff charges to EV charging providers under CIEV-1/PL could likely exceed revenues, forcing the private EV charging provider to either not deploy the charger, deploy a charger only in higher volume/usage areas or increase the cost to charge to EV drivers using the charger.
Mr. Legg’s testimony confirmed that the CIEV/PL tariff may adversely impact EV charging deployment by private enterprise.
Mr. Nelson proposed a distinct and default rate (not a rider) for EV charging with the following features:
A time-of-use energy rate with on-peak, mid-peak, and off-peak periods. 
The on-peak period should be narrow (e.g. five hours long, such as from 3pm-8pm.)
There should be a high price differential between the on- and off-peak periods (e.g. 3:1 or 4:1 on:off peak). 
The rates should be seasonally differentiated to reflect the variations in system costs throughout the year.
A demand charge that recovers only distribution costs.
All generation and transmission costs should be recovered through the volumetric time of use (“TOU”) charges, rather than demand charges.
No demand charge ratchet
While this rate should be the default for separately-metered EV customers, Mr. Nelson also encouraged the Company to provide options to customers which would allow a separately metered EV rate or as native load under a pre-existing tariff.  Proposed tariff rates consistent with Mr. Nelson’s recommendations from Alabama Power and Xcel Minnesota are attached as Exhibit “A.”  
The rate structure proposed by Mr. Nelson is both simple and appropriate, particularly at this stage of EV market development.  His simple tariff proposal starkly contrasts the complex and technical tariff rider proposed by the Company. Company witness, Mr. Legg did not agree that a simple tariff would be sufficient to cover the “high demand” of EV charging.  But, Mr. Legg agreed that a tariff imposing high electricity costs that cannot be recovered in revenues can deter the private EV charging provider from deploying the charger.  If Georgia intends to encourage EV deployment, AACE urges the Commission to reject the Company’s CIEV-1 rider to the PL tariffs for EV charging and direct it to file a tariff for EV charging consistent with Alabama Power or Xcel Minnesota tariffs.  The Commission can review the impact of the tariff in the next Rate Case which is only three (3) years away.  
C.  Level Playing Field/Same Terms – Same Conditions
If the Company deploys EV charging stations in competition with private industry, the Commission must establish a level playing field for all providers.  On cross-examination, Company witness, Mr. Legg, confirmed that electricity supplied by the Company to its EV chargers will not be provided pursuant to any tariff.  Instead, the Company will supply electricity to its EV chargers at its cost to provide electricity to itself, and it will not impute the tariff charges (that all other EV providers must pay) to its own EV chargers.  This increases the cost to deploy a privately owned charger and keeps the private provider at competitive disadvantage.  Even Mr. Nelson’s proposed tariffs do not eliminate the cost advantages enjoyed by the Company as the rates set by the tariffs will always exceed the Company’s costs.  
Mr. Selvidge testified that the tariffed rate is not the only cost disadvantage imposed by the Company on private EV Charging providers.  The PL tariffs onto which the CIEV-1 rider is attached also charges customers for several cost riders which private EV chargers will have to pay, but the Company’s chargers will not.  These riders increase the private EV charging provider’s costs significantly.  Combined, the cost increases imposed by the Company’s tariffs and the additional fee riders increase the costs of a private provider to deploy an EV charger by more than $110,000.00 over the ten (10) year life of the EV charger.  This will not produce a level playing field in Georgia for a competitive EV charging market between private EV charging providers and Georgia Power.  Mr. Selvidge testified that cost discrimination between EV charging providers deters development of a robust private sector to meet consumer demand for EV changing.  He urged the Commission to establish a level-playing field now, not ten (10) years down the road.
Mr. Selvidge also noted that the Company can implement operational policies to favor its EV charging business.  In direct testimony, the Company stated that it has created an EV charging team to handle all EV issues.  For example, Georgia Power’s standard Make Ready contract requires the program participant to provide complete EV charger usage information and data to Georgia Power after the EV charger is operable.  No other entity will have access to or be able to use that information which could be competitively valuable to the Company in understanding the developing EV charging market.  
As Mr. Selvidge testified, the best way to insure that all EV charging providers are treated equally is to require the Company to deploy its EV chargers through a separate subsidiary.  Short of that, the Commission should require the Company provide service to private EV charging providers on the “same terms, same conditions” as it provides electricity and services to its own EV chargers, including imputation of the same costs it imposes on private charging providers to its EV chargers. The Commission has consistently imposed structural and non-structural separation and accounting safeguards between the competitive services provided by an entity (such as an incumbent monopoly provider, a municipality or electric membership corporation) that possesses the ability to utilize its position to cross-subsidize its competitive affiliate or gain competitive advantage by favoring its affiliate’s competitive service.  This issue has arisen in the provision of telecommunications, natural gas and broadband deployment.  This Commission has established precedent that it can and should apply to prevent the Company from favoring its EV charging business. 
The Commission must implement a level-playing field now from the very beginning of Georgia’s EV charging market development.  By doing so, Georgia will avoid considerable competitive issues that will inevitably arise if a discriminatory regulatory regime is initially allowed for EV charging.  This Commission has a clean slate from which to work to develop a level playing field now.  The result will be robust, market driven competition to provide ubiquitous EV charging by the Company and private enterprise all across Georgia for the benefit of the traveling public.
In rebuttal, the Company stated that it did not intend to deploy EV charging equipment, except as a provider of last resort in “charging deserts” through its Community Charging program.  If so, the Commission’s Order in this docket should document the Company’s representation and hold it to it. The Commission should require the Company to provide notice of any future change in the Company’s intent and conduct a hearing thereon to implement policies consistent with a level playing field if the Company’s intent changes.
THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED MAKE-READY PROGRAM
The Abramovitz panel and Mr. Legg testified that Georgia Power is requesting $90 million for EV infrastructure deployment, with 90% ($81 million) allocated to the Make Ready program, and the remaining 10% ($9 million) for the Community Charging program.  AACE supports a Make-Ready type program, provided it is based on targeted goals, objectives and criteria.  Mr. Akers’s testimony set forth the criteria that AACE members believe should be required of any Company Make-Ready program. The criteria for the Make-Ready Program proposed by AACE are set out on Exhibit “B.”  The criteria are reasonable and should be required by this Commission for any approval of a Make Ready program, should the Commission decide to approve the amount of the Company’s request or any portion thereof.


THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED COMMUNITY CHARGING PROGRAM
The Company wants $9 million for its Community Charging program in which the Company incurs the cost to install the infrastructure from the customer’s meter to the EV charger, owns, operates and maintains the EV charger and gets the revenue generated from its use.  All costs in the Community Charging program are borne by ratepayers.  The Abramovitz panel testified that the Company will only own and operate Community Chargers with deployment limited to locations at which the Company will be the “provider of last resort.”  Like Make Ready, there are no objective or identifiable criteria to determine the need for a community charger at any proposed Community Charging location. Deployment rests largely within the discretion of the Company.
Hearing evidence did not support the Company’s assertion that its EV chargers deployed in the Community Charging program only serve charging deserts.  In response to STF PIA 1-8, the Company identified approximately 50 chargers at 25 Community Charging sites as of July 2021.  Of these, approximately 90% are fast chargers (45) and 10% (5) are Level 2.  However, investigation of these locations revealed that most of Company’s Community Chargers are actually located along heavily traveled commercial strip thoroughfares, near interstate interchanges, in central downtowns and near college campuses -- areas in which private enterprise will likely deploy an EV charger without ratepayer subsidy.  The Abramovitz panel confirmed the locations of the chargers – virtually none of which are deployed in truly rural locations.  
STF PIA 1-8 also identified costs and revenues associated with the Company’s Community Chargers.  The data showed that the Company’s cost to provide electricity to its EV chargers which are used most by the public significantly exceeded revenues -- even without the Company imputing a tariffed rate that it would charge a private enterprise EV charger and not including the Company’s $70,000 average cost of the EV charger.  This evidence leads to the conclusion that if the Company’s EV chargers identified in STF PIA 1-8 qualify as community chargers, virtually any charger deployed in any location may qualify given the status of EV market development at this time.  Ultimately, it became clear that the Company gave no consideration to the possibility that private enterprise would deploy a charger at Community Charging locations, as the Abramovitz panel testified.
The Panel opposed the Company’s Community Charging program, while acknowledging that a community charger may be needed to serve a specific location that private enterprise cannot.  To serve such a location, the Panel recommended that Community Charging guidelines be developed that include specific objective criteria by which the need for a community charger (placed in service and operated at ratepayer expense) can be identified.  It urged the Commission to again follow telecommunications precedent identified by Mr. Selvidge to establish the need and criteria for a community charger and to give private enterprise the first opportunity to serve the proposed EV charger location through private investment.  A specific definition for “community charger” and proposed criteria for a Community Charging program are attached as Exhibit “C.” If the Commission decides to approve the Company’s request for a Community Charging program, AACE urges the Commission to adopt the definition and criteria shown on Exhibit “C.”
CONCLUSION

Based on the evidence presented in the hearing, AACE requests that the Commission:

Reject the Company’s proposed CIEV-1 rider to the PL tariffs;
Direct the Company to implement a distinct and default tariff rate for EV charging as set forth on Exhibit “A,” attached hereto;
Direct the Company to provide services to private EV charging providers on the same terms, conditions and tariffed rates as it provides to its own EV chargers;
Approve a Make-Ready program based on targeted objectives and criteria approved by the Commission as set forth on Exhibit “B,” attached hereto; and
Reject the Company’s proposed Community Charging program, unless “community charger” is clearly defined to provide service where a private EV charging provider refuses to serve and pursuant to targeted objectives and criteria approved by the Commission as set forth on Exhibit “C.”

EXHIBIT “B” – Make -Ready

Priorities for deployment (based on the proposed location and nature of the site):
1.  Charging infrastructure at public locations (gas stations, downtown areas, shopping malls, community centers, gas stations, grocery stores, etc.) that offer 24/7 amenities, on-site employees, restrooms, food etc.); 
2.  Charging infrastructure serving public transit fleets; 
3.  Charging infrastructure at workplaces and multi-unit dwellings (“MUD”); 
4.  Charging infrastructure serving other non-transit fleets which primarily serve private companies and not the general public but help electrify MHDVs; and 
5.  Within these categories, charging infrastructure sites located in rural and economically challenged communities which are expected to have lower EV adoption rates in the near term

Public transit fleets and public charging sites: 70% of make-ready costs

Other fleets: 30% of make-ready costs
Bonus: 20% of make-ready costs if the EV charging site is located in a rural or economically distressed community.
Procedural requirements/Commission review:
1.  the Commission should review and approve requirements, terms, conditions and contracts used by the Company in the Make Ready program; and
2.   the Commission should review the benefits and costs of the Make Ready program triennially in the Company’s Integrated Resources Plan or Rate Case.

EXHIBIT “C” – Community Charging


Definition of “community charging equipment:”

A “community charging equipment” means electric vehicle charging equipment placed in service at a location which:

fulfills a public policy objective in health, safety, public welfare or responds to a unique transportation necessity;

would not otherwise exist as the result of a competitive marketplace; and

is not located in an urbanized area or urban cluster, as defined by the United States Census Bureau;


Company authority to own/recover costs:

The Company may provide, own, operate and maintain community charging equipment pursuant to approval of the commission, as set forth herein.

Commencing in calendar year 2023 and concluding in 2028, the Company may file a plan with the commission once each calendar year which identifies up to eleven (11) proposed locations at which it desires to install community charging equipment.

The Company’s plan shall include the address or location at which community charging equipment will be installed and state the basis on which the proposed installation satisfies the definition of community charging equipment [definition above].

The Commission shall review the Company’s plan and take comments from interested parties thereon.

Prior to the Commission’s approval of the Company’s plan, an electric vehicle charging provider, other than the Company, may notify the Commission that within two (2) years from the date of submission of the Company’s plan it will install electric vehicle charging equipment within fifteen (15) miles of a proposed location for installation of community charging equipment identified in the Company’s plan, and such location shall be removed therefrom. 

The Commission may approve, deny or modify the Company’s plan.

The Company’s actual costs incurred to own, operate and maintain approved community charging equipment may be recovered in its rates for retail electric service.

The Company shall provide a report to the Commission each year which identifies all community charging equipment, the actual costs incurred in the operation thereof and all revenues received therefrom.

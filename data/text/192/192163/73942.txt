Q.Please summarize your recommendations to the Commission.
A.I recommend that the Commission take the following actions on the Company’s DG proposals and related matters:
Deny the Company’s request to require future residential Schedule RNR customers to take service under Schedule Time of Use – Residential Demand (“Schedule TOU-RD”).
Deny the Company’s request for a monthly netting revenue erosion adjustment.
Reinstate the Schedule RNR monthly netting option with the following guardrails in place: 
Limit participation in the monthly netting program to 3% of the Company’s 2021 peak load;
Revisit and assess the program when participation approaches the 3% cap; and
Require residential customers that enroll in the expanded monthly netting option to pay a minimum monthly bill of $22.34/month, which is equivalent to the residential class customer-related unit cost.
Establish legacy rights for both current and future Schedule RNR systems for a term of 20 years or longer that allow qualifying customers to: (a) remain on the DG tariff option of their choice (e.g., monthly netting); and (b) take supply service under any rate otherwise available to non-DG customers within their rate class.
Establish that legacy rights will not be affected by either: (a) the subsequent addition of battery storage to a system; or (b) system repairs or equipment replacements that increase the generating capacity of the system by no more than the greater of 1 kW or 10% of its originally installed capacity.
II.The Company’s Cost Shifting Argument & Related Proposals
A.Summary of the Company’s Proposals
Q.Please describe the Company’s specific DG-related Proposals and its justifiction for those proposals.
A.The Company makes two specific proposals in its supplemental testimony. First, GPC continues to oppose any expansion of the Schedule RNR monthly netting option beyond the current cap of 5,000 customers or 32 megawatts of capacity, whichever came first. Second, it proposes to require all residential customers that enroll on Schedule RNR on or after January 1, 2023 to take service under Schedule TOU-RD, the residential demand rate. The consequence of these proposals will be that new residential DG customers would be compensated for all instantaneous exports to the grid at the Company’s Solar Avoided Cost Rate (2.89 cents/kWh for 2023) and would no longer have their choice of supply rate from otherwise available residential rate schedules. 
The Company justifies these proposals by pointing to its purported analysis of cost shifting under the Residential Service Schedule (“Schedule R”) and Schedule TOU-RD with both monthly netting and instantaneous export compensation regimes. This analysis calculates forgone Company revenues (less fuel and other variable costs) based on what a customer would pay before installing DG and what they would pay after installing DG under the different DG tariff and supply rate scenarios. Essentially, the Company characterizes all customer cost savings (less fuel and other variable costs) – including customer savings resulting from the customer’s consumption of on-site generated electricity behind the meter – as a cost shift. The Company’s proposed solution to this revenue erosion is to require Schedule RNR customers to take service under a rate, Schedule TOU-RD, that significantly reduces DG customers’ opportunities to save money. 
Q.Has the Company modified its proposal for the $1.4 million revenue erosion adjustment associated with the existing monthly netting option?
A.This element remains unchanged from what the Company proposed in its direct case. In its supplemental testimony, the Company presents a slightly different analysis of potential revenue erosion associated with monthly netting and customer participation on Schedule RNR more generally, but this does not appear to have changed its position on the amount of the proposed revenue adjustment.  
B.Company Framing of Purported Cost Shift
Q.Has the Company’s framing of the question of cost-shifting changed since it filed its Direct testimony?
A.No. The results of the Company’s revenue erosion estimate have changed for several reasons, such as differences in how the Company modeled DG customer bill savings, the inclusion of riders as opposed to just base rates, and the use of a different “value” rate for exports to the grid. These differences aside, the Company continues to inappropriately conflate potential revenue erosion with interclass and intraclass cost shifting. The Company’s request remains based on the idea that it is entitled to a specific amount of revenue towards embedded costs, deriving this amount by estimating what DG customers would have paid in the absence of DG compared to what they would pay after installing DG. 
In its supplemental testimony, the Company digs deeper into this misplaced reasoning by extending its argument beyond potential revenue erosion associated with monthly netting to an alarming perspective that also encompasses all customer savings from DG, including generation produced and consumed directly behind the customer meter. Thus, GPC’s position is effectively this: Any time a customer reduces what they pay the Company, they are engaged in cost shifting.  
Q.Please explain why potential revenue erosion is not equivalent to shifting costs to non-DG customers.
A.At most, potential revenue erosion reflects only an interparty cost shift between DG solar customers and the Company’s shareholders. An interclass or intraclass cost shift does not exist currently. Rather, an interclass or intraclass cost shift will exist only if the Company is permitted to establish its proposed revenue adjustment. Absent that adjustment, all other GPC customers are unaffected by, and indifferent to, the current population of DG solar customers, or future DG customers, as long as the Company’s earnings remain within the earnings band. 
Q.During the November 8–10 hearing, There was some discussion of scenarios under which the Company could experience revenue erosion, and how that potential should be addressed. Could you explain AND RESPOND TO the Company’s position AS YOU UNDERSTAND It?
A.Yes. I understand the Company’s position to be that it is appropriate to adjust its revenues outside of its cost of service study, and outside of the earnings band and revenue sharing mechanism, for variations that are not captured in the forecasted billing determinants which form the basis for calculating the rates necessary to meet the revenue requirement. During cross examination in the November hearing, Company counsel questioned me about examples involving the potential for lower revenues due to customers using less electricity through the installation of DG or other means or by switching to a rate that is less costly for them. The Company’s position appears to be that those possibilities constitute a potential cost similar to “known and measurable changes,” and merit possible adjustments to things like rate base or O&M costs outside of the reference test year. 
I disagree. Revenue variations that arise from variations in load relative to forecasts are distinctly different from cost-related changes because total load variations are inherently not measurable. Variations in load and the accompanying variations in revenue arise due to many factors (e.g., weather, customer accretion or attrition) that are both unpredictable and beyond the control of either customers or the Company. In other words, while certain factors which influence load estimates upon which rates are based can be known, the aggregate impact cannot be reliably measured. The aggregate impact is critically important because it is what defines realized revenue. For example, during a rate year or over the course of a rate planning period, the Company might experience a gross loss in revenue due to customers installing DG or switching to less costly rates, but during the same reference period, it may experience a greater gain in revenue due factors like weather or appliance electrification that contribute to increased customer usage.  The end result is a net revenue increase and higher return on equity (“ROE”) due to factors independent of those within the Company’s control. 
This hypothetical scenario constitutes realized revenue accretion rather than revenue erosion, and there would be no basis for a revenue adjustment that further increases the net revenue gain. That is, revenue erosion or accretion should be evaluated from an aggregate standpoint that implicitly grants equal weight to the multiple contributing factors that may ultimately net positively or negatively. This is precisely the purpose of the earnings band and sharing mechanism, which seeks to balance the interests of shareholders and ratepayers from an aggregate revenue and cost standpoint. A practice of separating out specific line items contravenes this objective and introduces bias towards shareholders into that balance under the circumstances in which the Company typically operates.
Q.Please elaborate on how the company’s proposal introduces “bias” into the earnings band and sharing mechanism.
A.Consider the following hypothetical example: Assume that (a) DG customers collectively avoid paying $1 million in charges that they would have otherwise paid if they had not installed DG, and (b) the Company’s earned return is above the authorized return but below the ceiling under which the sharing of excess revenue would be required, as testimony has previously shown is quite typical. 
In this scenario, the Company’s proposed revenue adjustment would be spread across forecasted billing determinants, effectively creating an incremental charge. For instance, in this hypothetical example, if forecasted sales are 10 billion kWh, the incremental charge is derived by dividing $1 million by 10 billion kWh, resulting in an incremental charge of $0.0001/kWh (0.01 cents/kWh). 
In this reasonable scenario, the Company’s proposed revenue adjustment results in further overearning as the Company is not obligated to return any money to customers. This is what I am referring to as “bias” that benefits shareholders at the expense of customers. Furthermore, the incremental amount of overearning may even be higher than the nominal $1 million revenue adjustment because the overearning may itself be caused by actual sales being higher than forecasted sales. That is, the incremental rate could recover more than $1 million because it is assessed on actual sales amounts which are higher than what was necessary to raise $1 million in incremental revenue. Using the above example, if actual sales were 10.1 billion kWh (1% over forecasted sales), the incremental rate would recover $1.01 million (i.e., the product of 10.1 billion kWh and 0.01 cents/kWh), an additional $10,000. 
Q.Does the Company acknowledge that its evaluation framework is based on retention of utility revenue?
A.It does. In response to a data request about the interaction between the earnings band and revenue sharing mechanism and DG-related revenue erosion, the Company stated:
Any revenue erosion not captured through retail rates, which occurs during the 3-year alternate rate plan will serve to lower the Company’s earned ROE. As a matter of appropriate ratemaking principles, to set retail rates at the correct level to recover the Company’s retail cost of service from customers, any amount of revenues lost from, or avoided by, a certain group of customers must be recovered from the remaining body of customers.

I agree that retail rates should be designed to recover the Company’s costs to serve customers and provide it with a reasonable opportunity to earn an appropriate return. But the Company neglects to mention that recent history indicates that it always overearns relative to its authorized ROE. Regardless of the reasons this has occurred, the existence of these overearnings is undeniable fact. Moreover, if one views the “correct level” of rates necessary to recover the Company’s costs of providing service to be the amounts that produce an earned ROE in line with its authorized ROE, the Company’s proposal for additional cost recovery is in conflict with this objective since it would result in rates that produce more revenue even as the Company is already consistently overearning. It appears that the Company wants to have its cake and eat it too. The sense of entitlement is truly astounding. 
C.Inaccuracies in the Company’s Revenue Erosion & Cost Shifting Analysis
Q.HAVE YOU IDENTIFIED errors in the Company’s COST SHIFT analysis? IF SO, PLeASE EXPLAIN.
A.Yes, I have identified numerous and egregious errors in the Company’s cost shift analysis. As I have already discussed, the Company’s analysis suffers from a foundational flaw in that it conflates the potential for revenue erosion with cost shifting. Setting that framing issue aside, the Company’s modeling of potential revenue erosion associated with residential DG is rife with errors that grossly overstate the magnitude of the “problem,” even if the Commission were to accept the Company’s framing. 
The specific inaccuracies that I have identified stem from two sources: (a) a major formula error in the Company’s modeling of pre-solar bills under Schedule R, which incorrectly inflates the revenue erosion estimate by 63%, and (b) assumptions used in the Company’s solar production modeling. Both cause the Company to overstate potential customer savings, and in turn overstate its revenue erosion estimates. 
Q.Please describe the Formula Error that you identified in the Company’s calculation of Pre-Solar bills Under Schedule R.
A.The Company appears to have inadvertently multiplied the charge for the third tier of summer energy usage (greater than 1,000 kWh) by two in its pre-solar bill estimate. Specifically, the formula contains a syntax mistake that causes the result to be multiplied by two rather than rounded to two decimal points. The erroneous portion of the equation is highlighted in the furthest right portion of the text. I have also attached my workpaper containing a corrected version of the Company’s revised response to STF-TAI-7-1, Attachment B, labeled as Exhibit JRB-4.

Corrected Formula:

 That error dramatically inflates the Company’s DG savings projections, in particular for the high usage customer quartiles used in its analysis. Correcting it reduces the Company’s calculated revenue erosion for Schedule R with instantaneous netting from $1,093/customer per year to $560/customer per year (a reduction of $533/customer per year). The total alleged cost shift is also reduced by $533/customer per year, from $1,356/customer per year to $823/customer per year. These revised amounts are exclusive to this correction. They do not reflect the correction of other problems with the Company’s calculations that I describe below. 
Q.Please describe the Problems you identified in the Company’s solar production modeling.
A.I have identified the following issues with the Company’s PVWatts modeling assumptions:
The Company’s estimates of solar production for a reference system are based on a due south system orientation. In reality, few residential systems are installed with full direct south-facing orientation because they are limited by the orientation of the plane or planes of the rooftop on which they are installed. An average Southwest or Southeast orientation in PVWatts reduces annual production per kilowatt (“kW”) by roughly 4%, while a primarily East or West orientation reduces annual production per kW by approximately 13–14%.
The Company’s estimates of solar production for a reference system are based on first-year production values. Due to normal degradation of panels over time, this overstates average annual system production by 5% over the course of a hypothetical 20-year system lifetime assuming typical production degradation of 0.5% annually. 
The Company also erred by modeling solar systems as “fixed tilt – open rack” systems rather than “roof mount” systems in PVWatts, as would be typical for residential systems, which overstates expected production by a small amount. 
The Company entered inflated system sizes into PVWatts with one decimal place, in each case rounding up to the nearest tenth of a kW compared to the average system size that it calculated elsewhere in its analysis. This produces an overstatement of production up to 50 kWh annually.  
Q.What is the collective effect of the errors that you identified in the Company’s solar modeling?
A.The average annual production from the Company’s modeling is 1,421 kWh per kW-DC. Using a Southwest system orientation (i.e., primarily but not due South) and correcting the system type and system size inputs, the estimated annual production is reduced to 1,366 kWh per kW-DC (a 4% reduction). A due West orientation would reduce estimated production further to 1,240 kWh per kW-DC (a 14.6% reduction). After applying a 5% system degradation adjustment to reflect average annual production over the life of a system, the average annual production estimates for the Southwest- and West-facing systems, respectively, are 1,298 kWh per kW-DC (9.5% reduction) and 1,178 kWh per kW-DC (20.7% reduction). 
Because system production feeds directly into the Company’s calculation of lost revenues, these modeling inaccuracies overstate the Company’s revenue erosion estimates by roughly 10% to 20%. These amounts would be increased by any further losses due to factors like shading that are higher than the PVWatts default assumptions (e.g., 3% for shading).
Q.What are your conclusions regarding the Company’s modeling of potential revenue erosion from residential DG installations?
A.I reject the dubious premise that customer bill savings against embedded costs are equivalent to harmful cost shifting. Such a conclusion can only be reached with a far more extensive analysis that comprehensively identifies the short-term impacts on allocation of those embedded costs, and the long-term avoided costs (i.e., marginal costs) for all aspects of the system. Under the present circumstances, it is clear that there is no cost shift whatsoever because absent the Company’s proposed revenue adjustment, non-DG customers would not be allocated any additional costs or otherwise pay higher rates due to the installation of DG by a subset of customers.   
That issue aside, I have demonstrated that the Company’s estimates of the amount of DG-related revenue erosion cannot be relied upon. To the extent that the Commission gives any weight to the Company’s framing of the matter as it considers DG policies and rates, it should be aware that the Company’s estimates simply cannot be trusted. 
III.Mandatory Service under Schedule TOU-RD
Q.Please describe in general how demand rates tend to impact DG customers.
A.There are two effects. First, demand rates invariably reduce how much money a customer can save by installing DG because solar typically provides little to no reduction in a customer’s non-coincident demand during any given month, and the inclusion of a demand rate component lowers the rates for any volumetric components. 
Second, demand rates in general are advantageous for higher load factor customers, which in the residential context are typically customers with well above average energy consumption. Customers with below average or average usage tend to be disadvantaged by demand rate structures. The former group can be thought of as “structural savers,” whereas the latter group are “structural non-savers.” In other words, holding all other factors constant, any given rate structure has winners and losers relative to another, and demand rates tend to create winners out of high usage customers. It follows then that demand rates tend to disadvantage DG customers because DG lowers the total amount of electricity that a customer consumes from the grid. 
Q.Is there any specific evidence in the record of this case which relates to the impacts of Schedule TOU-RD on relatively lower usage customers?
A.Yes. As I and other intervenor and Staff witnesses demonstrated in direct case testimony filed on October 20, 2022, Schedule TOU-RD resulted in significantly higher rates and significantly higher overall bills relative to other available rates since the beginning of 2021. This coincided with the establishment of Schedule TOU-RD as the default rate for new residential premises, a dramatic increase in Schedule TOU-RD enrollment, and a corresponding significant decline in average usage by Schedule TOU-RD customers. Accordingly, Schedule TOU-RD was generally harmful to those new customers, who happened to also be customers with relatively lower energy usage. I also demonstrated that these same customers who paid more on average on Schedule TOU-RD were less costly to serve according to the metrics used in the Company’s cost of service study. 
Q.How would making Schedule TOU-RD mandatory for new residential DG customers affect those customers?
A.It would reduce customer savings due to DG for all new DG customers, and further reduce total net savings for customers that are already structural non-savers by increasing their pre-solar bill relative to what it would have been under another rate. The combined effects would be greatest on customers with relatively lower pre-solar usage since they are most adversely affected by the demand rate structure itself.

A.The key takeaways are the following. Schedule TOU-RD:
(1) is equally misaligned with cost causation for DG customers as it is for residential customers as a whole, 
(2) would cause net DG savings rates for lower usage customers to approach zero, and potentially be negative for some customers, and

The Company’s proposal makes DG installation totally impractical for its less affluent customers. 
Q.are there any other investor-owned utilities (“IOUs”) in the Country that require residential DG customers to take electric service under a demand rate?
A.I am only aware of one utility that has been permitted to do so (Black Hills Power in Wyoming), despite numerous utility proposals of this type over the last several years. Two other IOUs, Arizona Public Service (“APS”) and Alabama Power Company (“AL Power”), have used demand rate service as a customer option that allows customers to avoid paying an additional fee based on system nameplate capacity, but there are nuances to both of these examples. In the case of APS, a modest solar capacity fee ($0.93/kW) was authorized several years ago but has since been rescinded, allowing residential solar customers to take service under an energy-only time of use rate without any additional charge. AL Power’s solar capacity fee is still in place, but a customer may avoid paying that fee by taking service under the Residential Time Advantage (“RTA”) rate accompanied by paying a higher energy rate during summer peak hours or by participating in AL Power’s Residential Service Demand (“FD-D”) rate. The AL Power RTA rate has separate energy-only and demand rate options, so it is not clear that a customer must take demand rate service in order to avoid the solar capacity fee. Even so, AL Power’s solar rates should not be viewed as a strong example; only a couple of hundred Alabamians have onsite solar.  Moving toward policies like those adopted by Alabama Power would be a step back, not forward. Thus the only definitive example is Black Hills Power in Wyoming. 
Q.Based on the national environment what can the Commission conclude about the general level of acceptability of the Company’s mandatory TOU-RD service proposal?
A.The Commission can conclude that it would be well outside of the norm and contrary to the repeated instances in which regulators in other states have declined to approve such proposals. Approving it would make Georgia a very rare outlier. 
IV.Proposal for schedule RNR Monthly Netting Expansion
Q.How do you recommend the Commission act on the Schedule RNR Monthly netting option?
A.I recommend that the Commission expand the availability of the Schedule RNR monthly netting option with two conditions:
Residential customers under the expanded program should be subject to a minimum bill of $22.34/month, equivalent to $0.7344/day.
The expanded option is available until enrollment in the monthly netting option reaches 3% of the Company’s 2021 peak demand. 
Q.How should the Commission address DG tariff options beyond the 3% of peak demand cap that you propose?
A.The Commission should undertake a more extensive evaluation of DG tariffs in the Company’s next rate case, or at its election, prior to that time (e.g., if enrollment approaches the cap again). In order to support that evaluation, the Commission should direct the Company to immediately begin collecting the data necessary to conduct a robust cost of service analysis of DG customers, including but not limited to the interval data needed to properly characterize DG customer load profiles. 
A.Residential Monthly Minimum Bill
Q.Please describe the basis for the $22.34/month residential minimum bill that you recommend.
A.This amount is based on my calculation of residential class customer-related costs based on the Company’s historic test year cost of service study. As I discussed in my testimony filed October 20, 2022, I calculated this amount without modifying any aspects of the methodology that the Company used in its study (including those which I challenged), so it includes a portion of the costs associated with the shared distribution system (i.e., infrastructure beyond the customer’s service drop) as identified by the Company’s zero-intercept study. 
Q.Why would this amount be appropriate for establishing a minimum bill for residential customers under the expanded Monthly netting option that you recommend?
A.The minimum bill is intended to ensure that DG customers fairly contribute to the recovery of costs that do not vary in relation to their energy use or demand. Although I do not support the use of the zero-intercept method in general as a proper basis for establishing the residential basic service charge (“BSC”), it would not be unreasonable to use it to establish a minimum bill for residential DG customers since it treats a significant portion of the costs of the shared distribution system as unavoidable by distributed generation. 
Q.Are there other state precedents for using a minimum bill design in this fashion?
AYes. Monthly minimum bills specific to DG customers have also been adopted in the Solar Choice net metering successor tariffs now in place in the South Carolina service territories of Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress, and for new net metering customers in El Paso Electric’s Texas service territory. California and Hawaii have established monthly minimum bills higher than the standard fixed monthly BSC for all residential customers, as has Duke Energy Florida.
Q.Are you recommending that the Commission establish a minimum bill for non-residential customers that enroll in the expanded monthly netting tariff?
A.No, for several reasons. First, most monthly netting participants are likely to be residential customers. Second, the Company’s cost of service study only breaks out the customer-related component for the residential class, so it is not possible calculate equivalent minimum bill amounts, and how they compare to the respective fixed BSC, for other customer classes. Third, many non-residential customers pay demand charges anyway, which in most cases would cause their bills to exceed any reasonable minimum bill amount that is based on customer-related costs.  
B.Aggregate Cap of 3% of Peak Load
QHow did you come to 3% of peak demand as your proposed aggregate cap for the expanded monthly netting program?
A.My recommendation is based on consideration of several factors, as follows:
Allowing sufficient headroom to create a reasonable likelihood that the program will not be filled significantly in advance of the Company’s next rate case.
Limiting risk to the Company and its customers and allowing the Commission to monitor all potential impacts from the program in the future. 
Typical caps in other states throughout the country and in the Southeast.
Q.What is a “typical” aggregate cap imposed on net metering programs?
A.At least 25 states have historically made net metering available without any aggregate cap at all. Where caps have been imposed, they have typically been set at a minimum of 1% of a utility’s peak demand, with some states setting them considerably higher in the range of 5% - 10%. Below is a listing of the aggregate caps present in the Southeast in states where net metering is currently authorized. 
Arkansas: Uncapped
Florida: Uncapped
Kentucky: 1%
North Carolina: Uncapped
South Carolina: 2% (traditional net metering); uncapped (Solar Choice monthly net metering)
Virginia: 6% (1% reserved for low-income customers)
West Virginia: 3%
A 3% cap in Georgia would in no way be out of the ordinary, especially considering that several of the previously listed states do not impose a cap on net metering at all. In this regard, 3% could be considered conservative, and I believe that a higher cap could be reasonable. 
Q.How would potential revenue erosion as the result of an expanded monthly netting program with a 3% cap compare to the Company’s historic overearnings?
A.As I previously discussed, the Company has overstated potential revenue losses in its analysis, but even if one accepts its projections in total, the potential loss attributable to monthly netting at full enrollment confined exclusively to residential customers would amount to only roughly $19.7 million/year. By comparison, GASEIA/SEIA/Vote Solar Witness Lucas calculated the Company’s average excess earnings within the ROE band at $135 million/year from 2011 – 2021. The average for 2020 and 2021 was roughly $230 million/year. In an environment of consistent overearning of this magnitude, this modest amount of revenue attrition would in no way compromise the Company’s financial integrity or otherwise induce a hardship.  
Q.How do you respond to Company Witness DR. Gattie’s Contention that Net Metering is no Longer A “preferred Policy Option”?
A.While it is true that some states have transitioned away from annual retail rate net metering, Company Witness Dr. Gattie’s assertion is inconsistent with the fact that some form of net metering remains an option throughout the country in most states. By my count, there are 38 states plus the District of Columbia in which some form of net metering – monthly or annual – is currently available. Moreover, a number of the states that have moved to non-net metering-based successor regimes, such as Arizona and Hawaii, are in no way comparable to Georgia in terms of the relative penetration of rooftop solar, and the “successor” regimes in states that include California, New York, New Hampshire, Nevada, South Carolina, and Vermont are in fact variations on net metering. 
 Georgia can use monthly net metering exactly as these other states have done to provide the Company’s customers with reasonable opportunities to benefit from on-site solar generation and allow the market for these services to grow and mature. And to be clear, unless the Commission grants the Company’s requested revenue erosion rate adjustment, those benefits will accrue at zero cost to non-participants. 
V.Legacy Rights for DG Customers
Q.Please describe what you mean by the term “legacy rights.”
A.I use the term “legacy rights” in a manner synonymous with the term “grandfathering.” Either term refers to a policy that provides a guarantee to customers that they will be permitted to continue taking service under a given tariff structure for a specified period of time, or in perpetuity, even after that tariff or tariff structure is closed to new customers. In the present context, legacy rights would have two aspects, because DG customers effectively take service under two rate schedules. One, they typically take service under Schedule RNR, including the monthly netting option. Two, they take electric supply service under one of the otherwise applicable rate schedules available to all customers within their respective customer class. 
Legacy rights, in typical practice, run with the system rather than with the original installing customer, such that they remain available to a subsequent customer should the user of the system change (e.g., a home with a solar system is sold to a new resident). 
Q.Does the establishment of legacy rights typically have the effect of fixing actual rates for qualifying customers?
A.No. As typically applied, a customer that is granted legacy rights is permitted to continue to take service under an otherwise available rate schedule (e.g., Schedule R), but would be subject to any generalized changes to that rate schedule. In other words, any changes to Schedule R would apply equally to both DG and non-DG customers. An exception to this could apply where a DG tariff is structured with a rate for exports to the grid that is different than what a customer pays for electricity from the grid. Under an “export rate” structure, the export rate itself might be fixed for a specified period of time upon the customer’s enrollment. This is commonly referred to as a “lock-in.”
Q.Pleased describe the policy rationale for establishing legacy rights for DG customers.
A.It is reasonable to assume that a customer considering installing a DG system would, among other considerations, weigh the potential financial benefits of such an investment over its anticipated lifetime against the upfront costs of installing the system. DG systems typically involve a substantial upfront cost, with financial benefits accruing over the life of the system in the form of offsetting electricity purchases from the utility. 
It is also reasonable to assume that a prospective DG customer, like most utility customers, should anticipate changes to certain rate components over time. Customers are accustomed to and generally accept that periodic and typically gradual rate changes will occur. That is, customers have an expectation based on decades of ratemaking that they are likely to experience relatively small rate changes from year to year (i.e., typical increases in existing rate components) rather than dramatic changes in rates or rate structure. This expectation is in large part attributable to the fact that utility regulators have historically made substantial efforts to avoid “rate shock” in ratemaking decisions, consistent with the principle of gradualism.
Q.Is there any corollary to DG customer legacy rights in the realm of utility cost recoverY?
A.Yes. GPC enjoys a type of legacy rights for its own investments. For instance, once a new generation investment has been determined to be prudent, the Commission has not historically taken actions that compromise the recovery of that investment assuming that the Company operated the unit responsibly. Even if an investment becomes uneconomical to operate for its full expected lifetime, the Company would typically still be permitted to recover its full capital costs and potentially a full equity return.  
The Commission’s establishment of the Schedule RNR monthly netting option was akin to a determination of prudency since it signaled to customers that DG might be a reasonable investment to make. After all, in the mind of a prospective DG customer, why would the Commission establish such a program that supports and promotes DG investments and then eliminate it or modify it in such a way that renders those investments severely compromised? Establishing legacy rights for DG customers would treat their investments as equivalent to those the Company makes.
Q.Have other state PUBLIC SERVICE COMMISSIONS addressed Legacy Rights for existing net metering customers?
A.Yes, within the spectrum of recent regulatory decisions affecting net metering and DG customer rates, legacy rights is perhaps the single most consistent element. I have developed a table, attached as Exhibit JRB-5, that provides an overview of how other state utility regulatory commissions have addressed legacy rights for existing DG customers in their consideration of changes to net metering and/or rate structures for those customers. As Exhibit JRB-5 shows, while there are some small differences in how states have approached legacy rights, there are common conclusions as well. The dominant conclusions with respect to legacy rights are as follows:
While certain elements vary from state to state, as a general policy principle, legacy rights have broad support from regulators.
The most common legacy period duration is at least 20 years, ranging upward to indefinite in some states.
Legacy rights eligibility is based on a customer submitting an application either before some future benchmark or date certain, or the date of a decision.
Legacy rights under a modified net metering policy or net metering successor policy tend to mirror the legacy rights period under the original net metering policy, or have slightly shorter time periods, although some states have not yet addressed this.
Q.What are your recommendations to the Commission for the establishment of legacy rights for GPC’s DG customers?
A.I recommend that customers enrolled in the Schedule RNR monthly netting option (and any potential future successor) be granted a legacy rights period of at least 20 years, and that these rights run with the DG system through any changes in ownership. The Commission could reasonably extend that period to the lifetime of the system in order to make it equivalent to the certainty that the Company enjoys for its own investments. Legacy rights should be applied to both: 
(a) the applicable DG service schedule (e.g., the Schedule RNR monthly netting option), and 
(b) the customer’s choice of base rate schedule from any qualifying rate schedules available to non-DG customers. 
Q.Are there any other elements of legacy rights that you recommend the Commission establish? 
A.Yes. Customers should be permitted to: 
(a) add storage to an existing system without jeopardizing the legacy period that applies to that system, and 
(b) make repairs or equipment replacements to an existing legacy system that do not materially change its generating capacity without forfeiting the legacy period that applies to that system. For the purpose of defining a material change in system size, I recommend that the threshold be set at the greater of 1 kW or 10% of original nameplate capacity. 
Q.Please explain why allowing storage additions to legacy systems is justified.
A.The addition of storage does not increase the generating capacity of a DG system. Moreover, battery-paired systems provide additional benefits to the customer, in the form of greater resiliency and potential cost savings, while also making a DG system capable of generating greater value for the broader grid. As it is a win-win for both the customer and the broader grid, existing DG customers should be encouraged to add storage, and part of that encouragement means eliminating potential barriers, such as the forfeiture of legacy rights. 
Q.Please explain why small changes in system size should be permitted with a system’s legacy period.
A.This element is necessary to allow customers to make system repairs or equipment replacements during the system lifetime. It is not uncommon that an equipment replacement (e.g., to an inoperable panel or inverter) cannot be accomplished on a 100% like-for-like basis for a variety of reasons. For instance, the existing equipment model may no longer be in production, or may have changed in terms of dimensions or capacity rating. Allowing a small amount of sizing flexibility permits the customer to select the best replacement option without fear of negative consequences.  
VI.Conclusion
Q.Do you have any closing remarks to make to the Commission on the matters that you have addressed in your Testimony?
A.Yes. Below I have included a set of key facts that I have demonstrated in my analysis of the Company’s proposals and the conclusions the Commission should reach based on this evidence:
Due to the mechanics of the earnings and sharing mechanism and GPC’s pattern of consistent and persistent overearning, non-DG customers are indifferent to further DG deployment – unless the Commission adopts the Company’s proposed DG revenue erosion rate adjustment. Absent approval of this proposed adjustment, they would not be allocated any additional costs or otherwise pay higher rates as a result of residential DG deployment.
The Company’s analysis of the projected amount of revenue erosion associated with residential DG is wholly unreliable due to both a very basic calculation error and numerous inaccurate assumptions incorporated into the modeling. The calculation error alone increases the Company’s revenue erosion estimate by 63% ($533/customer per year) and fully correcting the other flawed assumptions I identified would reduce the estimated revenue erosion by at least 10% more. 
It is undeniable that the Company’s proposal to require customers that enroll on Schedule RNR on or after January 1, 2023 to take electricity service under Schedule TOU-RD will severely reduce residential customer opportunities to deploy DG. All prospective DG customers would see significant declines in their DG savings rate as a result of the Company’s proposals. However, the full impacts of the Company’s proposals on DG opportunities will not be evenly distributed. The customers that would experience the most detrimental impacts are customers with relatively lower pre-solar usage, because for these customers, Schedule TOU-RD is punitive in and of itself.
As I have demonstrated using the Company’s own modeling (with a significant error corrected), the collective effect of Schedule TOU-RD is to reduce net customer savings for lower usage customers to near zero relative to taking Schedule R service and not installing DG. This is represented by a total annual net cost savings of only $50 - $100 for an average size (4.9 kW) solar system depending on whether monthly netting or instantaneous netting is used, equivalent to a DG savings rate of 0.7 – 1.5 cents/kWh. In either case, such low savings will undeniably place DG entirely out of reach for these types of customers, who are likely to skew towards the less affluent end of the spectrum of DG customers. At the same time, the Company’s own modeling illustrates that these customers: 
(a) are likely less costly to serve since their load profile shows peak demands occurring exclusively during off-peak and super off-peak periods, and 
(b) produce less revenue erosion, or cost shift from the Company’s perspective, on an absolute basis ($/customer per year) and on solar capacity basis ($/kW of installed solar per year). 
The Commission can address all of these issues by denying the Company’s mandatory Schedule TOU-RD proposal, adopting my proposal for expanding the monthly netting cap, and denying the Company’s requested DG revenue erosion adjustment. All of these actions can be taken without appreciably affecting the Company’s opportunity to earn a reasonable rate of return on its investments and recover its costs of providing service to customers. 
q.Please provide a summary of your Specific recommendations to the Commission on DG-related issues.
A.I recommend that the Commission take the following actions on the Company’s DG proposals and other matters related to DG policy and rates:
Deny the Company’s request to require future residential Schedule RNR customers to take service under Schedule TOU-RD.
Deny the Company’s request for a monthly netting revenue erosion adjustment.
Expand the cap of the Schedule RNR monthly netting option to 3% of the Company’s 2021 peak load.
Require residential customers that enroll in the expanded monthly netting option to pay a minimum monthly bill of $22.34/month, equivalent to the residential class’s customer-related unit cost.
Establish legacy rights for both current and future Schedule RNR systems for a term of 20 years or longer that allow qualifying customers to: (a) remain on the DG tariff option of their choice (e.g., monthly netting), and (b) take electricity service under any rate otherwise available to non-DG customers within their rate class.
Establish that legacy rights are not affected by either: (a) the subsequent addition of battery storage to a system, or (b) system repairs or equipment replacements that increase the generating capacity of the system by no more than the greater of 1 kW or 10% of its originally installed capacity.

A.Yes.

17   Q.   PLEASE DESCRIBE IN GENERAL HOW DEMAND RATES TEND TO IMPACT

18        DG CUSTOMERS.

19   A.   There are two effects. First, demand rates invariably reduce how much money a customer

20        can save by installing DG because solar typically provides little to no reduction in a

21        customer’s non-coincident demand during any given month, and the inclusion of a demand

22        rate component lowers the rates for any volumetric components.


                       Justin Barnes Supplemental Direct Testimony, on behalf of GIPL
                                          GPSC Docket No. 44280
                                                  Page 14

1                     Second, demand rates in general are advantageous for higher load factor customers,

2            which in the residential context are typically customers with well above average energy

3            consumption. Customers with below average or average usage tend to be disadvantaged by

4            demand rate structures. The former group can be thought of as “structural savers,” whereas

5            the latter group are “structural non-savers.” In other words, holding all other factors

6            constant, any given rate structure has winners and losers relative to another, and demand

7            rates tend to create winners out of high usage customers. It follows then that demand rates

8            tend to disadvantage DG customers because DG lowers the total amount of electricity that

 9           a customer consumes from the grid.

20                      V.      LEGACY RIGHTS FOR DG CUSTOMERS

21   Q.   PLEASE DESCRIBE WHAT YOU MEAN BY THE TERM “LEGACY RIGHTS.”

22   A.   I use the term “legacy rights” in a manner synonymous with the term “grandfathering.”

23        Either term refers to a policy that provides a guarantee to customers that they will be

                     Justin Barnes Supplemental Direct Testimony, on behalf of GIPL
                                        GPSC Docket No. 44280
                                                Page 29

1         permitted to continue taking service under a given tariff structure for a specified period of

2         time, or in perpetuity, even after that tariff or tariff structure is closed to new customers. In

3         the present context, legacy rights would have two aspects, because DG customers

4         effectively take service under two rate schedules. One, they typically take service under

5         Schedule RNR, including the monthly netting option. Two, they take electric supply

6         service under one of the otherwise applicable rate schedules available to all customers

7         within their respective customer class.

 8                Legacy rights, in typical practice, run with the system rather than with the original

 9        installing customer, such that they remain available to a subsequent customer should the

10        user of the system change (e.g., a home with a solar system is sold to a new resident).

11   Q.   DOES THE ESTABLISHMENT OF LEGACY RIGHTS TYPICALLY HAVE THE

12        EFFECT OF FIXING ACTUAL RATES FOR QUALIFYING CUSTOMERS?

13   A.   No. As typically applied, a customer that is granted legacy rights is permitted to continue

14        to take service under an otherwise available rate schedule (e.g., Schedule R), but would be

15        subject to any generalized changes to that rate schedule. In other words, any changes to

16        Schedule R would apply equally to both DG and non-DG customers. An exception to this

17        could apply where a DG tariff is structured with a rate for exports to the grid that is different

18        than what a customer pays for electricity from the grid. Under an “export rate” structure,

19        the export rate itself might be fixed for a specified period of time upon the customer’s

20        enrollment. This is commonly referred to as a “lock-in.”


                      Justin Barnes Supplemental Direct Testimony, on behalf of GIPL
                                         GPSC Docket No. 44280
                                                 Page 30

 1   Q.   PLEASED DESCRIBE THE POLICY RATIONALE FOR ESTABLISHING

 2        LEGACY RIGHTS FOR DG CUSTOMERS.

 3   A.   It is reasonable to assume that a customer considering installing a DG system would,

4         among other considerations, weigh the potential financial benefits of such an investment

5         over its anticipated lifetime against the upfront costs of installing the system. DG systems

6         typically involve a substantial upfront cost, with financial benefits accruing over the life of

7         the system in the form of offsetting electricity purchases from the utility.

 8               It is also reasonable to assume that a prospective DG customer, like most utility

 9        customers, should anticipate changes to certain rate components over time. Customers are

10        accustomed to and generally accept that periodic and typically gradual rate changes will

11        occur. That is, customers have an expectation based on decades of ratemaking that they are

12        likely to experience relatively small rate changes from year to year (i.e., typical increases

13        in existing rate components) rather than dramatic changes in rates or rate structure. This

14        expectation is in large part attributable to the fact that utility regulators have historically

15        made substantial efforts to avoid “rate shock” in ratemaking decisions, consistent with the

16        principle of gradualism.

17   Q.   IS THERE ANY COROLLARY TO DG CUSTOMER LEGACY RIGHTS IN THE

18        REALM OF UTILITY COST RECOVERY?

19   A.   Yes. GPC enjoys a type of legacy rights for its own investments. For instance, once a new

20        generation investment has been determined to be prudent, the Commission has not

21        historically taken actions that compromise the recovery of that investment assuming that

22        the Company operated the unit responsibly. Even if an investment becomes uneconomical


                     Justin Barnes Supplemental Direct Testimony, on behalf of GIPL
                                        GPSC Docket No. 44280
                                                Page 31

 1        to operate for its full expected lifetime, the Company would typically still be permitted to

 2        recover its full capital costs and potentially a full equity return.

 3                The Commission’s establishment of the Schedule RNR monthly netting option was

 4        akin to a determination of prudency since it signaled to customers that DG might be a

 5        reasonable investment to make. After all, in the mind of a prospective DG customer, why

 6        would the Commission establish such a program that supports and promotes DG

 7        investments and then eliminate it or modify it in such a way that renders those investments

 8        severely compromised? Establishing legacy rights for DG customers would treat their

 9        investments as equivalent to those the Company makes.

11        LEGACY RIGHTS FOR EXISTING NET METERING CUSTOMERS?

12   A.   Yes, within the spectrum of recent regulatory decisions affecting net metering and DG

13        customer rates, legacy rights is perhaps the single most consistent element. I have

14        developed a table, attached as Exhibit JRB-5, that provides an overview of how other state

15        utility regulatory commissions have addressed legacy rights for existing DG customers in

17        As Exhibit JRB-5 shows, while there are some small differences in how states have

18        approached legacy rights, there are common conclusions as well. The dominant

19        conclusions with respect to legacy rights are as follows:

20        •   While certain elements vary from state to state, as a general policy principle, legacy

21            rights have broad support from regulators.

22        •   The most common legacy period duration is at least 20 years, ranging upward to

23            indefinite in some states.

 6   Q.   WHAT ARE YOUR RECOMMENDATIONS TO THE COMMISSION FOR THE

 7        ESTABLISHMENT OF LEGACY RIGHTS FOR GPC’S DG CUSTOMERS?

 8   A.   I recommend that customers enrolled in the Schedule RNR monthly netting option (and

 9        any potential future successor) be granted a legacy rights period of at least 20 years, and

10        that these rights run with the DG system through any changes in ownership. The

11        Commission could reasonably extend that period to the lifetime of the system in order to

12        make it equivalent to the certainty that the Company enjoys for its own investments.

13        Legacy rights should be applied to both:

14               (a) the applicable DG service schedule (e.g., the Schedule RNR monthly netting

15               option), and

16               (b) the customer’s choice of base rate schedule from any qualifying rate schedules

17               available to non-DG customers.

19        RECOMMEND THE COMMISSION ESTABLISH?

20   A.   Yes. Customers should be permitted to:

21               (a) add storage to an existing system without jeopardizing the legacy period that

22               applies to that system, and

 1                  (b) make repairs or equipment replacements to an existing legacy system that do

 2                  not materially change its generating capacity without forfeiting the legacy period

 3                  that applies to that system. For the purpose of defining a material change in system

 4                  size, I recommend that the threshold be set at the greater of 1 kW or 10% of original

 6   Q.   PLEASE EXPLAIN WHY ALLOWING STORAGE ADDITIONS TO LEGACY

 7        SYSTEMS IS JUSTIFIED.

 8   A.   The addition of storage does not increase the generating capacity of a DG system.

 9        Moreover, battery-paired systems provide additional benefits to the customer, in the form

10        of greater resiliency and potential cost savings, while also making a DG system capable of

11        generating greater value for the broader grid. As it is a win-win for both the customer and

12        the broader grid, existing DG customers should be encouraged to add storage, and part of

13        that encouragement means eliminating potential barriers, such as the forfeiture of legacy

14        rights.

15   Q.   PLEASE EXPLAIN WHY SMALL CHANGES IN SYSTEM SIZE SHOULD BE

16        PERMITTED WITH A SYSTEM’S LEGACY PERIOD.

17   A.   This element is necessary to allow customers to make system repairs or equipment

18        replacements during the system lifetime. It is not uncommon that an equipment

19        replacement (e.g., to an inoperable panel or inverter) cannot be accomplished on a 100%

20        like-for-like basis for a variety of reasons. For instance, the existing equipment model may

21        no longer be in production, or may have changed in terms of dimensions or capacity rating.

22        Allowing a small amount of sizing flexibility permits the customer to select the best

23        replacement option without fear of negative consequences.

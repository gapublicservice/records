Background
Q. WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF YOUR TESTIMONY? 
A.In the July 29, 2022 Georgia Power Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) Order, the Commission ordered Staff and Georgia Power to address the structure and pricing of the Renewable and Non-Renewable (“RNR”) tariff in the Company’s 2022 Rate Case.  Specifically, the Commission approved the following motion from Commissioner Shaw:
Rather than continuing to try to address rooftop solar with a series of temporary and partial measures where some customers receive net metering while others receive only avoided cost or, in some cases, nothing at all, I move that the Commission direct Staff and the Company to each address the appropriate structure and pricing of the RNR-10 tariff in the current rate case so that the Commission can move towards a fair and long term way of addressing the issue. 

In this IRP, we simply do not have the cost information necessary to set just and reasonable rates, terms, and conditions for rooftop solar.  In addition, we do not have the information to determine whether or how such rates, terms, and conditions would impact other customers. Under my motion, the parties in the rate case would have the opportunity to provide, test, and challenge cost-based data and the Commission would have more options than simply deciding whether or not to continue temporarily doling out net metering to a subset of customers.
Based on the Commission’s decision in the 2022 IRP, a modification to the Procedural and Scheduling Order (“PSO”) for the 2022 Georgia Power Rate Case was approved. The modifications to the 2022 Rate Case PSO included the following dates for the filing of testimony to address the RNR monthly netting issues: October 20, 2022 for the Company to file Supplemental Direct Testimony; November 18, 2022 for other parties to file Supplemental Direct Testimony; and November 23, 2022 for the Company to file Supplemental Rebuttal Testimony.  Our testimony responds to the recommendations provided in the Supplemental Direct Testimony of Company Witnesses Larry Legg and Lee Evans and Dr. David Gattie.

Q.IS GEORGIA POWER REQUIRED TO PURCHASE EXCESS GENERATION FROM CUSTOMER RENEWABLE GENERATOR FACILITIES?
A.Yes.  The Georgia Cogeneration and Distributed Generation Act of 2001 (“Cogen Act”) requires Georgia Power to purchase excess renewable energy from its customers.  The Georgia General Assembly established the Cogen Act to encourage private investment in renewable energy resources, stimulate in-state economic growth, enhance the continued diversification of the state's energy resource mix, and reduce interconnection and administrative costs.  The Cogen Act was a result of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (“FERC”) Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 (“PURPA”) which was implemented to encourage the development of cogeneration and renewable energy facilities in the United States.  The Cogen Act requires electric service providers to purchase renewable power from facilities with a peak generating capacity of not more than 10 kilowatts (“kW”) for residential systems and 100 kW for commercial systems.
Q. PLEASE DESCRIBE GEORGIA POWER’S RNR TARIFF AND THE ACTIONS TAKEN BY THE COMMISSION IN GEORGIA POWER’S 2019 rATE CASE. 
A. Georgia Power developed the RNR tariff to comply with the Cogen Act.  The first RNR tariff was approved by this Commission in August 2002.  Customers on the RNR tariff with either a Renewable Energy Resource or Nonrenewable Energy Resource as defined in the tariff can use such facilities to offset their usage and sell any excess energy back to the Company. 
The RNR tariff was originally offered with only an instantaneous netting compensation option to satisfy the requirements of the Cogen Act. This option, which is now referred to as RNR-Instantaneous Netting, is available to all eligible customers on a first come, first served basis until the cumulative generating capacity of all renewable sources equals to 0.2 percent (0.2%) of the Company’s annual peak demand in the previous year, as dictated by the Cogen Act. 
Under RNR-Instantaneous Netting, all excess energy from a customer’s system is credited to the customer’s bill at the solar avoided energy cost rate based on the Company’s filing in Docket No. 16573.  Also, all of the energy delivered by Georgia Power to the customer’s premises is purchased from the Company at the retail rate.
In Docket No. 42516, in a February 6, 2020 Order Adopting Settlement as Modified, the Commission concluded, after approval of a motion by Commissioner Echols, that the BTM netting period for customers receiving service on the RNR tariff shall be changed from instantaneous to monthly netting for the first 5,000 rooftop solar ratepayers or until new installed capacity reaches 32 megawatts (“MW”), whichever occurs first.  Under monthly netting, a customer’s usage is netted against generation that is supplied into the grid.  In this regard, and due to the R Rate inverted-block rate structure in the summer months and a declining-block rate structure in the non-summer months, the effective value for the netting per kilowatt hour (“kWh”) that an individual customer receives varies depending on the customer’s total net usage and varies across the four summer months and eight non-summer months.

A.On May 13, 2020, the Company filed the RNR-10 tariff to be in compliance with the Order Adopting Settlement as Modified. There was a delay in filing the tariff  due to the Company’s system not being able to implement monthly netting billing.  For customers receiving service on a TOU tariff, monthly netting was delayed until end of year 2020.  However, once a customer became active on the RNR tariff, the customer received the appropriate netting dating back to January 1, 2020.  
At the end of 2019, there were approximately 1,071 customers on the RNR Tariff with approximately 11 MW of capacity according to the Company’s response to STF-DEA-1-3 in the 2022 IRP proceeding and is being provided as Staff Exhibit __ BCW-4. These customers were first to be offered monthly netting once the Company’s billing system was ready. Therefore, the new RNR-Monthly Netting option was available to nearly 4,000 new RNR customers, up to the 32 MW capacity limit. The RNR-10 tariff was approved by the Commission on June 2, 2020.

Q. Why does staff support the continued growth of customer owned generation?  
A. Staff supports the continued growth of customer-owned generation, located behind the customer’s meter, because of its various benefits. First, customer owned generation, primarily rooftop solar, provides customers with the option for reducing their energy bills, supplying their own energy and potentially securing backup power. Secondly, customer owned generation is primarily renewable energy, which contributes to Georgia’s continued transition to clean energy. Finally, customer owned generation reduces the Company’s net loads on the grid, requiring fewer Company-owned assets to be built and paid for by customers.

Q.What Policies have these other state Commissions adopted to address COMPENSATION FOR ENERGY EXPORTED BY ROOFTOP SOLAR SYSTEMS?
A.While each state has enacted its own unique policy, there are two distinct policy categories that have been adopted by other state commissions.  The first category consists of the continuation of NEM with the addition of billing mechanisms such as minimum bills, TOU, or demand rates. South Carolina is one example which requires future net metering customers to be on a TOU rate and imposes a small minimum bill (similar in value to the customer base charge).  Duke Energy in North Carolina has filed a stipulation with other stakeholders which would impose a minimum bill and non-bypassable charges for certain riders (North Carolina Utilities Commission, Docket No. E-100, Sub 180).  Arkansas also chose this structure, allowing customers on demand-based rates to maintain net metering, although non-demand-based customers will also receive net metering through 2022, at which point, utilities may propose an alternate compensation rate, similar to the second category below (Arkansas Public Service Commission, Docket No. 16-027-R).
The other category of NEM policy is what is often referred to as an export rate. An export rate is the same mechanism as Georgia Power’s instantaneous netting policy; however, other states have set different values for their export rate other than the avoided cost. Michigan’s export (or “outflow” as they refer to it) rate is based on the retail rate, with the portion of costs related to the distribution system removed (Michigan Public Service Commission, Case No. U-20836). This mechanism compensates the customer generator for the energy and capacity value of their energy, but does not compensate them for distribution system costs. Utah also provides an export credit rate for solar equal to the avoided energy cost plus total avoided generation, transmission, and distribution capacity costs of 3.53 cents/kWh (Utah PSC October 30, 2020 Order in Docket No. 17-035-61). Mississippi also uses this type of compensation for solar export rates as its rate is based on avoided cost plus a substantial credit for Non-Quantifiable Expected Benefits of 2.5 cents per kWh, and an additional credit for low-income customers of 2 cents per kWh (Mississippi Public Service Commission, Docket No. 2021-AD-19).
Staff’s analysis of RNR monthly netting
Q.IN ITS SUPPLEMENTAL FILING, DID GEORGIA POWER PROVIDE RELEVANT “COST INFORMATION NECESSARY TO SET JUST AND REASONABLE RATES, TERMS, AND CONDITIONS FOR ROOFTOP SOLAR?”
A.No.  The Company simply conducted an analysis of the revenue erosion it confronts as a result of: (a) customers reducing their energy requirements simply due to supplemental self-generation; and (b) the netting value associated with RNR Monthly Netting.
Q.IN ITS SUPPLEMENTAL DIRECT TESTIMONY, DID GEORGIA POWER PROVIDE “INFORMATION TO DETERMINE WHETHER OR HOW SUCH RATES, TERMS, AND CONDITIONS WOULD IMPACT OTHER CUSTOMERS?”
A.To some extent, yes.  That is, and as mentioned above, the analyses conducted by Company Witnesses Legg and Evans provides an estimate of the residential revenue erosion attributable to rooftop solar.  As a result, the Company asserts that this reduced revenue must be made up by all other residential customers in order for the Company to achieve its authorized residential revenue requirement.  In other words, the Company’s total cost of service remains fixed (for all intents and purposes), but with rooftop solar there is less kilowatt hours of consumption, which translates into less revenue.  Therefore, the reduction in revenue must be made up by all non-solar customers in order for the Company to recover its total costs.

Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN THE $1,093 COMPONENT WITHIN COMPANY WITNESS LEGG’S ANALYSIS.
A.The $1,093 revenue erosion is simply the reduction in revenue as a result of a customer self-generating some of their energy requirements.  To more fully explain, the Company first approximated the net kWh consumed and delivered for average rooftop solar customers based on 12 months of billing data.  The Company then estimated the kWh produced by the typical customer’s rooftop solar, which then allowed them to estimate the average energy requirements of a rooftop solar customer if they did not have rooftop solar installed.  The Company then priced these estimated monthly usages to determine what a customer’s bill would have been (net of fuel and MFF) if they did not have a solar system installed.  The difference between what a customer’s bill would be without rooftop solar and an estimate of that customer’s bill due to reduced kWh purchased from Georgia Power is $1,093 per customer per year. 
In short, the $1,093 average annual revenue erosion per customer is simply the reduced revenue due to a customer using less energy provided by Georgia Power due to that customer’s supplemental self-generation.  To be clear, this amount has nothing to do with monthly netting.  It is simply reduced revenue attributable to a customer purchasing fewer kWh from Georgia Power.  As an analogy, if a customer replaced its heat pump with a highly efficient unit, this $1,093 component relates to the reduced revenue due to a customer purchasing less energy than before. 
Q.IS IT APPROPRIATE TO CONSIDER THE REDUCED REVENUE SIMPLY DUE TO A CUSTOMER USING LESS ENERGY BECAUSE THEY SELF-GENERATE SOME OF THEIR ENERGY NEEDS?
A.No.  A customer is, and should be, able to use any legal means available to reduce their energy purchased from Georgia Power.  This would include energy conservation measures as well as supplemental self-generation.  Therefore, Mr. Legg’s $1,093 revenue erosion component should not be considered.          
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN THE $284 REVENUE EROSION COMPONENT WITHIN COMPANY WITNESS LEGG’S ANALYSIS.
A.The $284 revenue erosion reflects the difference between the customer’s annual monthly netting bill and the customer’s annual bill under instantaneous netting.  
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN THE NEGATIVE $21 COMPONENT WITHIN COMPANY WITNESS LEGG’S ANALYSIS.
A.This amount reflects what Mr. Legg refers to as the avoided “production energy-related costs per kWh” of 0.207¢.  Because the revenue erosion components discussed above are net of fuel, this 0.207¢ per kWh reflects the avoided non-fuel production cost.  
Q.DOES STAFF AGREE WITH THE ASSUMPTIONS AND MODELING COMPANY WITNESS LEGG USED WITHIN HIS ANALYSIS?
A.No.  Mr. Legg’s analysis is based on a sample of 1,110 residential monthly netting customers.  While Staff has no objection to the use of a sample, Staff believes the assumptions used by the Company to estimate the lost revenue from RNR customers overinflate the bill savings that RNR customers receive primarily by overestimating the production by customer facilities. However, more concerning is the lack of validation of the Company’s methodology using historical data for estimating RNR customer solar production and the customer’s consumption before the installation of their solar system.
Primarily, Staff has concerns about the Company’s use of the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s PVWatts Calculator for solar production estimations. According to the Company’s response to STF-TAI-8-6, the Company used all the default inputs for the PVWatts Calculator tool, except for a geographic location, which was set at one of four locations in Georgia. The default options for the PVWatts Calculator assume a ground mounted solar array with ideal south-facing orientation, no shading as a result of nearby trees or structures, and no future system degradation. All of these factors provide a best-case scenario for the first year of solar production and will lead to a significant overestimate of the average rooftop solar system production, which will only increase the overestimate as these systems degrade over time. 
The Company has not provided or utilized any historical data from existing RNR customers in order to validate the method it used to estimate RNR lost revenue. Without a benchmark to historical data, we cannot ensure the accuracy of the Company’s estimates of customer usage prior to solar installation, or the appropriateness of the chosen customer load profiles for representing solar customers. The Company should have extensive hourly data provided by its Advanced Metering Infrastructure (“AMI”) system as well as historical hourly consumption for most, if not all, of the RNR customers. While the Company has indicated that its historical data was inadequate to conduct its analysis (Response to STF-TAI-8-7 part a), there appears that there was no attempt by the Company to use even a subset of historical data to validate the accuracy of the methodologies used by the Company for its analysis.
Q.EARLIER YOU INDICATED THAT, TO SOME EXTENT, THE COMPANY PROVIDED INFORMATION TO DETERMINE WHETHER OR HOW SUCH RATES, TERMS, AND CONDITIONS WOULD IMPACT OTHER CUSTOMERS.  PLEASE ELABORATE ON THIS OBSERVATION.
A.Company Witness Legg estimates that an average RNR customer receives $284 per year more under RNR-Monthly Netting than that customer would receive under RNR Instantaneous Netting.  He then multiplied this $284 by an assumed level of 5,000 RNR customers to arrive at a total revenue erosion of $1,418,434 per year.  The current residential revenue excluding fuel is $2,935,169,922.  Therefore, the residential revenue erosion attributable to monthly netting is 0.00048 (0.048%).  To be clear, the monthly netting impact on residential customers is less than five one-hundredths of one percent.  By any measure, this is de minimis.   
Q.DURING CROSS-EXAMINATION, COMPANY WITNESS LEGG ACKNOWLEDGED THAT THE REVENUE EROSION DUE TO MONTHLY NETTING CURRENTLY REPRESENTS A VERY SMALL PERCENTAGE OF RESIDENTIAL REVENUE AND THAT THIS PERCENTAGE IS BASED ON 5,000 CUSTOMERS.  HOWEVER, MR. LEGG IS CONCERNED THAT ROOFTOP SOLAR WILL CONTINUE TO GROW SUCH THAT THE PERCENTAGE WILL INCREASE IN THE FUTURE.  IF RNR MONTHLY NETTING WERE TO INCREASE BY A FACTOR OF TEN, WOULD THIS HAVE A MATERIAL IMPACT ON THE REVENUE COLLECTED FROM THE RESIDENTIAL CLASS?
A.No.  Even if RNR Monthly Netting were to expand by a factor of ten in the foreseeable future, the revenue erosion associated with the program would represent approximately one half of one percent (0.5%) of the Company’s total residential revenue.  At the same time, it must be remembered that RNR customers receive monthly netting due to excess generation that provides value to the Company and other ratepayers.       
Q.DOES STAFF FIND IT INCONSISTENT THAT THE COMPANY IS CONCERNED ABOUT A REVENUE EROSION OF LESS THAN FIVE ONE-HUNDREDTHS OF ONE PERCENT, WHICH IT REFERS TO AS A “COST SHIFT”?
A.Yes.  While the Company is concerned with the residential revenue erosion of less than five one-hundredths of one percent due to monthly netting, it is not at all concerned that some classes and rate schedules are significantly over-contributing to costs while other classes and rate schedules are significantly under-contributing to costs. The Company’s proposal in this case is to simply increase all rates by an equal percentage such that there is no movement towards parity of cost of service across classes and rate schedules.  This decision, of course, results in significant cost shifting across classes and individual rate schedules.  
Q.HAS STAFF CONDUCTED ANALYSES UTILIZING ACTUAL DATA ASSOCIATED WITH GEORGIA POWER’S RNR MONTHLY NETTING CUSTOMERS?

This data included (on an individual account and monthly basis) delivered kWh, excess generation kWh, and net kWh (delivered minus excess generation). The Company could not identify eight customers that were included in its sample that were also provided in response to STF-TAI-4-1.  As such, Staff’s sample is based on 1,102 customers (1,110 minus 8).

Finally, Staff calculated the average R Rate customers’ monthly bill separately for the summer and non-summer periods. Q.WHY IS STAFF’S ANALYSIS AND FINDING THAT RNR MONTHLY NETTING CUSTOMERS CONTRIBUTE A SIGNIFICANT LEVEL OF REVENUE TO THE COMPANY PARTICULARLY RELEVANT?    
A.The ratemaking process is the result of averaging.  From a theoretical standpoint, the cost to provide service varies for every single customer.  As examples, due to the amount of distribution equipment required to serve sparsely populated areas, it may cost more on a per kW or kWh basis to serve rural customers relative to suburban or urban customers.  Similarly, because there are fewer kWh to recover revenue from residential customers that use natural gas for heating, it may cost more on a per kW or kWh basis to serve these customers relative to customers that use electricity for both heating and cooling.  However, within the ratemaking calculus, we do not distinguish between these two examples or any other criteria that affects individual costs.  As such, the average monthly revenue contributed by a monthly netting customer of $112.82 ($1,354 per year) is significant.

Q.HAS STAFF CONDUCTED ANALYSES OF POSSIBLE ALTERNATIVE TO VALUE THE EXCESS GENERATION  EXPORTED TO THE GRID?
A.Yes.  The valuation of excess generation supplied to the grid is a policy decision for the Commission.  As such, Staff is providing a range of possible of values for the excess solar energy that is supplied to the grid.  
As mentioned earlier, under the current monthly netting mechanism, the value of solar energy supplied to the grid varies by not only season but also varies depending on a customer’s total delivered kWh.  Due to the inverted-block rate structure in the four summer months, a larger usage customer receives a higher monthly netting value per kWh of solar energy supplied to the grid than a smaller usage customer that supplies solar energy to the grid.  For example, under current rates, a large usage customer that has a monthly delivered kWh of 1,200 kWh will receive monthly netting value of 15.68¢ per kWh while a customer with monthly delivered kWh of 650 kWh will only receive a monthly netting value of 10.36¢ per kWh.  
Similarly, due to the declining-block rate structure in the eight non-summer months, a larger usage customer receives a lower monthly netting value per kWh of energy supplied to the grid than a smaller usage customer.  For example, a large usage customer that has monthly delivered kWh of 1,200 kWh will receive a monthly netting value of 9.12¢ per kWh while a customer with monthly delivered kWh of 650 kWh will receive a higher monthly netting value of 10.29¢ per kWh.  Staff is of the opinion that the value of excess solar generation supplied to the grid is the same per kWh for both a large usage customer and for a small usage customer.       
Based on the 2021 usage characteristics of the sample, Staff has calculated that the annual weighted average solar netting for monthly netting customers is about 10.22¢ per kWh (9.01¢ in the eight non-summer months and 12.94¢ in the four summer months).  
In evaluating alternative ways to value solar energy exported to the grid, Staff first considered the capital costs and attendant implicit revenue requirement per kWh associated with Georgia Power’s owned solar facilities that are included in rate base.  Georgia Power’s customers are paying for the value of Company-owned solar facilities such that a surrogate for the value of rooftop solar pumped into the grid can be thought of as the same as the value of Company-owned solar.  
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN STAFF’S ANALYSIS OF THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT PER KWH ASSOCIATED WITH GEORGIA POWER’S SOLAR FACILITIES.
A.In response to STF-TAI-1-5, the Company provided an itemization of each solar facility’s net plant, depreciation, and kWh output during the forecasted test year of 2023.  As a result, Staff was able to calculate the fixed (capital) cost revenue requirement per kWh of the Company’s solar facilities.  This analysis is provided in Trade Secret Staff Exhibit __ BCW-11.  As indicated in this Exhibit, ratepayers will pay 8.336¢ per kWh for Georgia Power solar energy utilizing the Company’s proposed ROE of 11.00% and 7.353¢ per kWh utilizing Staff’s proposed ROE of 9.45%.  In developing these estimates, Staff has reflected a 2.6¢ Production Tax Credit per kWh consistent with the recently enacted IRA.  Furthermore, Staff’s estimate does not incorporate or reflect any maintenance costs associated with the Company’s solar facilities.  
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN THE SECOND STUDY STAFF CONDUCTED REGARDING A POSSIBLE ALTERNATIVE VALUATION FOR SOLAR ENERGY EXPORTED TO THE GRID.
A.The second study Staff conducted is based on the recent net metering compensation method approved by the Michigan Public Service Commission for DTE Energy (formerly Detroit Edison).  The theory behind this analysis is that excess generation supplied to the grid by rooftop solar customers relies on the Company’s distribution system to supply its energy to the grid.  Therefore, the value (compensation for excess generation) of solar energy supplied to the grid is equal to the cost of generation related and transmission related costs.  
Staff conducted a study based on the Company’s class cost of service study in which the residential direct and indirect distribution costs (rate base and expenses) were subtracted from the total bundled revenues such that the allocated amounts for only generation and transmission were considered.  This study resulted in a possible monthly netting compensation rate of 6.99¢ per kWh at current rates. For illustrative purposes, if the Commission authorized half of the Company requested base rate increase this amount would increase to 7.63¢.  The details of this study are provided in Staff Exhibit __ BCW-12.    
Q.WHY IS IT CORRECT TO INCLUDE THE COST OF TRANSMISSION RELATED COSTS IN THE ABOVE ANALYSIS?
A.Because electricity takes the path of least resistance, any excess generation supplied to the grid will remain within Georgia Power’s distribution system.  As a result, this supplied energy does not rely upon the Company’s transmission system, and can therefore, be thought of as avoiding additional transmission capacity and costs in a long-run concept.

Q.DOES STAFF RECOMMEND THAT RNR CUSTOMERS After JANuary 1, 2023 BE REQUIRED TO take service ON THE TOU-RD RATE?
A.No. Due to its structure, with a high demand charge, and extremely low off-peak energy price, TOU-RD is not well-suited for RNR customers.  Under the current TOU-RD rate structure, there are only about 511 on-peak hours in the year and about 8,249 off-peak hours during the year.  First, consider the scenario of the continuation of  monthly netting. The customer would only receive a netting value of about 1.0¢ per kWh, less than half of the current solar avoided cost rate of 2.68¢ per kWh during all off-peak daylight hours.  Second, consider the scenario of continuing instantaneous netting.  During off-peak hours, the customer would pay about 1.0¢ per kWh for delivered energy but receive a netting value of about 2.68¢ per kWh for excess energy supplied to the grid.  
Q.IS IT TRUE THAT A CUSTOMER ON RNR WILL PLACE HIGHER DEMANDS ON THE SYSTEM DURING NON-DAYLIGHT HOURS? If so, does this justify putting RNR customers on TOU-RD?
A.While this is true that RNR customers are likely to have a higher demand in non-daylight hours than daylight hours, it is important to understand when this customer’s peak demand is likely to occur relative to the system load.  As shown in PIA Staff’s Direct Testimony of Jamie Barber, Benjamin Deitchman, and Glenn A. Watkins Staff Exhibit __ BDW-12, the residential class tends to peak between 6:00 p.m. and 7:00 p.m.  The system tends to peak between 4:00 p.m. and 5:00 p.m. wherein the system load declines from there.  During the summer, there is still significant daylight such that a solar net metering customer will likely peak between 8:00 p.m. and 9:00 p.m.  By this time, the system load has declined significantly from its peak that occurred earlier in the evening.           
Q.IN ITS SUPPLEMENTAL DIRECT TESTIMONY, Does the Company outline other policy issues that remain unresolved regarding current RNR customers?
A.Yes.  The Company poses two questions for the Commission to address.  First, the Company asked whether and for how long existing RNR customers should be allowed to remain receiving service and not be on a three-part rate. The second issue is whether and for how long customers on monthly netting should be allowed to receive monthly netting. In its testimony, the Company suggests that the Commission could consider allowing the 5,000 monthly netting pilot customers to continue monthly netting for a time period sufficient to recover their investment in their BTM generation system (Supplemental Direct Testimony of Lee Evans and Larry Legg, pg. 15, line 15-18).
Q.REGARDING HOW LONG CURRENT RNR MONTHLY NETTING CUSTOMERS SHOULD BE ALLOWED TO REMAIN ON THE SYSTEM WITHOUT RECEIVING SERVICE ON A THREE PART RATE, DOES STAFF HAVE A RECOMMENDATION?
A.Yes. Staff recommends that neither current or future RNR customers be required to move to a three part or demand based rate. 
Q.REGARDING HOW LONG CURRENT RNR MONTHLY NETTING CUSTOMERS SHOULD BE ALLOWED TO CONTINUE RECEIVING MONTHLY NETTING, DOES staff HAVE A POSITION ON THAT ISSUE?
A.Yes. Staff recommends  that if the Commission, in this case, does not continue with RNR Monthly Netting, that customers currently receiving monthly netting should be grandfathered and allowed to receive monthly netting for at least 15 years.  
As stated on Page 63 of NARUC’s Distributed Energy Resources RATE DESIGN AND COMPENSATION Manual:
The primary argument supporting shielding current DER customers from a change in rates/policy (possibly due to meeting a regulatory or statutory threshold) is that customers desire and expect some level of certainty when making decisions about their individual investments in DER. While individual investment decisions are personal, a regulator should consider whether the policies of the jurisdiction require or desire using rate making as a policy and technology support tool.

By grandfathering current RNR Monthly Netting customers for at least 15 years, the extension will allow  customers to receive a  payback for their solar investment as these customers installed their rooftop solar system with the expectation to be able to continue to receive monthly netting. Without the continuation of monthly netting, these customers would be harmed financially as it will take longer to recover a return on their rooftop solar investment.
Q.DID THE NARUC DER RATE DESIGN AND COMPENSATION MANUAL PROVIDE EXAMPLES OF OTHER STATES THAT ALLOWED GRANDFATHERING FOR EXISTING ROOFTOP SOLAR CUSTOMERS?
A.Yes. On page 64 of the NARUC DER Rate Design and Compensation Manual, the following examples were provided:
California implemented grandfathering for a 20-year period for both NEM 1.0 customers and new customers taking service under the NEM 2.0 tariff.
Kansas grandfathered customers that began operating a renewable energy resource before July 1, 2014, for 15 years until December 31, 2029.
Nevada agreed to a 20-year grandfathering period for customers that had installed or had active applications before December 31, 2015. 
Q.You previously mentioned that the 0.2% threshold would be reached very soon, what implicationS could that have if the commission sets a credit for excess generation as opposed to Continuing monthly netting?
A.Unlike monthly netting where the Company is not purchasing the customer’s excess generation, the Company’s obligation to purchase excess generation may end when the 0.2% of the Company’s prior year annual peak demand is reached.  This could mean that customers who install a rooftop system after the limit is reached would receive no value for their excess energy.

Q.Is staff Aware of any consumer RELATED issues regarding THE INCREASED INSTALLATION OF rooftop solar in georgia?
A.Yes.  As noted in the Company’s Supplemental Direct Testimony of Legg and Evans, p. 16 lines 13-15, Staff is aware of a growing segment of dissatisfied solar customers. The Commission has received complaints from customers who are in dispute with their rooftop solar installer as a result of delays, improper installation, or unmet expectations. Staff believes that these issues arise from both unknowledgeable and disreputable installers.
Staff is particularly concerned about the increase in inaccurate information that is being provided to customers which gives these customers unrealistic expectations regarding savings associated with the installation of a system or in some cases that the customer will no longer have an electric bill. Specific items of concern that Staff has discussed with customers include: the oversizing of the solar system when compared to the customer’s load, overly optimistic payback periods, promises of monthly netting although the program cap had been reached, and the apparent lack of understanding by the solar installer of the Company’s rates and how customer bills are calculated.
Q.DID THE Company PROVIDE A PROPOSAL to ADDRESS THE CONSUMER ISSUES RELATING TO ROOFTOP SOLAR?
A.Yes. In its Supplemental Direct Testimony of Witnesses Legg and Evans, the Company proposed the development of a formal framework for Commission referral of consumer complaints to the Consumer Protection Division of the Attorney General’s Office.
Q.DO YOU KNOW HOW THIS FORMAL PROCESS WOULD WORK?
A.No. During the recent hearing on the Company’s Supplemental Direct Testimony, Witness Legg stated that “we don’t have any particular idea of what exactly that would look like. We would be open to meeting with the Commission and Commission Staff to discuss any formalization of that… We want to make sure a robust process is in place for those complaints to be followed up on”.  However, Witness Legg did offer that this could include “keeping a tracked list and following up to see what happens with those [complaints]”. 
Q.CURRENTLY WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE COMMISSION RECEIVES A COMPLAINT FROM A CONSUMER REGARDING THEIR ROOFTOP SOLAR INSTALLATION?
A.Currently, if a complaint is received from a consumer regarding a solar rooftop system and the issue is not directly related to Georgia Power, the customer’s bill or the RNR tariff, the Commission’s Consumer Affairs Staff sends the complaint to the Consumer Protection Division of the Attorney General’s Office. 
Q.HOW MANY COMPLAINTS HAS THE COMMISSION RECEIVED FROM CONSUMERS WITH ROOFTOP SOLAR SYSTEMS?
A.Complaints from customers that are not directly related to Georgia Power are not entered into the Commission’s database; however, Staff was told by the Consumer Affairs Manager that 24 complaints in total have been referred to the Georgia Attorney General’s Office.
Q.DOES STAFF SUPPORT THE COMPANY’S RECOMMENDATION FOR A MORE FORMAL FRAMEWORK OF REFERRING COMPLAINTS TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S OFFICE?
A.Staff agrees that a meeting between the Commission, Georgia Power and the Consumer Affairs Division of the Georgia Attorney General’s Office could be beneficial. While the Commission has not been tasked with regulating the rooftop solar industry, it can assist with consumer complaints received by the Commission by ensuring that consumers are provided the appropriate information necessary to file a complaint with the Attorney General’s Consumer Affairs Division office. That said, the Commission already refers consumers who contact the Commission to the Consumer Affairs Division of the Georgia Attorney General’s Office.  So, it may be fruitful for the Staff and the Company to continue to explore additional ways to protect consumers.

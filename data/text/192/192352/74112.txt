BRIEF AND PROPOSED MOTIONS OF THE
SOUTHERN RENEWABLE ENERGY ASSOCIATION

Pursuant to Rule 515-2-1-.04 of the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission” or “PSC”) and the August 5, 2022 Procedural and Scheduling Order and subsequent Orders in this matter, the Southern Renewable Energy Association (“SREA”) respectfully files this brief to address issues in Georgia Power Company’s (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) 2022 Rate Case and the hearings that followed.
SREA PROPOSED MOTIONS FOR COMMISSION CONSIDERATION
MOTION: “The Commission will hire an independent transmission consultant to provide a report by July 1, 2024 that evaluates multiple scenarios of generation additions and retirements and measures multiple reliability, economic, and other quantifiable benefits associated with specific transmission project recommendations. The Company will work with the Commission, Staff, stakeholders, and the hired consultant to provide the data and any other information necessary to complete the report.”
MOTION: “The Commission will open a new docket to evaluate the current transmission planning processes and evaluate recommendations for reform and improvements.”  

ARGUMENTS
Georgia has been a leader in solar development and economic growth over the past decade. Transmission investment is vitally important to ensure Georgia continues its record of success; however, investing in the correct type of transmission investment is becoming more and more difficult for the Company to identify, and for the Commission to approve. In many ways, this rate case is predominately a case about transmission investment – which investments to make, when, where, and at what cost. While the Company has failed to provide a compelling case for its specific transmission plans, the Commission Staff have similarly failed to provide robust alternatives to the Company’s proposals. Without robust transmission planning alternatives, the Commission’s decision making is limited to information provided by the Company. We recommend that the Commission hire additional expertise to develop independent transmission plans that may help inform future decisions. Further, we recommend that the Commission open a new docket on transmission to identify ways to improve transmission planning in Georgia. Without significant improvements to Georgia’s transmission planning process, the grid will not be ready to support renewable energy and Georgia’s burgeoning economy. 
Transmission Improvements are Necessary
In this rate case, the Company presents its Grid Investment Plan (GIP) as a program to replace “aging transmission lines and substations”. As a program that began in 2019, the Company notes that no changes have been made to the GIP, stating, “The Grid Investment Plan objectives remain unchanged: distribution investments focusing on improving reliability for customers and transmission investments focusing on replacing aging system assets.” The Company mentions that it “must also make transmission and distribution investments to support the transition of the generation fleet to more economical and cleaner resources”.  While SREA agrees that transmission investments and better planning are necessary, the Company provided no analysis showing that the transmission plans in this case will actually enable more economic and cleaner resources. 
At least two major transmission concerns exist in Georgia: transmission necessary to retire existing uneconomic coal-fired generation units, and transmission necessary to integrate new low-cost renewable energy. Through the discovery process in this rate case, the Commission Staff alluded to these two issues when asking the Company about transmission constraints that arose in the 2022 Integrated Resource Plan from earlier this year. Staff asked:
Are any of the transmission projects contemplated in the Company's North Georgia Reliability and Resilience Action Plan being requested for rate base treatment in the Company's 2022 Rate Case? 

Are any of the transmission projects that the Company is requesting to rate base in the Company's 2022 Rate Case being proposed to provide transmission pathways for renewable projects in south Georgia to meet customer load requirements in north Georgia? 


The North Georgia Reliability and Resilience Action Plan is included in the 2022 IRP and is associated with the retirement of the coal-fired Plant Bowen. The Action Plan calls for “the development of a strategic portfolio of projects to address the long-term transmission planning and operational needs of north Georgia, and the development of an integrated generation and transmission expansion plan aimed at meeting the long-term needs of the north Georgia area.”  To properly close Plant Bowen, transmission in north Georgia needs to be built. The Company also initially proposed a renewable request for proposals focused on northern Georgia; however, the Georgia PSC removed that request after noting that most renewable projects are developing in southern Georgia. The Company noted that it “…will continue to coordinate with other in-state utilities and ITS Participants to prepare for the possible addition of renewable resources in south Georgia.” Notably absent from this coordination is the Commission, Commission Staff, and the renewable development community. Additional transmission from south Georgia to north Georgia likely will be necessary to integrate significant quantities of renewable resources. 
There have been no public updates regarding the status of the North Georgia Reliability and Resilience Action Plan after the IRP, and none in this rate case. As a response to the Commission Staff’s questions, the Company responded, “No. The transmission projects associated with the North Georgia Reliability and Resilience Action Plan are currently under study and development in conjunction with the Integrated Transmission System Participants. As agreed in the Stipulation approved by the Commission in Docket No. 44160, the Company will meet with PIA Staff on an annual basis to update and report the status of these projects.” Neither of these major transmission concerns are being addressed in this rate case, yet the Company is requesting significant transmission cost recovery over the next three years. Significant transmission constraints exist, but are not being resolved in this rate case. 

Current Transmission Planning is Not Adequate 
The current process for distribution and transmission planning and rate recovery is inadequate for long term planning purposes and needs revision. Several themes during the rate case highlight the need for transmission planning reform including high costs, a lack of benefits for ratepayers, the unwillingness or inability to evaluate benefits or alternatives, and a lack of solutions for ongoing transmission problems. 
Proposed Grid Investment Plan Costs are High
Commission Staff witness Raj Rana notes in his testimony that the Company’s program is a “massive” $6.8 billion from 2023-2025. This “whopping increase” is 164 percent higher than previous years, and represents “$7.72 of the proposed rate increase of $16.29 per month.” Staff witnesses Stephen Alvarez and Dennis Stephens note that in the 2019 rate case, $2.6 billion of the GIP was dedicated to the Transmission Investment Plan (TIP) while $4.9 billion was dedicated to the Distribution Investment Plan (DIP). Now, even without continuing the GIP (which comprises of the TIP and the DIP), the Company’s proposed regular transmission and distribution spending is “…two times (197%) the historical levels of investment before 2020.”

Proposed Transmission Investments Will Not Significantly Improve Reliability
Even though there are transmission upgrade needs in Georgia, Staff Witnesses Rana, Alvarez and Stephens note that the Company’s proposed plans do not provide benefits that exceed the costs. Alvarez and Stephens state that, “…the Company attributes no reliability improvements at all to its TIP spending. But our biggest concern relates to the lack of performance accountability…” and “There does not appear to be any grid reliability emergency at Georgia Power that must be addressed immediately, or that would justify the massive increase in the transmission and distribution capital investment proposed by the Company.”  Staff Witness Rana notes that, “The GIP is not necessary to address growth in Georgia.” Further, “the Company has repeatedly indicated in this Rate Case filing and discovery that the Company’s transmission investment is not expected to improve current levels of customer reliability.  Therefore, the proposed increase in transmission capital expenditure under GIP will result in increased customer rates without the benefit of customer reliability improvements.” In effect, the Company’s proposed transmission solutions are fixing a problem that does not exist, while other transmission problems go unresolved. 
Proposed Transmission Investments Were Not Adequately Evaluated
When asked if the Company performed a cost-benefit analysis evaluating its transmission plans, “the Company indicated that a cost/benefit study was not conducted for the proposed transmission investments.” Further, the expert witness pointed out that, “Georgia Power bears the burden to prove that premature equipment replacement is cost-effective for customers. Georgia Power has not prepared any cost-benefit analyses of its TIP, or its premature equipment replacement program, or its distribution investment plan packages…. Indeed, Georgia Power Witness Mr. Robinson claimed that it is not possible to complete such cost-benefit analyses.” On rebuttal, Company Witness Robinson defended the Company’s decision to not conduct a cost-benefit analysis on transmission stating “…it is impractical to perform a cost-benefit analysis…” His justification was that the system changes significantly making it hard to forecast system needs; however, that is the basic nature of the electric system, which changes based on load and generation, on a second-by-second basis. The Commission routinely requires cost benefit analysis for generation assets, even though generation assets are subject to the same sorts of variables that Mr. Robinson claims exempts $2.6 billion transmission and distribution from any analysis. Suggesting that because the system is dynamic that there should be no cost benefit analysis would be a costly precedent if applied to generation assets, as is being applied by the Company to transmission and distribution assets in this rate case. 
Commission Staff Witnesses noted that the Company has a financial incentive to not adequately conduct transmission planning. Commission Staff Rana stated, “Q: Do you have any theories as to why the Company is proposing such a massive Transmission Investment Plan that will not result in any reliability improvement? A: One theory is that the Company is interested in unnecessarily growing the rate base. The Company is front-loading transmission investments under the pretext of GIP.” Commission Staff witnesses Alvarez and Stephens shared Mr. Rana’s assessment by stating, “Investor-owned utilities are motivated to replace assets once they are fully depreciated, as fully depreciated assets have no book value and earn no rate of return.” In summary: the Company is incentivized to overspend on distribution and transmission upgrades that may not be necessary, and effectively discouraged from conducting cost benefit analysis because doing so would likely show such investments to be unnecessary.
Transmission Plan Oversight is Not Adequate
The Commission Staff Witnesses made a recommendation that the GIP be wholly rejected, while the remainder of the transmission plan be approved. Alvarez and Stephens suggested, “Finally, we believe the Commission should consider the positives of limiting T&D capital spending to ‘only’ a doubling of pre-GIP levels.” When cross examined, the GPSC staff witnesses noted that they did not have the time nor data available to evaluate the rest of the Company’s transmission plans, but only had the ability to focus on the GIP. Therefore, Alvarez and Stephens’ recommendation is not an approval of the remainder of the Company’s transmission plans, but an acquiescence. Further, the GPSC staff witnesses neither had the time nor data available to conduct alternative transmission planning exercises, and were unable to propose transmission alternatives. Because no other stakeholders are allowed to conduct discovery in the rate case proceeding, there is no possible way for any stakeholder to provide alternative transmission plans or recommendations. In effect, neither the Company, nor staff expert witnesses, provided any cost benefit analysis for transmission planning in the rate case, and distribution and transmission plans have received scant review regarding effectively the largest portions of the rates in this rate case.

 
Conclusions
Not all transmission plans and expenditures are equal. Commission Staff Witnesses noted that “The proposed GIP will dispose of some healthy, functional transmission assets and replace them with new ones, thus diverting investments from where they are most needed.” SREA believes that significant transmission upgrades are necessary for the North Georgia Reliability and Resilience Action Plan, and to integrate renewable resources from south Georgia; but the costs associated with the GIP may cause delays to those transmission plans. SREA is extremely concerned about the Company’s opposition to conducting cost benefit analysis for transmission planning, and the Commission Staff’s inability to provide transmission alternatives to the Company’s proposals. Without the ability for stakeholders, Commission Staff, or the Commission to directly provide feedback and input into the Company’s transmission plans before the plans are presented in an IRP or rate case, there is limited ability to fix the proposed plan in those docketed proceedings. As in this rate case, the Commission is faced with an “all or nothing” transmission planning approach between the Company and Commission Staff instead of providing the Commission a more nuanced approach with multiple alternatives based on sound analysis.  
Transmission and distribution represent some of the largest costs in the 2022 rate case, but have received some of the least amount of scrutiny. During cross examination, Commission Staff witnesses noted that there are many potential benefits of transmission that neither the Company nor they evaluated in this rate case such as reliability, system economics, competition, reserve margin savings, access to lower cost renewables, and ability to retire uneconomic higher cost resources. If enough transmission benefits are calculated against the costs, well-planned transmission investments can result in more benefits than costs, reducing costs for ratepayers. Good transmission planning can reduce ratepayer costs, but only if conducted from the beginning and not as an afterthought. However, as noted by the Commission Staff witnesses, the Company is not financially incentivized to identify transmission solutions where the benefits outweigh the costs. Indeed, no witness in this rate case had the ability to conduct such an analysis, except the Company, which opted not to do so because they felt like they were incapable of conducting such an analysis. Of course, the Commission has many options in this rate case regarding the Company’s transmission and distribution plan: the Commission could approve the transmission and distribution plan case as-is without modification, add to it, reject the GIP, reject just a portion of the GIP such as the DIP (the largest cost of the GIP), or some other permutation, but any decision will still lack robust cost benefit analysis. 
SREA’s recommended motions provide reasonable guard rails for the Commission to “trust but verify” the Company’s transmission planning processes. The Commission readily hires independent consultants to run engineering modeling software to evaluate resource procurement decisions by the Company. Also, independent consultants are hired to conduct competitive bidding practices in the RFP processes. By hiring independent experts to help both the RFP and IRP processes, the Commission can ensure that ratepayers are better protected. Hiring an independent third party can protect ratepayers and save money. However, the Commission has never hired an independent transmission consultant to conduct the highly complicated transmission planning analyses to verify or make recommendations regarding the Company’s transmission plans. Essentially, the Commission has been approving billions of dollars in transmission plans without third-party analysis or independent monitoring. Without an independent consultant, the Commission will have no ability to truly regulate the Company’s transmission plans and proactively plan for the future. SREA’s proposed motions enable the Commission to hire a third-party, independent transmission analysis that will give the Commission the ability to compare and contrast the Company’s proposals against alternatives. The Commission would be under no obligation to use the analysis as its sole basis for decision making; the consultant and the analysis would be a trusted advisor and provide valuable data in an otherwise opaque process. 
Additionally, the Commission may need to open a new docket to evaluate its transmission planning processes. As Georgia’s resource mix continues to evolve, transmission planning will become ever more important and difficult, necessitating an overhaul of the transmission planning process and requirements. Opening a docket to evaluate recommendations for reform and improvements in the Georgia transmission planning process, while hiring an independent transmission consultant, would both be state-led, proactive measures that could be used to highlight that federal transmission planning processes are unnecessary.

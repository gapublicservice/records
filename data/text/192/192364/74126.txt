                      POST-HEARING BRIEF OF CHARGEPOINT, INC.

         ChargePoint, Inc. (ChargePoint) respectfully files this post-hearing brief pursuant to Rule

515-2-1-.04 of the Georgia Public Service Commission (Commission) and the Commission’s April

7, 2022 Procedural and Scheduling Order in the above-captioned proceeding.

I.       INTRODUCTION

         On June 24, 2022, Georgia Power Company (Georgia Power or Company) filed this

general rate case to address any changes in revenue requirements that may have occurred since the

Company’s last rate case in 2019.1 As part of that filing, Georgia Power included several EV

proposals, which are the focus of this brief and ChargePoint’s participation in this proceeding.

         First, Georgia Power proposes to increase its investments in EV charging facilities and

infrastructure upgrades to support EV charging.2 ChargePoint supports Georgia Power’s proposal

to invest in EV charging facilities and infrastructure upgrades, as long as site hosts that participate

in the make ready and community charging infrastructure programs continue to be able to choose

their preferred charging equipment.

         Second, Georgia Power proposes to establish a new rider rate schedule for separately

metered non-residential EV charging, designated Schedule Charge It Electric Vehicle (Rider

II.     ARGUMENT

        A.      The Commission should approve Georgia Power’s proposed EV make-ready
                infrastructure upgrades and investments in community charging facilities
                while ensuring that site host choice remains a tenet of the programs.

        In the competitive marketplace for EV charging services, the ability of site hosts to select

the technologies they prefer in an open market, invest their own capital, seek any incentives

available through public agencies or utilities, and, in the case of commercial stations, offer

competitive charging services to attract drivers and recoup necessary expenses, is paramount.4 The

term “site host” refers to the owner or lessor of the property on which an EV charging station is

located.5 Site hosts can include residential customers; owners of multi-family housing (MFH);

       ChargePoint’s understanding is that Georgia Power’s proposals would continue to provide

                1.     Rider CIEV-1 will effectively mitigate the impact of demand charges on
                       EV charging station site hosts for a four-year period while their load
                       factor is relatively low.

       Demand charges can pose a significant challenge and expense for EV charging station site

hosts, especially during periods of low utilization.10 In low utilization scenarios, a site host may

experience a relatively small number of charging sessions in a month, but if several vehicles charge

at the same time, the demand charge will be set for the entire month (and potentially much longer

when there is a demand ratchet provision, as there is for schedules PLS, PLM, and PLL).11 Rider

CIEV-1 will mitigate the impact of demand charges on EV charging station site hosts for a four-

year period while their load factor is relatively low, thus reducing this significant challenge and

expense for site hosts. By mitigating the impact of demand charges on site hosts, Rider CIEV-1

will encourage more site hosts to deploy EV chargers for the benefit of EV drivers and Georgia

Power’s customers. Accordingly, the Commission should approve Rider CIEV-1, including the

modification described below.

         C.      The Commission should clarify that the sale of EV charging services is not
                 considered the “resale” of electricity.

         ChargePoint witness Wilson explains the importance of EV charging station operators (site

hosts) being able to sell EV charging services by the kilowatt-hour in Georgia.13 This step is crucial

to encouraging EV charging station deployment and EV adoption. Currently, if a non-utility site

host sells EV charging services on a $/kWh basis, it risks violating Georgia Power’s prohibition

on resale of electricity, which appears in Georgia Power’s tariff.14 This risk discourages many

prospective site hosts from installing public EV chargers.

                 1.      The nature of EV charging service is distinct from how a utility sells
                         electricity.

         Historically, the only commodity or service priced on a $/kWh basis was electricity, so any

person or entity using a $/kWh pricing arrangement is generally considered to be reselling

electricity. This creates uncertainty and risk that a site host would violate Georgia Power’s tariff

by charging drivers to charge their EVs on a $/kWh basis. As a result, site hosts are effectively

prevented from charging on a $/kWh basis in Georgia, which limits entry into the competitive EV

market.

        In reality, EV charging stations use electricity to provide a service; specifically, the service

of charging the battery of an EV.15 Just because the energy stored in a battery is measured in

kilowatt-hours does not mean the EV charging station is reselling electricity. Instead, the EV

charging station is using electricity to perform the value-added service of charging a battery, which

stores the energy.16

        As ChargePoint witness Wilson explains, the nature of EV charging service is distinct from

the way that a utility sells electricity. A utility sells electricity as a commodity. A customer then

uses that commodity for a wide-variety of purposes, such as powering an air conditioner, cooking,

or charging their smart phone. An EV charging station, on the other hand, uses electricity for a

single purpose – charging the battery of an EV.

        Similar to EV charging station site hosts, many businesses use electricity to provide

services to the public. Take a laundromat, for example. The laundromat uses electricity to run its

washing machines, but in doing so, does not resell electricity to its customers who use the machines

to wash and dry their clothes. Rather, the more accurate description of the laundromat’s business

is that it uses electricity to provide the services of washing and drying customers’ clothes. By the

same token, it is inaccurate to say that an EV charging station site host is reselling electricity to

EV drivers. Rather, the accurate way to describe the transaction between a site host and an EV

driver is that the site host uses electricity to provide the service of charging the battery of an EV.17

                2.    Permitting EV charging station site hosts to price charging services on a
                      $/kWh basis will result in more accurate and fair costs for battery
                      charging services.

       A per-minute pricing arrangement fails to account for the amount of energy actually

delivered to an EV’s battery.18 This is because different EV models have different maximum

charging speeds. As such, billing on a per-minute basis leads EV drivers to pay varying costs for

the same amount of energy consumed.19 For example, at a DC fast charger, an older EV may take

90 minutes to add 30 kWh of charge, whereas a newer model equipped with a high maximum

charging speed may only take 30 minutes to add the same 30 kWh of charge.20 Under the prevailing

$/minute scheme that most site hosts have adopted to avoid violating the prohibition on resale, it

would cost the older EV triple the amount to add the same 30 kWh charge to its battery.21 This is

akin to gas stations charging customers varying rates for gas based solely on their car’s MPG,

which would be absurd and would not be tolerated by customers. Similarly, having the cost of

charging not correlate to the amount of energy delivered to the battery is counterintuitive, and

negatively impacts those EV drivers with older vehicle models.22 The Commission should clarify

that charging on a $/kWh basis does not constitute the resale of electricity to mitigate this

unequitable result.

               3.       Forty-three states already allow site hosts to charge EV drivers on a
                        $/kWh basis.

       In each of the 43 states that have addressed this issue, as well as in the District of Columbia

and Puerto Rico, EV charging station site hosts may charge EV drivers on a $/kWh basis.23

ChargePoint witness Wilson notes that the uncertainty and risk faced by site hosts preventing them

from charging on a $/kWh basis has arisen in different ways in different states and has accordingly

been resolved in different ways.24 In some states this issue has been resolved through a statutory

change, in other states through a statewide regulatory commission proceeding, and other states

through a utility-specific proceeding (such as this docket).25 Nevertheless, in each of the 43 states

and the District of Columbia the result is the same: EV charging station site hosts may charge EV

drivers using their preferred pricing arrangement, including on a $/kWh basis.26

               4.       The federal government is implementing charging on a $/kWh basis as a
                        requirement for NEVI funding.

       ChargePoint witness Wilson also notes that the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA)

is in the process of implementing the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) formula

program, which was created by the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) passed in 2021.27

Based on current estimates, Georgia is expected to receive $135 million over 5 years to support

the expansion of EV charging; Georgia will also have the opportunity to apply for $2.5 billion in

grant funding dedicated to EV charging.28 In its Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, the FHWA

           However, any EV charging station that followed this federal guidance would violate

III.       CONCLUSION

           For the foregoing reasons, ChargePoint respectfully urges the Commission to adopt the

following recommendations, as set out in this post-hearing brief:

       •   Approve Georgia Power’s proposed investments in community charging facilities and EV

           charging infrastructure upgrades across the state;

       •   Approve Rider CIEV-1 and direct Georgia Power to allow any qualifying customer to take

           service on it (not just new customers);

       •   Clarify that the sale of EV charging services by non-utilities shall not be considered resale

           of electricity.

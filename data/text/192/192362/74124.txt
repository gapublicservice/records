POST HEARING BRIEF AND PROPOSED ORDER OF THE GEORGIA COALITION OF LOCAL GOVERNMENTS
Pursuant to Rule 515-2-1-.04 of the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”) and the Commission’s August 5, 2022 Second Amended Procedural and Scheduling Order in the above-captioned proceeding, the Georgia Coalition of Local Governments (“the Coalition”) respectfully files this post-hearing brief and proposed order.

INTRODUCTION
Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or “the Company”) seeks approval of its 2022 Rate Case in Docket No. 44280. The Company is requesting a rate increase of $2.9 billion over the three-year rate cycle, based upon an approved ROE of 11% and a capital structure of 56% equity and 44% debt. This request will result in an increase in the average residential bill of 12% and will place additional pressures on many customers already struggling to afford utility costs and other living expenses. As part of this rate request, the Company is also proposing changes to the structures of several rates, riders, and tariffs, including the RNR tariff for behind-the-meter solar. 
In the following pages, the Coalition will outline its recommendations on several issues that are of particular importance to our member-governments and communities. To be clear, these recommendations are not intended to be comprehensive. Failure to address any particular issue should not be construed as agreement with the Company’s position. 


RECOMMENDATIONS
The Coalition respectfully requests that the Commission amend any future stipulation between Staff and the Company as needed to incorporate the following recommendations: 
Reject the Company’s request to place all RNR customers on a three-part rate with a demand charge, like the Smart Usage rate
Uncap the monthly netting pilot for the next three years and initiate a non-litigated stakeholder process to develop a successor tariff ahead of the 2025 rate case
Adopt the PIA Staff’s proposed community solar subscription price of $20/kW for residential customers and $22/kW for commercial customers 
Order Georgia Power to reprogram or replace meters as necessary to ensure all residential and commercial accounts with AMI meters can access hourly usage data as contemplated in the Commission’s March 18, 2021 order
Approve the Company’s Request for $81 million for the EV Make Ready Program and direct the Company to allocate funds on a first-come, first-served basis for all eligible projects, regardless of project size
Discontinue use of the Smart Usage tariff as the default residential tariff and restore the R tariff as the default
Require the Company to offer the Fixed Pricing Alternative (FPA) tariff to eligible customers at all times
Reject the Company’s proposed ROE and capital structure and adopt the alternative presented by Staff Witness Gorman 
Expand the Income Qualified Senior Citizen Discount to all income qualified customers

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Monthly Netting/RNR
The Company’s proposal regarding the RNR tariff has two key parts. First, the Company has proposed to maintain the 5,000 customer cap on the monthly-netting pilot, which would require all new solar customers to take service under instantaneous netting. Second, the Company is proposing that all new solar customers be required to take service under a three-part rate with a demand charge. As Coalition Witness Dr. David Hill testified, neither change is justified, and the latter will effectively end deployment of rooftop solar in Georgia.
The three-part rate that the Company is proposing to impose on residential customers, the TOU-RD or poorly-named “Smart Usage” rate, includes a non-coincident demand charge that is assessed on a customer’s highest 60-minute kW measurement during the current month. A customer’s demand for billing purposes can be set at any hour of any day of the month, making it virtually impossible to manage with solar. Consider the example of a customer coming home to make dinner after dark, a common scenario in the winter months. Using an oven, induction stove, and heat pump simultaneously could set a hefty demand charge and is completely unavoidable with solar unless the customer is somehow able to make dinner and pre-heat their home during solar-producing hours every day of the month.     
Imposing a non-coincident demand charge on solar customers will significantly erode customer savings opportunities. As Dr. Hill’s modeling of customer economics using NREL’s System Advisor Model revealed, solar customers on the TOU-RD rate can expect simple paybacks in excess of 30 years, longer than the expected useful life of the system. The Company’s analysis is even more sobering, predicting average customer savings of only $256-$313 per year, depending on whether an instantaneous or monthly netting interval is used. For an average 7 kW system, savings that small would push the payback well beyond 40 years. For this reason alone, the Company’s proposal to require all solar customers to take service under a three part rate should be rejected. 
Of course, there are other reasons to reject mandatory three-part rates for solar customers. Chief among them is the issue of fairness. Forcing solar customers onto a single rate, when no other subset of residential customers is required to take service under a single rate, is discriminatory and punitive. Let’s recognize this proposal for what it is—an attempt to extract nearly equal cost recovery for the delivery of substantially less energy. We can have good faith disagreements about the value of energy put back onto the grid, but it is altogether inappropriate for the Company  to devalue the energy that is used behind a customer’s meter.
Thus, the question for the Commission boils down to the value of exported solar. Currently, with the oversubscription of the monthly netting pilot, customers receive the Renewable Cost Benefit (RCB) avoided cost, which is currently $0.034/kWh and only considers avoided-energy benefits. The Company asserts that this is appropriate because “a customer participating under the RNR tariff is likely not providing any immediate demand- or customer-related services when they deliver energy back onto the grid.” This is demonstrably untrue. In 2021, Georgia Power’s system peaked on July 29th at 4 pm, when both southern- and western-oriented solar arrays are still producing. In aggregate, the effect of behind the meter solar production will be a reduction in the Company’s peak load forecasts, resulting in the procurement of less generation capacity. The Company responds by arguing that BTM resources have no contractual obligation to generate, and therefore are not included in the Company’s planning, but that is also not true. Behind the meter solar, even when not considered to be a resource by the Company and even when not actively encouraged as a form of load reduction, does appear in the Company’s load forecast as a load reduction and therefore influences the Company’s planning. 
It has been said that beauty is in the eye of the beholder, and the same can be said for value. If the Company has no desire to use solar for anything other than offsetting energy needs, then the observed benefits will be small. However, there is a wealth of experience from other states that demonstrates the myopia of the Company’s position.,,
Fortunately, we don’t even have to look to other states to see a value proposition for rooftop solar, especially when paired with battery storage, rate structures, or incentive programs that align customer behavior with system needs. As an example, consider the situation in North Georgia. In its 2022 Integrated Resource Plan order, the Commission approved the Company’s request to create the North Georgia Reliability Plan, which is meant to ensure the Company’s continued ability to serve load in North Georgia following the retirement of several coal-fired generation units. The Company’s solution is to install ELG controls at Bowen Units 3 and 4 (two economically questionable coal-fired generation units) and make significant investments in the transmission system to connect utility-scale renewable generation in the southern part of the state with the load in metro-Atlanta and north Georgia. This process will take at least two additional IRP cycles to complete. This plan will increase rates in two ways:first, through higher production costs and the continued generation of coal ash and second, through substantial new investments that will grow the Company’s rate base and profits. 
As Southface/SACE Witness Hathaway noted, the load factor for the state of Georgia is around 40%, meaning the grid is only delivering about 40% of the energy it is capable of delivering within a given year. Instead of waiting 6+ years to build transmission lines to serve load during only a small percentage of hours, this Commission could unleash distributed energy investment through an expansion of the monthly netting program and provide much-needed generation in metro Atlanta and other parts of North Georgia. Moreover, the Commission could direct the Company to explore how battery programs, incentive programs, and time-varying rate structures could be used to cost-effectively maintain reliability in that region. 
Beyond the harms to prospective solar customers and the shortsightedness of the Company’s proposal, the Coalition has two final concerns with the Company’s proposal to maintain the cap on the monthly netting program. First, this proposal will have disproportionate impacts on low- and moderate-income households. Second, this proposal will further erode local governments’ ability to achieve their clean energy goals. 
There has been a lot of speculation on behalf of the Company and from some Commissioners about the impact that the IRA will have on the growth of the solar market. While it is true that the IRA does represent a significant investment in clean energy, that investment will not be made equitably. As Coalition Witness Dr. Hill noted, the tax credits in the IRA are not refundable, so they are not easily usable by households with lower adjusted incomes. Though there are some workarounds, for example through direct pay or transferability provisions for commercial customers, the fact remains that the regulatory environment in Georgia will need to be supportive enough to attract those mission-oriented solar companies and non-profit solar organizations to the state in the first place. To be clear, lower income customers are interested in solar and have been investing in solar in Georgia over the past three years, but this is unlikely to persist without monthly netting. Considering the disproportionate impact that the Company’s requested rate increase will have on these households, effectively closing the door to rooftop solar will eliminate one option they have to lower their energy bills and hedge against future rate increases.
The Coalition’s final and largest concern is the impact that the Company’s proposal will have on our ability to reach the clean energy goals adopted by our communities. Through lengthy public engagement processes, Coalition member-governments have identified the clean energy transition as a key priority, with most setting the goal of supplying 100% of community-wide electricity use with clean and renewable energy by 2035 at the latest. Meeting these goals will take significant time, energy, and investment, and the Company’s proposal would effectively remove residential investment from this equation. As noted by Commissioner Echols, the monthly-netting pilot spurred investment in Georgia’s solar market from households across the state, thus helping  solar businesses grow, learn, and lower prices. Continuing this investment and supporting market maturation would help additional residential and commercial customers access solar energy at more favorable economics, create additional community benefits such as local job growth and increased spending, and allow residents to participate in the clean energy transition for which they have advocated and voted. 
To close on this issue, the Coalition understands and appreciates the concerns some Commissioners have with expanding the monthly netting pilot for a limited time. We also understand and appreciate the desire for a “permanent solution.” However, we urge you to consider the gravity of this decision. The policy choice that you will be making could represent the difference between job growth and job loss, between attracting new businesses to Georgia and sending them elsewhere, and between successful clean energy goals and unsuccessful ones. 
The fact is, the Commission cannot give full consideration to all possible options in an accelerated, adversarial 3-4 month proceeding. It can’t even fully probe and understand the options that have been put forth. Nevertheless, if the Commission does decide to accept some sort of intermediate or compromise position, we respectfully submit the following recommendations:
If the Commission proposes to close the R tariff to RNR customers, those customers should be placed on an energy-only time of use rate, NOT a rate with a demand charge. Moreover, if RNR customers are placed on a time of use rate, the peak hours should be identical to the peak hours for non-RNR customers. 
If the Commission proposes a solution other than monthly netting, the Commission should offer additional support to low- and moderate-income customers, such as a low-income adder or rebate.
If the Commission maintains the status quo, and exports are valued at the RCB avoided cost, the Commission should direct the Company to develop a program or incentive structure to facilitate adoption of solar+storage.
If the Commission adopts one of the two sub-retail Staff proposals, intervenors should have an additional opportunity to review the calculations and propose changes to the methodology for Commission review. 
To conclude, the Coalition understands that monthly netting should not be maintained forever. However, given the current size of the solar market; the vanishingly small impact of any potential cost shift; the economic, environmental, and land use benefits of rooftop solar; and the system benefits that could be realized with sufficient creativity from the Company, we urge the Commission to maintain monthly netting for three years and initiate a collaborative stakeholder process to determine a future successor tariff.

Community Solar
The Company has proposed to increase the price of a 1kW subscription to $27.99 for residential customers and $29.99 for commercial customers. The company’s pricing approach is based off lost revenues and fails to provide a sufficient level of value to fully subscribe the program, although that is a stated Company goal. Existing community solar facilities provide value to all ratepayers by providing carbon-free electricity below the company’s avoided cost, and reaching full subscription and constructing new facilities would put further downward pressure on rates. The Company’s proposed solution to the program’s undersubscription, the Income-Qualified Community Solar program, relies on corporate sponsorship at a price far higher than the Company’s other REC offerings and does nothing to address the program’s poor value proposition for customers. Additionally, as confirmed by Witness Legg during cross-examination by the Coalition, the Company’s current pricing approach leaves customers vulnerable to overpaying if the solar facilities produce less than expected, a likely outcome in a world of increasing temperatures.
The Coalition supports PIA staff’s proposed price of $20 for residential customers and $22 for commercial customers, which strikes a more appropriate balance in terms of allocating costs and benefits and creates a more attractive program. The Company’s argument that bill savings are not the purpose of the program should be rejected. The Commission has the authority and opportunity to revisit the purpose of the program in this rate case and create an offering that provides customers the opportunity to support the growth of renewable energy on Georgia Power’s system AND realize bill savings. 

Hourly Usage Data
The Company is requesting $786,000 in cost recovery for expenses that it incurred in making hourly usage data more broadly available to residential and commercial customers through the My Power Usage (MPU) platform. This investment was the result of paragraph 22 of the 2019 rate case stipulation and Commission approval of a Company-proposed option in a March 18, 2021 order. 
The Coalition’s concern with this request for cost recovery is that several of our member-governments’ accounts are still unable to access this data, even though the Company is deeming the work to be complete. The Company asserted in its rebuttal testimony that the only accounts that are unable to access this data are those that have opted out of an AMI meter, unmetered accounts, Special Service accounts, and RTP customers. However, the experience of Coalition member-governments has been that some accounts that do not fall into any of the categories the Company listed are unable to access hourly usage data through My Power Usage, either because the meter is not capable of collecting that information or because the meter is not programmed to collect that information. 
The Company also asserts that it is under no obligation to provide this data to all residential and commercial customers, free of charge or otherwise. While the Company is correct that the stipulation only required them to “work with Staff to develop options for customer access to usage data and submit those options to the PSC for approval,” the Commission’s subsequent order suggests that the Commission’s intention in approving the Company’s proposed investment was that all residential and commercial accounts be able to access this data. An excerpt reads: 

WHEREFORE IT IS ORDERED that Option 1, as provided in the Company’s February 11, 2021 filing, shall be implemented in order to provide all Residential and Commercial customers with access to hourly usage data.

This interpretation of the Commission’s intent was confirmed in the hearings by Commissioner Echols, the self-described crafter of the motion that led to the Company’s investments. 
Given these findings, the Coalition respectfully requests that the Commission direct the Company to replace or reprogram residential and commercial meters as needed to collect this data with all reasonable haste and at the Company’s cost. For avoidance of confusion, the Coalition is not opposed to the limited exceptions defined by the Company in its rebuttal testimony. The Coalition also respectfully requests that the My Power Usage platform be modified to make it easier for customers to export data in bulk (i.e. 8760 format as a .csv file).  As shown in the screen capture on page 15 of Mr. Nifong and Mr. Deffley’s testimony, there is currently a limit of 33 days of data per export, therefore requiring 12 separate exports to obtain a full year of data. When seeking this data for multiple buildings at a time, as the Coalition member-governments often do, this can become very tedious very quickly.  
EV Make Ready
The Company has requested $81 million over the three-year rate cycle for the EV Make Ready program. This funding is critical to the expansion of EV charging infrastructure in the state and is necessary for Georgia to fully capitalize on the federal funding opportunities for electric vehicles provided in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) and Inflation Reduction Act (IRA). The Make Ready Program covers essential non-charger investments like panels, switchgears, wiring, conduits and disconnects that can make up more than 30% of the cost of a charger install. This infrastructure is often not covered or only partially covered by federal grants and can be cost-prohibitive, particularly for cash-strapped school districts and transit authorities. Because of this, the Coalition supports the Company’s full $81 million request. 
However, the Coalition has two recommendations to further improve the program. First, the Coalition respectfully requests that the Commission identify  funding in the Grid Investment Plan or other distribution spending category that can be set aside to cover any investments that need to be made to the Company’s distribution system to support EV charging, particularly for federal grant recipients. Electric school and transit buses require very powerful chargers that may necessitate upgrades to transformers, substations, and other front-of-the-meter equipment. These costs are expressly not covered by federal grants and can be quite expensive. If these costs are shifted in part or in full to the customer, many projects would become cost-prohibitive. 
Second, the Coalition requests that the terms and conditions or program guidelines be changed to allocate funds on a first-come, first-served basis regardless of the number of chargers involved. As the Coalition understands it, sites with fewer than five or six chargers are rarely if ever chosen for funding. This makes it difficult for Coalition member-governments to make investments at community centers, churches, and other gathering spots in charging deserts that may have limited space to accommodate charging. This change would not only be a step in the right direction for equitable charging, but would also put to rest any accusations of picking winners and losers among those who wish to offer charging for commercial reasons.
In summary, this funding is an essential support at a critical moment in the transition to electric vehicles. The charging investments that will be made possible because of this program will provide critical support to existing EV drivers and confidence to aspiring EV drivers, thus strengthening the market for the increasing numbers of EVs that will be manufactured and assembled in the state of Georgia. Beyond this incredible opportunity to support economic development and meet customer needs, the Make Ready Program supports strategic load growth that will put downward pressure on rates, especially as charger utilization increases over time. 

Residential Rate and Tariffs
The Company has proposed to close the standard residential tariff to newly constructed premises and maintain the TOU-RD or “Smart Usage” tariff as the default rate for those locations. The Coalition strongly opposes both proposals. 
The proposal to close the R tariff to newly constructed premises is arbitrary, discriminatory, and harmful to customers. Analyses from the Coalition, Staff, GIPL, and GSEIA/SEIA/Vote Solar all demonstrate that customers on the TOU-RD tariff are consistently charged higher $/kWh rates and pay higher bills than they would have under the R tariff, and that these discrepancies are especially high for low energy users.
Moreover, the TOU-RD rate has no basis in the needs of the system. When asked during cross examination if the TOU-RD rate has succeeded in reducing peak demand on the system, the primary driver of demand-based costs, Mr. Legg countered that peak demand reduction wasn’t the purpose at all—only cost recovery. 

It can have that impact, but the design of the rate is actually to assign costs and,
really, send a proper price signal to our residential customers so that they can economically use electricity.

This should not come as a surprise. As Staff and others point out, the rate’s non-coincident demand charge is not structured to reduce the demand that drives most system costs—coincident peak demand. Instead, it encourages customers to “flatten” their energy usage, or achieve a high load factor, which it has also failed to do. As shown in Table 21 of the Barber, Deitchman, Watkins testimony, customers on the TOU-RD rate actually had a lower load factor than customers on the R rate for every month in 2021 except March. 
The reason for this lack of response from customers is that the TOU-RD tariff provides few, if any, actionable ways for customers to control their demand and lower their bills. As Witnesses Barnes points out: 

The notion that customers should just avoid using major appliances at the same time is much harder to execute than it sounds, in particular because a single instance of relative inattention (or personal need) can produce an outsized bill impact. For instance, during the summer months when an air conditioner is likely to be cycling consistently, it is next to impossible to avoid using an electric dryer during the same time period when the air conditioner is operating. The same is true for other daily necessities, such as cooking with an electric range or oven, not to mention other things like using a dishwasher, coffee maker, toaster, or electric hair dryer.
 
Contrary to the Company’s positive spin that the TOU-RD rate provides three ways to save, the reality is that it is next to impossible to manage, even for more interested and sophisticated customers. 
In summary, the standard R tariff should be maintained and become the default rate for both newly constructed and pre-existing premises. If the TOU-RD rate is maintained, it should not be the default for any customers, should be fully optional, and should be adjusted so that the demand charge will only apply to peak hours (i.e. between 2 pm and 7 pm on non-holiday weekdays between June and September). Additionally, the Coalition supports PIA Staff’s recommendation that the Company be ordered to create a rate comparison tool for all residential customers to better enable them to select the best rate for their usage and demand profile. 

Fixed Price Alternative
The Company currently has discretion in offering the Fixed Price Alternative (FPA) tariff to customers who would like to leave Real Time Pricing (RTP) for a more predictable tariff. As the tariff currently reads, this rate option is offered “periodically, as determined by the Company, to eligible customers.” This determination is made in consideration of “uncertainty around environmental regulation or fuel markets that could impact the forecasted prices used in the development of FPA offers.” Staff recommends that the decision not be made solely by the Company, but in coordination with the Commission, and that the words “as determined by the Company” be struck from the tariff. The Coalition largely agrees with the Staff but further requests that the FPA be offered to all eligible customers at any time, unless the Commission orders otherwise.

Cost Control/ Energy Affordability
As stated in the introduction to this brief, the Company has requested a rate increase of $2.9 billion over the three-year rate cycle, based upon an approved ROE of 11% and a capital structure of 56% equity and 44% debt. This request will result in an increase in the average residential bill of 12% and will place additional pressures on many customers already struggling to afford utility costs and other living expenses. Given the rate increases the Company is expected to request during the February 2023 Fuel Docket and upon completion of Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4, the Coalition urges the Commission to identify ways to reduce the proposed base rate increase.
 One source of potential cost-reduction relates to the Company’s ROE. The Company’s requested ROE of 11% is higher than the national average for investor-owned utilities, and the Company has failed to provide sufficient evidence that approval of this request is necessary or beneficial to customers. While the Company has argued that the requested ROE is necessary to prevent further downgrades to its credit rating, the record shows that recent downgrades in the Company’s credit rating have been driven primarily by cost-overruns and delays at Plant Vogtle, not the ROE approved by this Commission. 
As Mr. Fetter notes, there are several qualitative factors that are at least as important to a Company’s credit rating as quantitative ones, including the perceived “constructiveness” of the regulatory environment. The Georgia Legislature and Georgia PSC have taken several steps to provide constructive regulation, including authorizing alternative rate plans, allowing cost-recovery prior to the completion of Plant Vogtle, and allowing the sharing of earnings above the authorized ROE band. In other words, there is reason to believe that the Company’s stern warnings about downgrades are overblown.  Based on this and other information in the record, the Coalition supports the ROE and capital structure proposed by Staff Witness Gorman.
In addition, the Coalition respectfully requests that the Commission consider expanding the income-qualified senior citizen discount to  all income-qualified customers. As the many public comment periods have made clear, even if the Company’s proposed rate increase is mitigated, many customers will struggle to pay their bills. Affordability issues have a wide variety of harms that reach far beyond the customers who are struggling to pay, including negative health impacts, economic participation impacts, and mental health/behavioral impacts. 

CONCLUSION
The Coalition thanks the Commission for this opportunity to provide feedback and for its careful consideration of the arguments above and in the enclosed proposed order, which is labeled as Exhibit A below.

Exhibit A – Proposed Order

WHEREFORE IT IS ORDERED, that the monthly netting pilot shall continue without a customer or capacity cap until the conclusion of the 2025 rate case.

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company’s request to require all RNR customers to take service under a three-part rate is rejected. RNR customers shall remain free to take service under any rate for which they are otherwise eligible. 

ORDERED FURTHER, the PIA Staff shall initiate a stakeholder proceeding to develop a successor tariff or selection of successor tariffs for Commission consideration in the next rate case. Such proceeding must be open to all interested parties, and the Company shall provide any information that the Staff deems necessary to evaluate proposed alternatives. 

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company’s proposed community solar prices of $27.99/kW for residential customers and $29.99/kW for general service customers are rejected. The Company shall file revised tariffs by January 1, 2022 that reflect the Staff-recommended prices of $20/kW for residential customers and $22/kW for general service customers 

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company shall reprogram or replace meters as necessary to ensure all residential and commercial accounts with AMI meters can access hourly usage data as contemplated in the Commission’s March 18, 2021 order. This is to be completed by March 1, 2023.

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company’s Request for $81 million for the EV Make Ready Program is approved.

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company shall amend the Make Ready program guidelines to allocate funds on a first-come, first-served basis for all eligible projects, regardless of project size. These amended guidelines shall be filed with the Commission by January 1, 2023. 

ORDERED FURTHER, the default tariff for residential customers shall be changed from the TOU-RD tariff to the R tariff.  The Company’s request to close the R tariff to newly constructed premises is rejected.

ORDERED FURTHER, the Fixed Price Alternative tariff shall be revised to strike the phrase “periodically, as determined by the Company.” The Company shall file the revised tariff with the Commission by January 1, 2023. 

ORDERED FURTHER, the Company’s authorized ROE shall be set at 9.45%, with a capital structure of 56% equity 2023 and 51% equity in 2024 and 2025, as recommended by Staff Witness Gorman.

ORDERED FURTHER, the Income Qualified Senior Citizen Discount shall be renamed to the Income Qualified Citizen Discount and shall be amended to have no age requirement. The rate of the discount and cost recovery mechanism shall be approved as proposed by the Company.

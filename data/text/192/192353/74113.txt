I.      INTRODUCTION

        In this proceeding, Georgia Power Company advances several proposals aimed at

“support[ing] the growth of electric transportation in Georgia.”1 Those proposals include

investments in the buildout of electrical infrastructure necessary to support electric vehicle (EV)

charging (otherwise known as the “Make Ready Program”); investments in Company-owned EV

chargers (otherwise known as the “Community Charging Program”); and an attractive new rate

for commercial EV charging (the “Charge-It Electric Vehicle” or “CIEV” tariff). At a high level,

EVgo Services LLC (EVgo) is generally supportive of these proposals. The record demonstrates

that the Company’s proposed EV-related infrastructure investments and rate design have the

potential to benefit not only participants but all Georgia Power customers.

        EVgo’s chief concern, however, is that the Company’s fine print (its public Make Ready

Program Terms and Conditions, as well as its internal Make Ready Program Standards—both

produced through discovery in this proceeding) arbitrarily picks winners and losers from the

several emerging business models in Georgia’s nascent EV charging market. Rather than

allowing all EV charging facilities to participate in and benefit from the Make Ready Program,

the Company’s eligibility requirements exclude EV charging stations owned and operated by

third-party EV service providers (EVSPs) on the premises of other commercial customers.

Worse still, whereas the Company has historically provided electric service to third-party EVSPs

(as the customer of record for an EV charging station), the Company’s internal documents

suggest that it will no longer offer electric service to charging stations owned and operated by an

EVSP. The Company does not offer any direct or rebuttal testimony acknowledging—let alone

justifying—its unlawful and discriminatory approach.

           The Company’s unexplained insistence on titling the playing field in the competitive EV

charging market unfairly privileges Georgia Power while undermining customer choice. That is

because Georgia Power’s eligibility requirements force commercial customers who want to host

an EV charging station to either host a Georgia Power-owned site, or (b) take on the burden of

owning and operating a charging site, but do not allow those customers to host a site owned and

operated by a third-party EVSP. Moreover, Georgia Power’s restrictive eligibility requirements

undermine its own Make Ready Program. Third-party owner-operators have deep technical

experience regarding the installation, operation and maintenance of EV charging stations and are

responsible for roughly three quarters of the public EV fast charging stations deployed nationally

today.2 The Company’s approach would prohibit participation for the predominant method of

deploying DCFC nationally to the detriment of the success of the Make Ready program as well

as Georgia Power’s greater goals to promote transportation electrification in its service territory.

           EVgo therefore submits this post-hearing brief pursuant to the Commission’s Second

Amended Procedural and Scheduling Order and the Commission’s Rule 515-2-1-.04 to request

that the Commission direct the Company to provide service to third-party EVSPs and allow those

owner-operators, who are just like any other commercial customer, to participate in the Make

Ready Program. EVgo also recommends certain modest modifications to the Company’s

II.     ARGUMENT

        A.       The Commission Should Direct Georgia Power to Discontinue Its
                 Unreasonable and Arbitrary Practice of Disqualifying Third-Party Owner-
                 Operators From Its Make Ready Program and Direct the Company to
                 Continue Providing Service to Third-Party Owned Chargers Consistent with
                 its Legal Obligations.

        “Make-ready” infrastructure refers to the electrical equipment necessary to operate an EV

charging station, including sub-panels, main panels, conductors, wiring, transformers and other

equipment.3 Utility investments in make-ready infrastructure can bolster market deployment of

charging stations because make-ready infrastructure often represents one of the biggest financial

hurdles to installing an EV charging station. Further, make-ready investments not only support

EV charging station owners and the EV drivers who use those stations, they can also benefit all

ratepayers. That is because EV loads can put downward pressure on rates for all customers;

indeed, Georgia Power’s Ratepayer Impact Measure testing indicates that its Make Ready

Program will do so.4 Commissions across the country have approved utility make-ready

programs in order to leverage a combination of utility and private capital investment in charging

infrastructure to provide ratepayer benefits.5

        Georgia Power implemented a Make Ready Infrastructure Program following its 2019

rate case. In this proceeding, Georgia Power proposes to substantially expand that program and

        However, while Georgia Power states that its program is available to all existing Georgia

Power commercial and industrial customers,7 Georgia Power does not and will not offer make-

ready funding to third-party EV charging service providers that own and operate a charging

station on a site host’s premises, even if that third-party owner-operator is a Georgia Power

a new account in a charging company’s name that is located on a Georgia Power customer’s

premise. The billing account must be in the name of the premise customer.”9

        The Commission should reject Georgia Power’s unlawful approach towards third-party

owner-operators. As a threshold matter, Georgia Power has no authority to pick and choose

customers or discriminate against certain customers. Ga. Pub. Serv. Comm’n v. Georgia Power

Co., 182 Ga. 706 (1936). As a regulated monopoly electric supplier, it is prohibited from having

or applying any service rule or regulation which unreasonably discriminates between classes of

consumers or between members within a class of consumers. O.G.C.A. § 46-3-11(a); see also

Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. r. 515-3-1-.02 (prohibiting unjust discrimination by utilities in the

conduct of their business). Georgia Power has an obligation to serve all customers desiring to

take service on a non-discriminatory basis and may not disallow third-party charging companies

from taking service simply because it would prefer the billing account to be in the name of a

continue providing service to third-party owned chargers consistent with its legal obligations.

       Further, Georgia Power’s unwillingness to allow third-party owner operators to

participate in its Make Ready Program is both discriminatory and anti-competitive. Again, under

Georgia law and basic principles of utility regulation, the Company may not apply program

eligibility requirements that discriminate against certain EV charger owners while putting other

EV charger owners at an advantage. O.C.G.A. § 46-3-11(a). Moreover, the Company’s approach

would effectively preference its own chargers, because commercial customers seeking to host,

but not own or operate, an EV charger would have no option but to install a Georgia Power-

owned charger.

       In addition to being discriminatory and plainly anti-competitive, the Company’s

insistence on excluding third-party owner-operators is unjust and unreasonable, for at least four

reasons. First, the Company’s eligibility requirements inappropriately put the Company’s “thumb

on the scale” of the competitive market. Those requirements effectively disadvantage one EV

charging business model (the owner-operator model) in an emerging market that features a range

of business models. Thus, while the Company purports to support a competitive charging

market, the Company’s failure to establish business model-agnostic eligibility requirements have

exactly the opposite effect.

       Second, the Company’s approach is arbitrary and has no record basis. Whether the

customer of record is a site host, a third-party charging provider, an owner-operator of EV

charging, or any other entity involved in a public EV charging arrangement should have no

bearing on whether a charging station is eligible for funding for make-ready infrastructure


                                                5

through the Company’s program. The Company offers no rationale for linking Make Ready

Program eligibility to the charging station’s ownership arrangement; indeed, none exists.

        Third, the Company’s approach makes it an outlier to all other make-ready programs

nationally. As EVgo witness Murchie observed, none of the many other utilities across the

country implementing a make-ready program exclude third-party owner-operators as Georgia

Power proposes to do.10 EVgo presented examples of typical make ready program requirements

approved and implemented in other states, which allow any applicant (the site host or an EV

charging owner-operator) to own, lease, or operate EV charging equipment at the site host

premise, and separately meter all load associated with charging equipment.11

        Finally, the Company’s approach undermines its own Make Ready Program and denies

its customers choice. Georgia Power’s commercial customers seeking to install public EV

charging sites must own and operate those sites in order to benefit from the Make-Ready

program, even if they have neither the expertise nor interest in doing so.12 Customers

uninterested in the complexities of owning, operating, financing, and maintaining a DCFC

station would have no other choice but to host a Georgia Power-owned charger (implicating the

competitive concerns described above). The Company’s approach would therefore tie its own

customers’ hands and lock out a market segment that is responsible for approximately three

quarters of the public fast charging deployed nationally today, for no apparent reason.

         B.       The Commission Should Adopt Reasonable Guardrails to Ensure that
                  Georgia Power’s Community Charging Program Does Not Discourage
                  Investments by the Competitive Charging Market.

         Georgia Power installs, operates, maintains and owns EV charging stations through its

“Community Charging Program.”15 In the Company’s 2019 rate case, the Commission approved

$2 million per year (2020-2022) in spending for the Community Charging Program.16 The

Company now proposes to extend and expand its program, and requests $3 million per year

between 2023 and 2025 for the Program.17 If approved, the Company’s request would result in

an additional 33 stations—more than doubling the Company’s current deployments through the

Community Charging Program.18

         EVgo believes that utility programs and private investments can play complementary

roles in creating a robust, balanced and competitive market for EV charging that will lead to

equitable EV adoption over the long-term.19 To achieve this outcome, however, it is critical that

utility deployments do not interfere with or hinder the growth of the competitive charging

market.

        Georgia Power asserts that it does not desire or intend to compete with private charging

providers. The Company states that it “primarily operates [its Community Charging] program as

a provider of last resort to provide public DC fast charging options for EV drivers in areas that

lack investment in public DC fast charging.”20 The Company’s process for identifying and

selecting Community Charging sites, however, leaves a substantial risk that the Company will in

fact directly compete with competitive charging providers. In order to determine whether the

competitive market is likely to serve a prospective Community Charging site, the Company

consults publicly available information such as the PlugShare EV Charging Station Map21 and

determines the site’s proximity to other existing or anticipated public fast charging sites.22 While

EVgo appreciates the intent, that process needs further refinement, as it is currently subjective,

qualitative, and not subject to Commission oversight. Importantly, competitive providers have no

effective way of incorporating the Company’s internal site evaluation process into their own

charging station planning and deployment processes, and additional transparency is needed.

        EVgo therefore recommends that the Commission add guardrails to the Company’s

Community Charging Program. Specifically, EVgo recommends that the Commission direct

Georgia Power to establish transparent, publicly-available siting criteria that quantitatively assess

whether a prospective Community Charging site is underserved by the private market.

        first. Participation in Charge It is available to new, separately metered charging

        EVgo supports the CIEV rate.34 It is a well-structured rate that offers an excellent

opportunity to encourage additional investment in EV charging infrastructure if implemented

appropriately.35 The procurement of power represents a significant portion of operating costs borne

by EV charging operators, so the availability of Commercial EV rates—rates that account for the

uniqueness of DCFC loads—factors into EVSPs’ decision-making when developing new charging

stations.36 In particular, the demand charge component of traditional commercial rates can present

a barrier to increased deployment of public DCFC infrastructure.37 The CIEV rider lowers that

barrier by reducing the demand charge borne by public DCFC operators for a limited period of

time.38 The CIEV rider can therefore be a valuable catalyst for greater investments in EV charging

infrastructure.39

rates and make-ready are complementary programs.40 They work hand in hand because each

program addresses different aspects of the economics of public charging.41 Therefore, it is essential

                              EXHIBIT A – PROPOSED ORDER

WHEREFORE IT IS ORDERED, Georgia Power Company must continue to provide separately
metered service to third party owner operators of electric vehicle charging stations desiring to
take separately metered service from the Company;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company’s proposal to invest $81 million (over
three years) in electric vehicle charging make-ready infrastructure is approved;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company must file make-ready program rules and
requirements in this docket within 30 days following a final order in this case. Those rules and
requirements must allow participation by third-party electric vehicle charging station owner-
operators;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company must develop a transparent, points-based
scoring rubric to prioritize make-ready program applications in collaboration with electric
vehicle charging service providers and other industry stakeholders;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company must allocate funds to make-ready
program applications already received by the Company that meet the updated make-ready
program rules and requirements;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company must develop, provide, and apply
transparent siting criteria that include specific geographic minimum distances from an existing or
proposed third party electric vehicle charging station for its Community Charging program;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power Company must provide a list of its proposed
Community Charging stations, updated on at least a quarterly basis, to inform the public of
locations it has identified as potential gaps in private charging deployment;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power must allow private sector providers the opportunity
to develop a proposed station where the private sector charging provider informs the Company
that it is building or intends to build a public charging station within the proposed limits of one
of Georgia Power’s proposed locations, and to remove the site from its Community Charging
program;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power must develop a pricing proposal for its Community
Charging program subject to stakeholder input—as a compliance filing within 30 days following
a final order in this case—to align its Community Charging pricing with the competitive market,
and;

ORDERED FURTHER, that Georgia Power’s proposed CIEV rider is approved, subject to the
modification that the Company must make the rate available to separately-metered charging
stations installed before January 1, 2023.

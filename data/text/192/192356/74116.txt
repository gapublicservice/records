POST-HEARING BRIEF OF THE COMMERCIAL GROUP

Pursuant to the procedural and scheduling order of the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”), the Commercial Group hereby respectfully files its post-hearing brief in the above-captioned docket.  In this brief, the Commercial Group focuses on four issues: 1) the Commission should move class rates closer to parity; 2) the Commission should reject calls to radically restructure Real-Time Pricing (“RTP”) rates; 3) the Commission should set a return on equity (“ROE”) for Georgia Power that would balance the interests of ratepayers and the utility and be low enough to help Georgia remain competitive in attracting and keeping businesses in the state; and 4) any earnings band should be directly tied to the ROE set in this proceeding.
SUMMARY
The Commercial Group is an ad hoc association of large commercial customers of Georgia Power that operate in a very competitive business environment. Therefore, since electricity is a significant operating cost, any increase in electric rates puts pressure on consumer prices and operations. Direct Testimony of Steve W. Chriss (“Chriss Direct”), p.3. 
A key theme in this case has been maintaining Georgia as a business-friendly state. One way to do this is by ensuring that electric rates are designed equitably. Right now, Medium Business and Large Business class rates are substantially above-cost (i.e., above rate parity) and should be moved toward parity. Further, such moves toward parity should not be undone by increasing the Medium Business class rates that are farthest above parity to subsidize an expansion of lower-cost TOU-FD rates. Instead, if the Commission expands TOU-FD eligibility to include unaffiliated restaurants, the Commission should contain any revenue erosion from such expansion to the TOU-FD rate itself. This is an uncontested issue as the only advocate for the TOU-FD change (Utility Management Services or “UMS”) has already agreed to contain any such revenue erosion to the TOU-FD rate. 
A second way to maintain Georgia as a business-friendly state is to reject the attempt to saddle Georgia businesses with hundreds of millions of dollars in new cost from a radical restructuring of the successful RTP rate schedule. Indeed, many businesses have moved to Georgia or expanded Georgia operations based on the RTP rate and changing it from a marginal-pricing to embedded-pricing rate would undercut Georgia’s ability to compete while upsetting settled contract expectations.
A third way to maintain Georgia as a business-friendly state is to allow Georgia Power sufficient revenue to provide safe, reliable electric service while keeping rates competitive with those in surrounding states. With a huge amount of Vogtle capital costs coming online as early as this year, setting Georgia Power’s ROE too high could harm Georgia’s ability to remain competitive with neighboring states. The Commercial Group has presented a number of objective yardsticks for which to judge the reasonableness of ROE testimony and urges the Commission to adopt an ROE and earnings band mechanism that would balance the interests of Georgia Power and its ratepayers.

ARGUMENT
Fairness in Rate Design
 The Commission should move class rates closer to parity
No party has challenged the fact that Power & Light – Medium (“PLM”) rates in particular and Medium Business class rates in general are significantly above cost and should move closer to cost. In fact, while the rate of return from Total Retail is 6.31 percent, the return from PLM is 9.43 percent. Georgia Power Exh. _ LPE-2 (pages 5, 8). Given that PLM load constitutes 90 percent of all Medium Business class load, it is not surprising that the Medium Business class return is also very high, at 9.35 percent, the highest return from any rate group. Georgia Power Exh. __ LPE-1 (pages 3-4); Chriss Direct, Table 3, page 16. The return from the Large Business class is high as well, at 8.66 percent. Id.
Further, although Georgia Power proposed an across-the-board rate increase in direct testimony, it stated in rebuttal testimony that it is “supportive of general movement toward rate parity,” so long as it is done on a rate group basis. Evans Legg Rebuttal Testimony, page 4, line 14. As such, moving Medium Business rates closer to cost is now an uncontested issue. The only witness presenting an overall move to parity analysis was witness Pollock for the industrial group GMA. He proposed that the Medium Business and Large Business classes should receive a base increase of 77 percent of the average retail revenue increase. GAM Pollock Direct, page 34 (Table 7). The Commercial Group recommends that the Commission adopt Mr. Pollock’s parity adjustment proposal.
Finally, moving PLM rates toward rate parity should not be undone by forcing PLM customers to subsidize an expansion of TOU-FD. Three rate schedules in the Medium Business class, PLM, TOU-GSD and TOU-FD, produce returns on investment of 9.53 percent, 11.71 percent, and 7.26 percent, respectively. Georgia Power Exh. _ LPE-2 (pages 5-8). Clearly, TOU-FD is the lowest-cost rate in the Medium Business class. UMS argued in the Direct Testimony of Brian Coughlan that the TOU-FD rate should be opened to new customers on a first-come, first-serve basis. The Commercial Group takes no position on this proposal. But, if the Commission decides to expand access to the TOU-FD rate, then revenue erosion caused by this expansion of TOU-FD should not undermine moves toward parity. Instead, as the Commission ordered in the 2019 rate case (Stipulation ¶26) and 2010 rate case (Stipulation ¶19) with respect to TOU-MB - the predecessor to the TOU-FD rate - the Commission should contain any revenue erosion from such expansion to the TOU-FD rate itself. Further, UMS has so agreed in emails of November 7, 2022 to counsel for Georgia Power and the Commercial Group, making this another uncontested issue. 
  The proposal to radically reshape RTP rates should be rejected
A legal structure unique to Georgia is the large load exception to monopoly utility service whereby businesses have a one-time choice of electric suppliers. This law is a key feature making Georgia a favorable place to do business with the RTP rate helping Georgia Power compete for this load. Under the RTP rate, businesses accept the risk of fluctuating marginal costs and contribute to Georgia Power’s fixed costs, thereby benefiting all ratepayers. Indeed, when natural gas prices spiked this year, RTP customers had to bear these higher marginal electric costs in real time. GMA Pollock Direct, pages 7-8. Further, pursuant to fixed cost RTP adders, RTP customers made a higher contribution to fixed cost as well; through the first half of 2022 alone, the contribution RTP rates made to fixed cost exceeded projections by $96 million. Id. at 9. And, according to Georgia Power’s cost studies, the Marginal rate group and RTP rates provide a return higher than retail load overall, even before the recent RTP cost increases are factored in GP Exh. __ (LPE-1, pages 3-4, LPE-2, pages 4-6).
Nevertheless, Staff witness Watkins proposed to shift a whopping $150 million of additional cost onto Georgia RTP businesses (see Table 14, page 54, of his direct testimony), as only the first step of such whopping increases. Over the years, Mr. Watkins has made many unsuccessful attempts at turning the marginal RTP rate into an embedded cost rate. In this case, he targets the ECCR mechanism, proposing to apply embedded cost for past coal activities to real-time marginal pricing and apply the ECCR charge on a per-kwh basis instead of the long-established percentage-bill basis. He does so even though the ECCR mechanism is a well-settled rate mechanism, created first in the 2007 rate case. Notably, that ECCR mechanism was agreed to as reasonable by Staff. See Final Order, Docket 25060, page 4 (Dec. 18, 2007). This mechanism was continued in each rate case since then, all via stipulation with Staff. But now, Staff witness Watkins claims the ECCR mechanism is not reasonable. Watkins Direct, page 51, line 5. Further, whereas Mr. Watkins testified in prior cases that $32 million (2013) or even $23 million (2010) of new RTP cost would create rate shock, his proposal in this case would shift five to seven times those amounts onto Georgia businesses, and do so as only a “first step” toward the ultimate destruction of the successful RTP rate. Watkins Direct, page 53, line 16. The Commission should reject this radical proposal out of hand, as it did with respect to Mr. Watkins’ previous RTP proposals.  

Fairness in Revenues
 The Commission should adopt an ROE that is closer to the national average than either extreme of the ROEs proposed in this proceeding. 

Over and over again in written and oral testimony, Georgia Power witnesses argued that this Georgia commission must follow the direction of New York financiers. According to this theory, even though Georgia Power ratings have been harmed by its own actions in Vogtle construction delays and cost overruns, the Commission nevertheless must award Georgia Power an 11.0 percent ROE, the highest ROE of any electric utility in the past few years, so as to strengthen Georgia Power’s ratings. Georgia Power even goes so far as to claim that Georgia Power ratepayers are benefited from such a rich ROE. Georgia’s businesses disagree. Such a rich ROE would add approximately $100 million in revenue requirement from the current 10.5 percent ROE. Chriss Direct, page 11, lines 10-17. It would also increase the revenue requirement by approximately $300 million per year over what an average ROE for vertically integrated utilities would require. Id. at pages 13-14. 
But what if the Commission wants to provide Georgia Power a higher than average return? As described further below, the Commission could award Georgia Power an ROE of 10.0 percent, which would be the 5th highest ROE awarded in the past five years (out of nearly 100 ROE decisions). Nov. 9, 2022 hearing (Day 2), 8:46. Surely, investors would not see such a rich top-five ROE as a departure from the Commission’s credit-supportive history. Plus, Georgia Power’s residential and business customers would receive some rate relief in this period of sharply escalating electric rates. This would be the kind of balancing of utility and ratepayer interests that is required by law. 
At the last moment of the hearings, Georgia Power introduced new evidence of recent ROEs. Mr. Coyne agreed that the average ROE for vertically integrated utilities over the first nine months of 2022 is 9.35 percent. Nov. 29, 2022 hearing Part 2, 2:05. But, he then suggested that more recent ROEs have trended upward at 9.5, 9.9, 10.1, 10.15, 10.2, 10.25, 10.3 and 10.8 percent. Id. Of course, at this final stage of the hearings there was no opportunity to rebut this late testimony. In fact, however, only two of these seven cited decisions are on point and they have an average ROE of 9.7 percent. 
First, three of the decisions Mr. Coyne cited are simply continuations of three California utilities’ existing ROEs via utility filings seeking to maintain existing ROE levels per a California-specific mechanism. These three decisions, all made on November 3, 2022, are not new ROEs that have been set. Two decisions cited by Mr. Coyne are simply limited-issue proceedings in Florida involving automatic adjustments from prior rate orders. Incidentally, Mr. Coyne conveniently omitted other limited-issue electric decisions with lower ROEs (both 9.35 percent) from the same month of October, 2022. The remaining two decisions cited by Mr. Coyne are the only two new rate decisions. The first decision set an ROE of 9.5 percent for Kingsport Power of Tennessee on a 48.90 percent equity ratio and the second decision set an ROE of 9.9 percent for DTE Electric Co. of Michigan on a 39.62 percent equity ratio. The average ROE of these two decisions is 9.7 percent and the average equity ratio is 44.26 percent, all far below the 11.0 percent ROE and 56.0 percent equity ratio proposed by Georgia Power.

The Commercial Group recommends that the Commission use these ROE yardsticks and set an ROE that is within this 9.35-10.0 percent range. Thus, if the Commission wants to provide Georgia Power with a rich ROE, it could set ROE at the top quartile of all integrated electric utilities at 9.9 percent or even at a top-5 ROE of 10.0 percent. Either of these ROEs clearly would provide Georgia Power with a reasonable return commensurate with investor expectations. Plus, either would help Georgia remain an attractive place in which to do business, while maintaining the Commission’s supportive rating by New York rating agencies.
Ultimately, New York financiers should not be allowed to force Georgia businesses to backstop Georgia Power’s checkered Vogtle construction record with an ROE of 11.0 percent, which would be the highest ROE in the nation. For all these reasons, the Commission should adopt an ROE that would balance the interests of ratepayers and the utility and be low enough to help Georgia remain competitive in attracting and keeping businesses in the state.
 Any Alternate Rate Plan should have an earnings band tied to the approved ROE 

Georgia Power indicated an Alternative Rate Plan (“ARP”) could only be adopted with its express approval. To date, no revenue settlement has been reached in this proceeding. In the absence of an ARP, Georgia Power’s rates will be set based on a traditional test-year approach. However, to the extent that an ARP is offered to Georgia Power, the Commercial Group believes any ARP earnings band should be directly tied to the ROE set in this proceeding. Chriss Direct, pages 8-9. For example, the Company proposed an ROE band of 9.5 percent to 12 percent, which is a range of 150 basis points below the proposed ROE of 11 percent to 100 basis points above the proposed ROE.  If the Commission approves a lower ROE in this docket than that proposed by the Company, the Commission should contemporaneously reduce the low and high band boundaries and maintain the basis point spread of the band around the approved ROE. Thus, if the Commission approves an ROE of 10 percent, the band would then be from 8.5 percent to 11 percent. Such a mechanism would balance the interests of Georgia Power and its ratepayers.

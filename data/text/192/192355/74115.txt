POST-HEARING BRIEF OF GEORGIA INTERFAITH POWER & LIGHT
Pursuant to Rule 515-2-1-.04 of the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”) and the Commission’s 2022 Procedural and Scheduling Order in the above captioned proceeding, Georgia Interfaith Power & Light (“GIPL”) respectfully files this Post-Hearing Brief.
INTRODUCTION
In June 2022, Georgia Power Company (“GPC,” “Georgia Power,” “the Company”) filed a rate case that seeks to recover $2.9 billion from customers over the three-year rate plan period. GPC’s proposal would raise their average residential customer’s electricity bill by about $16 per month. Critically, this rate hike is only the first of many to come over the next few years in Georgia. Customers will see their bills go up at least three times in the coming year: (1) January 1, 2023 when the current rate case increase hits customer bills; (2) Spring 2023 when Plant Vogtle Unit 3 becomes operational; and (3) Summer 2023 when fuel cost increases due to high gas prices hit customer bills. Shortly thereafter, in early 2024, customer bills will spike again when Plant Vogtle Unit 4 is operational and the Commission determines how much of the Company’s billions in cost overruns will be paid for by its customers.    
These successive and compounding rate hikes come at a time when GPC customers are struggling with record inflation, high fuel prices, and economic instability. Rather than finding a way to manage rising costs and consider the impact of yet another rate hike on customers, Georgia Power seems concerned primarily with protecting its sole shareholder, Southern Company, by increasing its already market-defying return on equity to 11%.
At the same time, Georgia Power proposes rate designs that limit customers’ control over their energy bills while padding Company profits: Specifically, Georgia Power seeks to forcibly expand customer reliance on a complicated and expensive residential demand charge rate, Schedule TOU-RD, and the Company maintains strong opposition to proposals to expand the popular monthly net metering program for customers that invest in rooftop solar. This simply isn’t fair. Customers have the right to buy less electricity from Georgia Power. And, when customers buy less, they should be able to actually pay less on monthly bills. 
SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATIONS
The Commission has an opportunity to significantly limit bill increases and ensure customers have much needed tools to combat rising electric bills. To do so, GIPL respectfully requests that the Commission: 
Adopt Staff Witness Gorman’s recommendation that the Company’s ROE be set at 9.45%, with an earnings band spanning 9.00% to 9.90%, and with a 51% common equity ratio for 2024 and 2025. 
Return to using the basic volumetric residential tariff, Schedule R, as the default rate and halt use of the complicated demand charge tariff, Schedule TOU-RD, as the default tariff for new residential premises. In the alternative, if the Commission prefers a time-varying rate as the default for new premises, it should set the time-of-use nights & weekends tariff, Schedule TOU-REO. 
Retain the basic volumetric residential tariff, Schedule R, and the time-of-use nights & weekends tariff, Schedule TOU-REO, options for residential customers and reject Georgia Power’s request to eliminate these tariffs. 
Reject Georgia Power’s attempts to force future RNR customers onto the complicated demand charge tariff, Schedule TOU-RD. 
Reject Georgia Power’s requested $1.4 million revenue adjustment related to the Company’s monthly netting program. 
Expand the popular monthly netting program for rooftop solar customers. The Commission can limit participation to 3% of the Company’s 2021 peak load and revisiting the program when participation approaches the limit. 
Reduce the residential community solar monthly subscription price to $20 per month per share, in line with Staff’s testimony. 
Reject the Company’s request to increase the basic service charge for residential customers. 
Require the Company to continue filing customer arrearage and disconnection data as it was required to do following the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.  
DISCUSSION
Rejecting Georgia Power’s Unlawful Sky-High Profit Proposal and Adopting a More Reasonable Alternative.
Georgia Power has proposed an authorized return on equity (“ROE”) of 11% and a credit structure of 56% at a time when most Georgians are struggling with rising costs for food and fuel, and after years of its customers paying for shockingly high profits for the Company’s shareholders. GPC’s proposal amounts to an exploitative, unjust, and unreasonable rate hike, because while GPC put forward plenty of evidence that high profit returns would be good for its credit scores (a contention that no one could dispute), it offered little to no analysis on the customer impact of those choices. This Commission must balance Georgia Power’s interests with “the consumer interest in being charged non-exploitative rates:” 
In addition to prohibiting rates so low as to be confiscatory, the holding of Hope Natural Gas makes clear that exploitative rates are illegal as well. … A regulated utility has no constitutional right to a profit.
 We urge this Commission to consider not just GPC’s wish list for profits, but to consider the customers, too, as legally required when setting just and reasonable rates. Staff’s ROE and capital structure recommendations strike this balance, and we urge the Commission to adopt them. 
It is critical to note that GPC’s analysis has several major flaws which inflate their recommendation to the detriment of customers: 
GPC’s profit recommendation will unnecessarily pad investor profits at the expense of customers. 
GPC’s Chief Financial Officer, Mr. Abramovitz, explained that every half of a percent of ROE authorized for this company will cost customers roughly $100,000,000. And as Vogtle expenses are folded into the rate base next year, the price tag for each percent of ROE will soar. 
1% of ROE = 200 MILLION DOLLARS IN BILLS 
(BEFORE Vogtle)
If the true goal of setting the ROE is to identify a return that will maintain a company’s financial integrity while attracting equity, then GIPL Expert Witness Mark Ellis has demonstrated that those goals can be met while saving customers one billion dollars per year. Mr. Ellis’ testimony considered together the concepts that the Company professes to espouse, but which it carefully silos in its analysis. Mr. Ellis considered how market-required equity returns and capital structure combine to change the amount of cash on hand the Company holds to satisfy credit rating agencies. Instead of dealing with this level of complex consideration, the Company evaded accountability and transparency, presenting this Commission with hardly any information about which profit levers it really needs and which ones will hurt its customers' pocketbooks. We understand that this exercise is complex and frankly, may be difficult for the Company – but Georgia Power can do difficult things. If it chooses. Its customers certainly do.   
The Supreme Court is clear: Just and reasonable equity returns require balancing the interests of GPC’s ratepayers with those of its investors. The Commission should reject GPC’s proposed exploitative ROE and capital structure and instead approve Staff’s generous, but far more reasonable, ROE and capital structure that considers impacts on ratepayers and more accurately accounts for the costs of capital.
This Commission must balance customer and investor interests, but GPC’s profit recommendation only considers how much money investors will earn. 
To meet the standard of “just and reasonable rates,” this Commission must balance the interests of the customers and the investors. For that analysis, the Commission must look to Staff and Intervenor expert recommendations, because GPC’s expert on ROE and credit structure, Mr. Coyne, admits that he did no analysis of the burden on customers: 
Ms. Whitfield [GIPL]:  … [S]peaking of the balancing test, did you calculate the burden on customers before providing your recommendation? 
A [Mr. Coyne/GPC]:No. It’s not part of the cost of capital analysis. Mine is a market-based determination. … It is not designed to determine the impact on customers. Although, of course, it does have an impact on customers. 
Even GPC admits that there are fair and reasonable returns on equity below 11%. 
Mr. Coyne admitted that his “market-based analysis” yielded returns on equity that are lower than his 11% and still “fair and reasonable.” And although he has provided a range of recommended options in other proceedings for other utilities, he chose not to testify to his range of reasonable options for this Commission. But Company Witness Coyne’s models for GPC produced cost of equity estimates as low as 8.99%. 
High returns are needed to compensate investors for funding risky utilities; Georgia Power is financially sound and, according to their own expert, “an average risk utility.” 
Mr. Coyne acknowledges a maxim of finance: riskier investments require higher returns. But Mr. Coyne determined that GPC is “an average risk utility.” At the same time, he recommended that GPC have an authorized ROE that outpaces not only other average risk utilities, but all utilities of any risk level. GPC’s current ROE of 10.5% is already over 1% ($200 million for customers) higher than the average for vertically integrated electric utilities. Mr. Coyne’s proposed ROE of 11% would far exceed other “average risk utilities,” and he offers no argument that GPC carries an above-average risk that would justify an above-average return.  
By looking at ROE and capital structure as independent factors, GPC failed to present this Commission with options that would allow the Company to access financing while minimizing the burden on ratepayers. 
Mr. Coyne agreed that it’s important to look at the capital structure and cost of equity together. This is because the credit impact on a utility is determined by both the ROE and the capital structure. But Mr. Coyne did not evaluate these two metrics together. He looked at the capital structures among his group of proxy utilities, but he did not consider each utility’s capital structure in relationship to its cost of equity. Alternatively, the approach suggested by Mr. Ellis – which does consider these factors in combination – would save customers money while still allowing the Company to sufficiently attract equity investors and access credit. 
A just and reasonable rate may not be so low that it is confiscatory, but none of the proposals before this Commission even approach confiscatory. Indeed, the lowest expert-supported ROE proposal before this Commission, which would save customers billions over the three-year rate plan period, was shown to provide investors with an adequate return and less risk compared to other major investment vehicles.
GIPL agrees that the Commission should provide Georgia Power a return on equity that allows it to efficiently access financial markets, but it must do so at a rate that minimizes the burden on Georgia Power ratepayers. The Company’s proposal is primarily concerned with its shareholder. This Commission must balance that concern against the interests of ratepayers. 
Retaining Key Residential Rate Options and Rejecting Georgia Power’s Proposals to Push More Customers onto its Demand Charge Tariff. 
The Commission should reject Georgia Power’s efforts to push more residential customers  onto the complicated and expensive residential demand charge tariff and to limit options to new residential premises moving forward. Specifically, GIPL recommends that the Commission: 
Retain the basic volumetric residential tariff, Schedule R, for new premises moving forward;
Retain the time-of-use nights & weekends tariff, Schedule TOU-REO, for new and existing customers moving forward; 
Halt use of the complicated demand charge tariff, Schedule TOU-RD, as the default rate for new residential premises and revert to using the basic volumetric residential tariff for new premises; or, in the alternative, make the time-of-use nights & weekends tariff, Schedule TOU-REO, the default rate for new premises; and
Reject the Company’s request to force all future Schedule RNR rooftop solar customers onto the complicated demand charge tariff; instead, continue allowing future Schedule RNR participants to choose their residential rate. 
Staff and numerous intervenors all proposed the same or similar recommendations. Staff, GIPL, and other intervenors all submitted extensive testimony demonstrating that Schedule TOU-RD has been significantly more expensive for customers than other available rates and that demand rates are inferior to volumetric time-of-use rates for providing efficient and actionable price signals to residential customers. For example, GIPL Expert Witness Barnes demonstrated that Georgia Power’s TOU-RD customers paid on average about $16/month more than they would have on the Schedule R tariff. Mr. Barnes’s analysis showed that on an annual basis, the TOU-RD rate is roughly 4.8 cents/kWh higher than the average of other rates – a jarring and significant deviation considering that Georgia Power’s average residential rate is about 13.9 cents/kWh. 
Furthermore, GIPL strongly opposes Georgia Power’s proposal to force all future rooftop solar customers that participate in the RNR tariff onto its TOU-RD rate. Under both state and federal law, Georgia Power is prohibited from discriminating against any particular group of customers. O.C.G.A. § 46-3-11 (“Every electric supplier is prohibited from having or applying any rate, charge, or service rule or regulation which unreasonably discriminates against or in favor of . . . any class of its customers as opposed to another class of customers for such purposes . . .”); 16 U.S.C. § 824a-3 (utility rates for sale to qualifying facilities “shall be just and reasonable and in the public interest, and . . . shall not discriminate against the qualifying cogenerators or qualifying small power producers”).  
Staff and Intervenors offered numerous reasons why the proposal should be rejected. For instance, demand charge tariffs like TOU-RD reduce the amount of money a customer can save by investing in rooftop solar. Further, demand charge tariffs tend to put lower and average electricity users at a disadvantage, making them less beneficial to solar customers who have lower electricity demands. Georgia Power even proposes forcing non-residential future RNR customers onto demand charge tariffs absent any analysis at all.
Importantly, at the heart of Georgia Power’s proposal to force solar customers onto its demand charge tariff was a deeply flawed revenue erosion analysis. Mr. Barnes pointed to an error in the Company’s analysis that inflated the alleged revenue erosion by 63%. That mistake, coupled with many other significant analytical errors that inflated the alleged revenue problem, make the Company’s analysis wholly unreliable.
Expanding Monthly Netting for Rooftop Solar Customers. 
The Commission should direct Georgia Power to expand its monthly netting option under its RNR tariff, effective January 1, 2023. Before the Commission created a limited monthly netting program, there were only about 1,000 customers participating in the Company’s RNR tariff.  Now, following implementation of the popular monthly netting program, there are more than 7,200 participants in the RNR tariff (only 5,000 of whom are eligible for monthly netting). The program was a success and should be expanded. 
In its final order in the 2022 Georgia Power IRP, the Commission adopted a motion by Commissioner Shaw that read: 
Rather than continuing to try to address rooftop solar with a series of temporary and partial measures where some customers receive net metering while others receive only avoided cost or, in some cases, nothing at all, I move that the Commission direct Staff and the Company to each address the appropriate structure and pricing of the RNR-10 tariff in the current rate case so that the Commission can move towards a fair and long term way of addressing the issue. 
In this IRP, we simply do not have the cost information necessary to set just and reasonable rates, terms, and conditions for rooftop solar. In addition, we do not have the information to determine whether or how such rates, terms, and conditions would impact other customers. Under my motion, the parties in the rate case would have the opportunity to provide, test, and challenge cost-based data and the Commission would have more options than simply deciding whether or not to continue temporarily doling out net metering to a subset of customers.
Several parties responded to the Commission’s IRP directive during this Rate Case. Unsurprisingly, Georgia Power strongly opposed any expansion of the monthly netting program. But many intervenors support expansion of the program. GIPL offered expert testimony in support of expanding the program, as did many others: the Georgia Solar Energy Industries Association, the Solar Energy Industries Association, Vote Solar, the Georgia Coalition for Local Governments, the Southern Alliance for Clean Energy, Southface Energy Institute, and the Georgia Solar Energy Association. The Commission’s Public Interest Advocacy Staff testified in support of continued growth of customer owned generation (e.g., rooftop solar) for three reasons: (1) customer owned generation offers customers a way to control their electric bills; (2) it supports Georgia’s transition to clean energy; and (3) it reduces Georgia Power’s net loads, requiring fewer assets to be built by the Company and paid for by all customers. Staff offered a range of possible policy choices for the Commission, one of which was the full retail rate – meaning, Staff is supportive of expanding the existing monthly netting program.
The Cogen Act Requires Georgia Power to Offer Monthly Netting.
The RNR tariff is this Commission’s means of implementing Georgia’s Cogeneration and Distributed Generation Act of 2001 (“the Cogen Act”). O.C.G.A. § 46-3-50 et seq. This state law ensures that customers who have made significant private investments in on-site solar generation receive fair credit and compensation for their system’s production. 
The Company’s instantaneous netting methodology conflicts with the plain language of the statute, which requires the monthly netting of electricity. Wherever a distributed generation facility is connected on the customer’s side of the meter – as is generally the case with rooftop solar – the Cogen Act requires Georgia Power to “measure the electricity produced or consumed during the billing period, in accordance with normal metering practices using bidirectional metering.” Customers receive their bills on a monthly or near-monthly basis. As a matter of customer experience and common sense, the billing period is the interval over which bills are rendered – the month. The Act therefore requires monthly netting – i.e., at the end of the billing period, the Company is required to net the customer’s total exports to the grid against their total consumption from the grid.  
Monthly netting allows solar customers to properly receive full value from their private investments. For example, if a solar customer consumed 1000 kWh from the grid over the month but exported 250 kWh, the customer’s final bill would be based on 750 kWh of net consumption. Under the Company’s current practice, by contrast, the customer would be billed for 1000 kWh at the retail rate while receiving the much lower solar avoided cost rate for their system’s excess production. A typical Georgia Power customer on the R tariff with a 6 kW solar array loses several hundred dollars in value per year under instantaneous netting. 
Under monthly netting, a customer’s solar production has the same value whether the customer consumes it (thereby avoiding a retail purchase) or their neighbor does. Considering that the Company receives the full retail rate for solar electrons consumed by the neighbor (after having purchased those electrons wholesale), monthly netting ensures fair treatment of solar customers while leaving Georgia Power no worse off. Monthly netting is better for the customer, and the enhanced value proposition it affords will in turn benefit Georgia’s solar installers and create more solar jobs. Monthly netting therefore advances two express purposes of the Cogen Act: to “[e]ncourage private investment in renewable energy resources” and to “[s]timulate the economic growth of Georgia.”
The Cogen Act makes a distinction between how energy flow is measured and how it is paid for. Measuring electricity produced or consumed during the billing period is only the first step. What follows is a comparison between the “electricity supplied by the electric service provider” and the “electricity generated by the customer’s distributed generation system.” This comparison yields one of two outcomes: either net consumption, in which case “the electricity shall be billed by the electric service provider, in accordance with tariffs filed with the commission,” or net production, in which case the customer “[s]hall be credited for the excess kilowatt-hours generated during the billing period at an agreed to rate as filed with the commission, with this kilowatt-hour credit appearing on the bill for the billing period.” 
If instantaneous netting were permissible, the statute would mandate separate calculations of imports and exports instead of what it actually does, which is to direct that imports and exports be compared to determine which exceeds the other before assigning a value to the net. When considering the meaning of a statute, the Commission “must presume that the General Assembly meant what it said and said what it meant.” The Commission must afford the statute its plain and ordinary meaning, reading the text in the “most natural and reasonable way, as an ordinary speaker of the English language would.” Georgia Power’s reading supplies language that isn’t in the statute while ignoring language that is. For that reason, it must be rejected. 
Thus, the Commission should find that the monthly netting program is consistent with Georgia law and expand the program on that basis. 
Evidence Supports a Policy Decision Expanding Monthly Netting.
Numerous intervenors and Staff provided substantial evidence that weighs in favor of expanding monthly netting for Georgia Power customers. Specifically, GIPL Witness Barnes proposed expanding the monthly netting program with two guardrails to address concerns expressed by the Commission: (1) Limit participation in the monthly netting program to 3% of the Company’s 2021 peak load, with the Commission revisiting the program when participation approaches the limit, and (2) require residential customers that participate to pay a minimum bill of $22.34 per month, in line with the Company’s own representation of customer-related costs in its cost of service study. 
As Mr. Barnes testified, at least 38 states currently offer some form of net metering (annual or monthly). The Company’s attempts to cherry pick jurisdictions that have changed net metering policies ignores how widespread net metering remains today and fails to meaningfully respond to testimony that jurisdictions transitioning away from annual retail rate net metering have significantly higher penetrations of rooftop solar than we have here in Georgia. Staff aptly notes that Georgia ranks 37th for the capacity of behind the meter solar installed, behind states like Nevada and Arkansas. Expanding monthly netting will help Georgia catch up to neighbors and ideally, to become a leader like we are in utility scale solar. 
During his testimony, Mr. Barnes was asked several questions by Chairwoman Pridemore and Georgia Power’s counsel Mr. Hewitson about rooftop solar policies in North Carolina. GIPL counsel asked for an opportunity to clarify confusion in the record and the Chair requested that GIPL address the issue in briefing.  Following up on that exchange from the hearing, Mr. Barnes accurately testified that in North Carolina both Duke utilities still offers retail rate net metering. Under those retail rate net metering policies, Duke North Carolina customers participating in the rooftop solar program can offset retail rate purchases with exported solar and even roll excess solar production over to the next month at the retail rate. 
Mr. Barnes was asked questions about North Carolina House Bill 589, a bill he is familiar with. Under H.B. 589, current retail rate net metering policies are allowed to remain in place unchanged until the end of 2026 under North Carolina law (H.B. 589).  However, H.B. 589 requires public utilities to file revised net metering rates and states that the “rates shall be nondiscriminatory and established only after an investigation of the costs and benefits of customer-sited generation. …” Nothing in H.B. 589 requires net metering rates to switch to avoided cost crediting for solar exports.
In 2021, Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress in North Carolina filed proposed successor tariffs with the North Carolina Utilities Commission in NCUC Docket No. E-100 Sub 180. Notably, multiple intervenors in this Georgia Power rate case were signatories to a settlement with Duke in support of the net metering successor tariff, including for instance, the Southern Environmental Law Center on behalf of Vote Solar and the Southern Alliance for Clean Energy; and the Solar Energy Industries Association. Critically important, as Mr. Barnes testified, the proposed revised net metering tariffs still allow solar exports to offset at the retail rate within time-of-use periods. Thus, the Duke utilities in North Carolina provide an example of neighboring, peer utilities that currently offer annual retail rate net metering and have proposed moving to a form of monthly net metering. 
Georgia Power’s claims alleging cost shift from rooftop solar customers (monthly netting and instantaneous netting customers alike) are unpersuasive and lack adequate support. Without an actual study of the costs to serve solar customers, Georgia Power cannot claim that a cost shift exists. Mr. Barnes offered an example from Dominion South Carolina: 
When I actually evaluated the company’s cost of service and applied the values [ ] that solar provides for reduction in coincident peaks at the transmission, generation, and distribution level, as it turns out, there was no cost shift at all. Other residential customers were benefiting, or at most, impacted in a de minimis way by having solar customers within their class because they were allocated lower costs due to the fact that these solar customers were reducing the allocators for things like production demand and transmission demand. 
He went on to explain “That’s exactly why we have to do these analyses. You can’t just kind of throw something together. You have to do the homework.” And Georgia Power hasn’t done its homework here. They’ve instead merely claimed that if solar customers buy less of their product, it’s a problem because they bring in less revenue. 
Absent a thorough analysis, GIPL urges the Commission to disregard the Company’s cost shift allegations, reject its request to make a $1.4 million per year revenue adjustment to compensate itself for the monthly netting program, and expand the popular monthly netting program. 
Setting a More Reasonable Subscription Price for Community Solar. 
GIPL urges the Commission to lower the residential customer subscription price for the Company’s community solar program to $20 per month per block. Staff testified that many community solar programs are intended to allow customers to realize the benefits of rooftop solar even if they may not be able to or may not wish to install solar at their home. But Georgia Power’s program is priced as a premium product and Georgia Power proposes to increase the price even more. Rather than effectively pricing its program based on lost revenue, the Company could base its program pricing on actual costs (or revenue requirement). Staff calculated that a residential price of $20/month per block would be sufficient to allow the Company to earn a profit, and it would cover all of the Company’s costs. Adopting Staff’s recommended alternative community solar pricing would make the program more affordable and more attractive to customers.  
Retaining the Existing Basic Service Charge for Residential Customers. 
The Commission should deny the Company’s proposed basic service charge increase for residential customers because the current charge already recovers costs in excess of the Company’s truly customer-related costs, and the Company’s cost of service analysis shows a decrease rather than an increase in customer-related costs.
Monitoring Customers Disconnections and Arrearages During a Time of Rising Electric Rates and Bills.
When the pandemic hit in 2020, a Commission order required Georgia Power to publicly file data on customer arrearages and disconnections for the first time. The data is critical to understanding how customers are faring, especially during a time of significant electric rate and bill increases. For example, the most recent filing for October 2022 shows that more than 24,000 residential customers were disconnected for nonpayment and nearly 15,000 residential customers are more than 90 days behind on electric bills. In that same single month – October 2022 – Georgia Power issued more than 420,000 disconnection notices to residential customers. Thus, the Commission should order Georgia Power to continue filing this critical monthly data on customer arrearages and disconnections as it has been doing for the last couple of years.

For the foregoing reasons, we urge the Commission to adopt GIPL’s recommendations outlined above.

           A. The Cogen Act Requires Georgia Power to Offer Monthly Netting.

           The RNR tariff is this Commission’s means of implementing Georgia’s Cogeneration and

Distributed Generation Act of 2001 (“the Cogen Act”). O.C.G.A. § 46-3-50 et seq. This state law

ensures that customers who have made significant private investments in on-site solar generation

receive fair credit and compensation for their system’s production.

           The Company’s instantaneous netting methodology conflicts with the plain language of the

statute, which requires the monthly netting of electricity. Wherever a distributed generation facility

is connected on the customer’s side of the meter – as is generally the case with rooftop solar – the

Cogen Act requires Georgia Power to “measure the electricity produced or consumed during the

billing period, in accordance with normal metering practices using bidirectional metering.” 33

Customers receive their bills on a monthly or near-monthly basis. As a matter of customer

experience and common sense, the billing period is the interval over which bills are rendered – the

month. The Act therefore requires monthly netting – i.e., at the end of the billing period, the

Company is required to net the customer’s total exports to the grid against their total consumption

from the grid.

           Monthly netting allows solar customers to properly receive full value from their private

investments. For example, if a solar customer consumed 1000 kWh from the grid over the month

but exported 250 kWh, the customer’s final bill would be based on 750 kWh of net consumption.

Under the Company’s current practice, by contrast, the customer would be billed for 1000 kWh at

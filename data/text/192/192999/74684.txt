STF-WG-8-4

Question:

Refer to the Transcript of Hearings November 9, 2022, page 1859, line 15, in which the Company’s attorney said “55 percent of the failed transformers over that time period would not have been identified for replacement through the objective tests.” 
Does this statement mean, or imply, that objective testing of power transformers is not reliable?  If not, please explain the meaning of the statement.
Provide any studies, research, articles, or other support for the implication that dissolved gas analysis or other objective power transformer tests are unreliable, or that power transformer age is a better predictor of failure than objective testing of power transformers.

Response:

No, the Company’s counsel did not intend this statement to imply that objective testing is not reliable.  Consistent with the Company’s response to STF-WG-8-3 part (b), this statement supports Georgia Power’s position that objective testing alone should not be solely relied upon to determine the appropriate time for equipment replacement, since objective tests cannot always accurately predict when equipment failure will occur on the system. For example, as cited by the Company’s counsel, 55% of the transformers that failed between 2012 and 2021, passed the most recent objective test conducted but nonetheless failed in service. As stated previously, due to the large number of transmission assets that are approaching or beyond Company expected life by the end of the Grid Investment Plan period, Georgia Power has determined that transformer age is an appropriate proxy for equipment replacement as part of the Grid Investment Plan.

The Company is unaware of any such research or studies. However, according to common understanding, the likelihood of equipment failure increases as an asset ages beyond its expected life. The Company determined that increased catastrophic equipment failures could occur in the absence of a proactive replacement approach. As explained in the Company’s response to STF-WG-2-25, catastrophic equipment failure is more costly for customers. Thus, as designed and implemented, the Grid Investment Plan will replace transmission assets in a planned and controlled manner to address the large number of aged and aging transmission assets on the system.

Upon arrival, it was determined that the transformer, as well as oil on the ground surrounding the transformer, was on fire. The transformer fire caused by the incident burned for approximately three days. Construction was performed to tie the Conyers 076462 – Solo Cup 46-kV and Conyers 076462 – Blue Circle 46-kV lines together to restore load served by the Conyers 076462 – Blue Circle 46-kV line. Question:

Refer to the November 9, 2022 hearing transcript page 1838, line 13 to page 1839, line 25.  For each substation equipment type, including i) power transformers; ii) circuit breakers; iii) relays; iv) switches; v) load-tap changers; vi) bushings; and vii) CTs, please provide:
The Iowa Survivor Curve (indicating years in consistent units along the horizontal, or “x” axis) employed by the Company’s depreciation expert to determine the depreciation rate for the substation equipment type;
The historical failure rate data on which the Company’s depreciation expert relied when determining which Iowa Survivor Curve to select for the substation equipment type;
The expected useful life (in years) the Company employs for the substation equipment type when selecting equipment of that type for replacement as part of its transmission investment plan; 
The historical failure rate data on which the Company relied when determining the expected useful life (in years) for the substation equipment type.  If historical failure rate data on which the Company relied is different than that provided to the Company’s depreciation expert, please explain why.

Response:

The Company follows FERC’s Electric Plant Instructions using group depreciation and uses a composite life for each plant account. The requested substation equipment categories are recorded to FERC accounts 353 for Transmission and 362 for Distribution. Please see Appendix Exhibit 2, Appendix C-2, pages 66 and 79, which depict the Iowa Curves employed by the Company’s depreciation expert to determine the deprecation rates for substation equipment associated with FERC accounts 353 and 362.

The Company’s depreciation consultant did not base the recommendations on historical failure rates. As discussed in the depreciation study, assets may retire for many reasons: failures, proactive replacements, capacity change, technological change, relocation projects, and/or changes in standards. The lives and Iowa Curves in the Company’s depreciation study were analyzed using historical retirement data from Georgia Power, which reflects the full range of reasons for asset retirements and is the best predictor of future retirement patterns.

The Company’s expected life for this equipment is 50 years for transformers, 35 years for breakers, and 20 years for relays. The Company uses group depreciation and composite useful lives for transmission and distribution plant accounts. Therefore, equipment-specific useful lives cannot be provided as requested. 

Please see the response to part (b) above.

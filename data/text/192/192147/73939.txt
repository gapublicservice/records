IV. FAULTS WITH GEORGIA POWER’S RATIONAL FOR ENDING NEM PILOT
Q.GIVEN THIS SUCCESS WHAT ARE THE COMPANY’S REASONS FOR DISCONTINUING THE NEM PILOT? 
A.As presented in their filing, the Company’s objections boil down to concerns over lost revenue, and cost shifting between participating and non-participating NEM customers.  
Figure 1 in the Supplemental Testimonies of Mr. Legg and Evans illustrates a simple comparison of the total summer-time monthly bill for a customer with distributed generation under the RNR tariff, in contrast to the same customer with monthly net billing.  The RNR tariff customer’s bill is $122 while the NEM customer’s bill is $76, a difference of $46.   They state, “This cost shift creates an unjustified and disproportionate subsidy that only benefits the customer participating in the monthly netting pilot.”  And continue, “The Company does not recommend continuing to subsidize BTM solar customers at the expense of other customers by using the monthly netting methodology combined with volumetric rate structures.” 
 Elsewhere, Dr. Gattie, another expert witness on the Company’s behalf states, “However, monthly net metering (or monthly netting here in Georgia) is a separate policy issue that involves shifting costs to ratepayers who do not participate as solar customer-generators. Significantly, my testimony on monthly netting should not be misconstrued as opposition to residential rooftop solar PV nor should it be misinterpreted as opposition to compensating solar customer-generators for electricity exported onto the grid. Quite the opposite, customer-generators should be compensated for the energy they export to the grid, and at a rate that is fair and equitable for all Georgia ratepayers and that does not shift costs to ratepayers who do not participate in distributed solar PV.”
Q.HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO THESE CONCERNS?
A.First, the Company’s emphasis on cost shifting ignores a fundamental feature of class-based rate making and revenue recovery that is inherent in Georgia and elsewhere.  Calling out a cost shift due to monthly net metering as the reason to abandon the monthly NEM pilot is a selective objection and it ignores the fact there is systemic and accepted “cost-shifting” throughout the system.  The Company’s opposition to NEM billing is based on an ideologically stringent, but wholly impractical, structure where all customers pay their full costs and there are no shared or socialized system costs. 
Two examples can help illustrate this point.  Any customer within a class who uses less than other customers in effect “shifts” costs towards other customers.  Say, for example, a family has several teenagers who move out of the household, thus reducing their monthly bills.  Or consider a household that installs a more efficient air conditioning system or heat pump water heater.  These are just some of the many reasons why individual members of a customer class may reduce, or for that matter increase, their consumption.  The utility recovers less revenue from customers who reduce consumption, and more from customers who are consuming more. Overall rates are set for a class in a manner allowing the utility to recover its costs along with a return on the equity (ROE) in the rate base.  As I will discuss below, Georgia Power has consistently collected revenues well above costs resulting in its ROE exceeding the central ROE that was authorized by the Commission and used to set rates.    
There are also clear differences, not reflected in rates, in the costs to serve each customer throughout the system. For example, it may be more expensive to serve customers in less densely populated rural areas of the service territory or be more expensive to serve single family households in comparison to multi-family units.  Neighborhoods with underground service may have higher costs than those with overhead poles and wires.  Again, rates reflect costs for an entire customer class (not the individual customers in that class) and they also reflect the consumption of the whole class (and not the consumption levels of individual customers within that class).  The Company’s primary argument against monthly NEM, that it is an “unjustified and disproportionate subsidy” is discriminatory against customers who have invested in distributed solar and should be soundly rejected on this basis alone.     
Q.BUT AREN’T THE LEVEL OF IMPACTS FROM NEM PILOT A LEGITIMATE CONCERN?
A.Not at the current stage of market development.  The Company leaves out important information to keep the NEM impacts in perspective.  In 2021, Georgia Power reported having 2,296,077 residential customers, with average residential consumption of 12,220 kWh/yr. and an average residential rate of $0.12 per kWh. Based on these, residential revenues can be calculated as roughly $3.47 Billion.  The 5,000 monthly net metered customers represent 0.2% (two tenths of one percent) of the residential customer base.  The annual impact of monthly netting, for which the Company is requesting a rate adjustment to be borne by all customers, is $1.42 million/yr. As argued above, it is inappropriate for the Company to request this rate adjustment to offset the reduced consumption from the subset of customers who have invested in distributed solar.  However, even if the Company’s argument and calculation of a $1.42 million are taken on their face value, then the impact per customer is $0.62 per residential customer per year.  The total impact on residential revenues is 0.04% (four hundredths of one percent).  
The values cited in the previous paragraph illustrate that Georgia is still in the very early stage of rooftop solar market development.  As Dr. Gattie points out in his testimony, monthly net metering has been an often adopted and successful mechanism for promoting solar during the early stages of market development.  Figure DH-2 illustrates Georgia’s current share of net metered residential customers to California and the rest of the United States based on data presented by Dr. Gattie in his testimony. 

Figure DH-2: Georgia Net Metering is in Early Stages & Could Grow by More than 5X Before Reaching National Average
When evaluating the Company’s recommendation to terminate the monthly net metering pilot, it is critical to recognize that Georgia is still in an early stage of market development, and therefore any impacts are very small.  As Dr. Gattie notes, like Georgia, many states have successfully used NEM to promote early market development for distributed generation.  As illustrated in Figure DH-1 Georgia’s NEM pilot has also been a success and the Company’s proposal to terminate net metering is premature and based on arguments that do not place the impacts in context.

Q. CAN YOU SUMMARIZE YOUR REASONS FOR DISAGREEING WITH THE COMPANY’S PROPOSAL TO END MONTHLY NET METERING?

A.Yes.  The main points are:
It has been shown in Georgia and elsewhere that monthly net metering is an effective mechanism for encouraging growth of distributed solar resources. 
The rationale that monthly net metering should be ended because it is a cost shift ignores the basic fact that cost shifting between customers in a rate class is systemic.  The Company’s position to call out distributed generation customers is selective and discriminatory.  
The Company argues that revenue reductions from net metering need to be recovered from other customers.  Yet, on a system basis the Company has consistently collected revenues above the authorized central ROE for the past eleven years, in amounts that far exceed any revenue erosion from net metered systems.  The Company does not provide any evidence that current or future levels of distributed solar are increasing system costs.   
The impacts from the monthly net metering are very small.  Even if accepted at face value, based on the Company’s flawed premise of using a cost shift as the basis for increased revenue requirements, the level of impact is minute in comparison to the other factors driving revenue requirements and rates.  The Company proposes to end net metering based on impacts that are 100 times less than the impacts from revenue collection above the authorized ROE, and 1,000 times less than requested revenue increases in the general rate case.  
V. CUSTOMER’S PERSPECTIVE – WHY MAINTAINING MONTHLY NET METERING MATTERS
Q.WHY IS IT IMPORTANT TO MAINTAIN MONTHLY NET METERING AND TO REJECT THE COMPANY’S PROPOSAL? 

A.When the monthly netting pilot reached capacity, customers with distributed generation were placed on an instantaneous netting tariff. This alone has had a serious negative impact on customer economics, and this in turn can be expected to slow growth in the small solar market.  Under monthly netting, customers receive full retail credit for all solar generation each month, up to their total usage.  Under instantaneous netting, by contrast, a customer receives full retail credit for all solar power consumed on site, along with a credit for solar power exported to the grid based on the Company’s solar avoided energy cost rate, which is cited as being $0.026763/kWh in the Legg and Evans Testimony.  
I have conducted an analysis of a customer’s perspective of the economics of investing in a rooftop solar system using the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (NREL) System Advisor Model (SAM).   Figure DH-5 illustrates that shifting to the instantaneous net metering as proposed by the Company has a significant negative impact on the customer’s economics, increasing the simple payback by 8 years.  The analysis includes the benefits of an increased 30% Federal Investment Tax Credit. 

Figure DH-5: Monthly Net Metering Provides More Favorable Customer Economics than Instant Netting
The testimony of Legg and Evans verifies the customer economics for distributed solar will worsen if monthly net metering is replaced by instantaneous net metering.  Figure 1 of their testimony indicates the bill in an illustrative summer month with 400 kWh of export and 1,000kWh of consumption for a customer with instantaneous net metering would be $122 while a customer with monthly net metering would have a bill of $76. 
Critically, the Company is not advocating for Instantaneous Netting as is currently in place. Rather, the Company is advocating to place all distributed generation customers on a three-part rate, which includes a non-coincident demand charge. Table DH-1 summarizes how this change would dramatically worsen the customer economics for rooftop solar, increasing the simple payback to more than 30 years.
   
Table DH-1: Proposed TOU RD Dramatically Worsens Customer Economics
The testimony of Legg and Evans verifies that the customer economics for distributed generation will be significantly worse if monthly netting is replaced by the Company’s proposal. Exhibit LPE/LTl-1 of their testimony indicates that customers can expect to save even less on an annual basis, only $254 per year, on the TOU-RD rate under instantaneous netting. Based on their analysis the simple payback would be more than 54 years. 
My analysis and the Company’s testimony both indicate ending monthly net metering significantly worsens the customer economics for rooftop solar.  Moreover, my analysis and the Company’s testimony both indicate that requiring distributed generation customers to take service under a three-part rate will render rooftop solar entirely uneconomic. 
The Company’s proposal may well be designed to slow or halt the growth of the distributed generation solar market in Georgia.  The Commission should reject such a position while directing the Company to enhance their consideration of distributed solar and other DERs in subsequent IRP planning processes.  I briefly discuss recommendations on improving the IRP with respect to distributed solar and other distributed energy resources in Section VIII.

VII. OTHER SUPPORT FOR DISTRIBUTED SOLAR
Q.DOES THE COMPANY PROVIDE OTHER OPTIONS FOR CUSTOMERS INTERESTED IN DISTRIBUTED SOLAR ENERGY?
A.Yes, the Company offers the year-round distributed generation (DG) customer-sited “CCSP” program (which allows customer-sited solar resources to provide energy to the grid through long-term, fixed price agreements with RECs retired on behalf of the host customer), the Simple Solar Program, the Community Solar Program and two “behind-the-meter” options: the “Energy Offset Only” (EOO) and the “Renewable Non-Renewable Resources” instantaneous netting program. Two programs, the Customer Renewable Supply Procurement (CRSP) program and the Distributed Generation Request for Proposals (DG RFP), have closed due to reaching full enrollment.  
Q.DO THESE INITIATIVES SERVE THE SAME MARKET OR PROVIDE THE SAME CUSTOMER BENEFITS AS THE NEM PROGRAM THE COMPANY PROPOSES TO END?
A.No. These other options do not serve the same market, nor provide similar customer economic benefits as NEM.  The alternatives are geared towards larger commercial customers and are suited for customers willing to pay more for renewable electricity rather than have solar provide savings. They do not provide customer economics that are favorable in comparison to NEM.  
For example, the two programs that have reached full enrollment (CRSP and DG RFP) are specifically designed for the C&I sector, the CCSP appears to be geared towards customers who have some degree of legal, administrative and electric power industry expertise (e.g. it involves being able to negotiate and enter into a long-term, fixed price power purchase agreement), and the Simple Solar program is targeted at customers who do not need any financial benefit, ever, from investing in renewables (the customer pays an additional 1 cent per kWh to Georgia Power so that the Company retires some portion of RECs). These programs can be beneficial for participating customers, and can support the development of large, renewable energy plants and assist businesses with meeting voluntary emissions reductions goals. The behind-the-meter EOO program is like the instantaneous net metering program with both resulting in longer payback periods compared to the monthly net metering offer. Ultimately, none of these programs replace the need to continue offering NEM for customers with lower energy demand, fewer financial and technical resources, and those customers who want to see a return on their solar investment rather than those willing to pay a premium. 
Q.DO THE COMMUNITY SOLAR OPTIONS OFFERED COMPARE FAVORABLY FOR CUSTOMERS TO NEM?
ANo, they do not. The Community Solar (CS) program entails customers paying Georgia Power a monthly Solar Charge rate of $24.99 (with a proposed increase to $27.99) per one kW block, in addition to the customer’s normal cost of electricity; in exchange, the Company offsets the customer’s purchase of delivered energy by the amount of energy produced at the solar facility supplying the CS program equivalent to the customer’s subscription level. This will vary, based on the array output. Further, “based on the structure of the program, a customer should anticipate the CS charges to participate in the program will exceed any bill credits received.” Similar to the programs for larger consumers and the C&I sector, this program is designed for customers who have no financial concerns regarding needing to see – even over a twenty-year time horizon – a neutral or cashflow positive result from their investment. 
The Income-Qualified Community Solar program is similarly designed, offering a household of four living off $55,000 a year the opportunity to pay an additional $6.99 per month to receive one kW block of solar from facilities owned by Georgia Power. A participating household could be expected to receive modest savings of roughly $72/year, based on 1,255kWh/kW of solar output and a retail rate of $0.12/kWh. While this program will be helpful in providing a limited solar option to lower income households, it should be noted that the program is limited to 5,000 1kW blocks and it will be subsidized by non-utility corporate sponsors.  I encourage the Company to offer and promote an attractive Community Solar option for all customers, because as currently designed it is likely the Income-Qualified Community Solar Program will struggle to scale upwards to a significant degree.
Q.HOW HAS THE CUSTOMER UPTAKE BEEN FOR THE COMMUNITY SOLAR PROGRAM?
A.As shown in Figure DH-10, there has been minimal customer uptake for the existing offer. The 2022 IRP mentions a community solar program capacity of 8 MW. As of February 2022, 1,954 blocks have been subscribed. If each block is 1 kW, then the total program subscription has achieved 24% in fifty months. Further, after 2019 when NEM became available, community solar participation has virtually flatlined. 
Figure DH-10: Community Solar Participation Flatlined Over Last Two Years in Comparison to NEM Growth in Figure DH-1.
This is a stark difference to the participant interest in the growth in NEM, presented in Figure DH-1, which was fully subscribed within its first two years.  This is not surprising as NEM offers customers an ability to realize savings whereas participants in the Company’s community solar program need to pay a premium for solar. I support community solar and encourage the Company to redesign each of their community solar offerings so that they can provide participating customers with savings as opposed to always paying a premium.
Q. ARE THERE REASONS IN ADDITION TO THOSE YOU HAVE DISCUSSED ABOVE WHY MAINTAINING NEM DURING THE NEXT RATE CYCLE MATTERS? 
A.Yes. First, the 2022 IRP contains several statements along the lines of “Georgia Power is committed to providing industry-leading customer service related to renewable energy”, including that “Georgia Power’s greatest accomplishment with respect to renewable development is effectively responding to customer needs and creating long-term value for customers”. While Georgia Power may be answering the need for some of the larger C&I customers with regards to renewable energy, it does not appear as though the Company is providing an equal degree of responsiveness and service, with regards to accessing renewable energy, to all customers. Customers with less demand and less access to technical and financial resources are not receiving the same service from the Company as those with more demand and greater access to technical and financial resources.
 Second, distributed generation, supported by NEM, is important because north Georgia needs more generation. Specifically, the IRP states that “there is a significant gap between generation and load forecasted in north Georgia which will be further increased by future coal retirements. This gap will require the transmission system to transport large amounts of energy from south to north Georgia and place additional strain on the existing transmission system. Moreover, overreliance on generation from distant geographic regions can create adverse conditions that aggravate power System reliability and resiliency issues.” The IRP further states that “new local area generation is a critical component of the long-term solution to continue reliably serving load in north Georgia.” But, “options to add new generation are generally limited by the location of suitable sites. Georgia Power’s ability to add new transmission assets is similarly limited based on land and right-of-way needs for optimum location of the lines and substations, as well as environmental permitting requirements.” Meanwhile, Georgia Power references that economic conditions are deteriorating amongst their coal plants, but that Plant Bowen Units 3-4 will continue to operate because they “remain critical to preserving System reliability and resiliency in north Georgia.” 
In sum: uneconomic coal plants are continuing to operate because more generation is needed in north Georgia. However, there is limited land in north Georgia and building new large-scale generation and transmission projects can face various environmental and right-of-way challenges. Meanwhile, a program that results in new generation, that has seen significant uptake, including an increase among low- and moderate-income households, that utilizes rooftops, and that leverages and stimulates significant private capital via customers paying for the permitting and construction of the generating array, is not only closed due to being fully enrolled, but the Company now suggests ending the successful program entirely.
When asked during cross examination if the Company considered behind the meter resources as a solution in North Georgia, the Company responded with concerns that the resources would not be available and online when contingencies happen. Certainly, that might be true if contingencies occur in the evening, but I will note that the Company has not proposed any policies, programs, or incentives to encourage adoption of solar + storage, which could be available and online around the clock to provide the needed services.  This lends even further weight to the conclusion that the Company’s proposal may well be designed to suppress customer-sited, behind the meter resources. 

VIII. TAKING GREATER ADVANTAGE OF DISTRIBUTED SOLAR IN THE COMPANY’S PLANNING

Q.DOES THE COMPANY’S ASSUMPTION OF ENERGY ONLY BENEFITS ALSO RAISE CONCERNS IN RELATION TO THE MODELING OF DISTRIBUTED SOLAR IN THEIR PLANNING?
A.Yes, while the IRP is not the focus of my testimony, the energy only assumption also has serious implications for the Company’s planning.  The consideration of benefits and costs in the IRP should be consistent with the framework presented in Figure DH-11. Further, if the Company does not kill distributed solar market growth through their ill-advised proposal to end monthly net metering as this time, then the forecast for distributed solar adoption is a key input into the development of an IRP. Most often utilities model forecasted adoption of distributed solar and/or distributed battery storage as a reduction to the energy and peak load forecast. Since these resources can reduce the utility’s load, and potentially offset future load growth and the need for new resources, it is crucial that stakeholders have access to the inputs that go into the development of the forecast. Given the importance of distributed solar and the implications that different levels of distributed solar growth can have on the IRP results, we recommend that the Company work collaboratively and transparently with stakeholders to develop distributed solar forecasts used as inputs for future IRPs. 
An example is the IRP process in Indiana, where the utilities hold several public stakeholder meetings prior to the filing of the IRP to discuss modeling inputs such as the load forecast, electric vehicle and distributed solar forecasts, existing resource costs and operating parameters, new resource costs, accreditation of existing and new resources, commodity forecasts, constraints placed in the model, scenarios that will be evaluated, and modeling results. This type of stakeholder meeting and information sharing are crucial to facilitate transparency in the IRP process. In Indiana, stakeholders can participate in these meetings and submit feedback and/or questions to the utility on any items discussed during the meeting. 
AES Indiana held five stakeholder meetings leading to its filing of the 2022 IRP in December 2022. During the first stakeholder workshop, AES Indiana presented information related to some of the model parameters used to develop the forecast for distributed solar, the different scenarios that would be evaluated, and preliminary forecast results. In conjunction with the meeting, AES Indiana also released supporting workbooks for the development of the distributed solar forecast to parties signing non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) to receive access to the data. After reviewing the information provided by AES Indiana, stakeholders had the opportunity to submit feedback on any items discussed during the meeting, including the distributed solar forecast.
I and my Energy Futures Group colleagues have experience participating in similar stakeholder workshops across many different jurisdictions. For any workshop to be fruitful it is imperative that they are held in a transparent and collaborative manner. For discussion of distributed solar in a workshop, it will be important for the Company to provide the assumptions used to develop the forecasted level(s) of distributed solar adoption and the results of various levels of adoption impact the Company’s load forecast.
VIII. SUMMARY AND PROPOSED SOLUTIONS
Q.CAN YOU PLEASE SUMMARIZE YOUR TESTIMONY?
A.The Company’s proposed end to NEM is based on a faulty logic over cost shifting, is overstated based on the level of market adoption, is unfavorable to the economics for rooftop solar, has negative equity impacts and risks missing the customer and system benefits of expanded distributed solar generation as a system resource. Distributed solar generation in Georgia, supported by net metering, can play a larger role in meeting portfolio objectives such as clean energy targets, improved resilience, grid flexibility, and portfolio diversification while also addressing equity issues.
Q.WHAT ARE YOUR RECOMMENDATIONS AND SOLUTIONS TO IMPROVE THE COMPANY’S NET METERING PROPOSAL?
A.I strongly recommend that the Commission reject the Company’s net metering proposal, except for taking steps to improve customer protections and consumer education, which I support.  
The Commission should order the Company to continue monthly NEM at least through next rate cycle.  If a numeric cap on the number of NEM participants is deemed necessary, it should be no less than 75,000 customers, a level that still represents a small share, roughly 2.3%, of the residential customer base. 
The Commission should reject the Company’s proposed requirement of the TOU- RD rate as a default for customers with distributed generation.  The Company should be directed to revise the design of the TOU-RD to address serious flaws, such as the undue impact on low consuming customers and the inappropriate treatment of demand charge based on non-coincident peak consumption. 
The Commission should direct the Company to create a workgroup (potentially with facilitation by staff or another non-utility party) to investigate, develop and recommend the next generation of mechanism to support distributed generation solar in Georgia prior to next rate case.  This recommendation recognizes that as distributed solar adoption increases (well beyond Georgia’s current and near-term levels) there is a need and benefit to mechanisms beyond NEM.  
As an example, the type of new mechanism to be worked out could be similar to RNR instant netting, but with compensation for export above the current solar avoided cost based on fuller recognition of distributed solar’s benefits, including adders for equity or environmental justice.
I recommend the following be used as guiding principles and additional supports for the market development as workgroup commences:  
Don’t create a separate customer class for distributed generation customers.
Conduct robust financial analyses from the customer’s perspective and design any successor mechanism to NEM so that it supports a reasonable payback (e.g., approximately 12 years), thereby encouraging adoption and sustained orderly market development.
Enhance the analysis of distributed generation in the IRP planning process. 
Consider and conduct robust analysis on mechanisms and rate designs to encourage (but not require) added value through the combination of solar with other distributed energy resources (such as solar+storage, or solar+flexible load management). 
Investigate and identify clear objectives related to equity and energy justice issues and design future mechanisms for DG and DERs consistent with and informed by these objectives.  
Design a flexible mechanism, in which specific elements can be modified as market conditions, costs, and benefits change and evolve over time, all within a relatively stable and enduring framework.
Redesign the Company’s community solar offerings so that participation enables participating customers to realize savings rather than simply being willing to pay a premium.

 2       JUST APPROVED IN 2022?

 3   A   The IRP update is precipitated by much higher load growth than was projected in the

 5       rate of 4% for the period 2023 through 2033. This is in stark contrast to the 0.4% per

                                 3. DEFERRED ACCOUNTING


 1   Q   WHAT IS DEFERRED ACCOUNTING?

 2   A   Deferred accounting is a process whereby a utility is authorized by the Commission to

 3       defer, rather than immediately expense, a specific cost for recovery in a future period.

 4       If a cost is immediately expensed, it could have an immediate (and potentially material)

 5       impact on a utility’s earnings; whereas, deferring the expense would match the

 6       expense with future revenues while spreading the burden of that cost into the future.


 7   Q   IS DEFERRED ACCOUNTING A NORMAL REGULATORY PRACTICE?

 8   A   No. Deferred accounting is an extraordinary regulatory practice. It is only used when

 9       it is necessary either to prevent material harm to the utility’s financial integrity, thereby

10       placing its credit rating in jeopardy, or to provide more stable rates, thereby averting

11       rate shock for the benefit of the utility’s customers.


12   Q   IS DEFERRED ACCOUNTING ALWAYS APPROPRIATE?

13   A   No.   Deferred accounting shifts regulatory risk from a utility’s shareholders onto

14       customers. Thus, it is contrary to the regulatory compact in which a utility should have

15       an opportunity — not a guarantee — to earn a reasonable return on its investment in

16       the facilities used in providing electricity to its captive customers. Therefore, deferred

17       accounting should be used sparingly, such as when a utility incurs an extraordinary

18       cost, such as storm damage, or it becomes necessary to spread recovery of that cost

19       over a reasonable period to prevent harm to a utility’s financial integrity or avoid rate

20       shock for customers.

 3   Q   DO YOU HAVE ANY RECOMMENDED CHANGES TO SCHEDULE DCL-1?

 4   A   Yes. Generation is not a natural monopoly. Allowing a regulated monopoly to install

 5       generation behind a customer’s meter at a discounted price to the customer while

 6       recovering the remaining costs, including a return on rate base, from its customer base

 7       would confer a competitive advantage over other (non-regulated) generation providers

 8       who do not have captive customers to recover their costs. To ensure that GPC does

 9       not disadvantage competitive generators, GPC should be required to competitively

10       procure the DER and related equipment from third-parties. Further, the customer

11       should be fully involved throughout the RFP process, including the preparation of the

12       RFP, identifying qualified providers, evaluating the proposals, and selecting the

13       winning bidder.


14   Q   DO YOU HAVE RECOMMENDED CHANGES TO SCHEDULE DCO-1?

15   A   Yes. Limiting Schedule DCO-1 to 10 MW is potentially anti-competitive. The customer

16       should have the ability to reserve the right to utilize the DER facility to provide any

17       needed thermal energy when the facility is being dispatched to serve system needs.

18       Further, it should be clear that the customer will be allowed to outsource the design,

19       build and fuel supply supporting the on-site DER provided, of course, that the customer

20       is willing to certify that the equipment selection and the maintenance plan are

21       consistent with generally accepted utility practice. Finally, GPC has not justified

22       placing a 10 MW limit on the size of a customer-owned DER. To do so, would clearly

23       discourage a customer from owning a DER in lieu of the Company ownership option,

 4   Q   DO YOU HAVE ANY PROPOSED CHANGES TO SCHEDULE CL-1?

 5   A   Yes. First, the specific circumstances that constitute a “Demand Response Event”

 6       should be identified (e.g., a shortage of available generation and/or transmission

 7       capacity, area protection, or a system emergency). Second, the credit should reflect

 8       the full avoided cost of generation capacity. In the event that a Demand Response

 9       Event can occur due to shortages or congestion with the transmission system, the

10       credit should also reflect avoided transmission capacity costs. The credit should not

11       be discounted because to do so would underpay the customer and fail to recognize

12       the benefit to firm customers who, as a result of increasing DR, will have more reliable

13       service and less exposure to potentially costly outages.

14                Third, customers should have the option to choose among a menu of different

15       options, such as contract duration and maximum daily/monthly/annual curtailment

16       hours, and the credit should reflect the value of the option selected. For example, a

17       longer contract term and/or more hours exposed to curtailments, should result in a

18       higher credit. This would allow a customer to better align its appetite for risk with the

19       potential benefits.

20                Finally, the compliance incentive should not exceed the capacity value

21       reflected in the currently applicable agreement.

 Duke looks to add more gas, offshore wind to meet demand boom in NC, SC

 Monday, February 5, 2024 3:48 PM CT

 By Abbie Bennett
 Platts

 Projecting massive load growth in the Carolinas, Duke Energy Carolinas LLC and Duke Energy Progress LLC have amended resource plans to add more gas
 plants, advanced nuclear and wind.

 "We're already projecting eight times the load growth we anticipated just two years ago," Duke North Carolina President Kendal Bowman and Duke South
 Carolina President Mike Callahan said in separate Jan. 31 statements.

 The Duke Energy Corp. utility subsidiaries told state regulators in the Carolinas their load forecast through 2030 had increased by about 2 GW compared to the
 forecast used to develop prior plans.

 That projected growth is due primarily to significant economic development planned in North Carolina and South Carolina, particularly manufacturing and
 technology, both industries that often "operate around the clock" and may require more on-demand energy resources, Duke Energy spokesperson Bill Norton
 told S&P Global Commodity Insights.

 "This load growth is an industry-wide challenge — and the states that move decisively to build new generation will be best-positioned to take advantage of the
 positive trend in economic development opportunities while protecting affordability and reliability," Norton said.

 North Carolina

 In November 2023, Duke warned North Carolina regulators that "unprecedented economic development" well above the company's "historical experience" in the
 Carolinas was driving projected load growth across both states that "substantially exceeds even the high-load growth case" included in the company's resource
 plans filed in August 2023. The company reiterated those projections in its Jan. 31 filings.

 To meet that growth, Duke proposed two large fossil gas plants, each at 1,360 MW, including at least one in Person County. The new gas plants would also be
 "hydrogen-capable," though that technology is not yet commercially available. Duke's proposed additions total nearly 9 GW of new gas before 2035, nearly
 tripling the gas generation proposed in its first carbon plan regulators approved in 2022.

 "This plan is tripling down on the coal-to-gas transition, saddling customers with risky investments in new polluting power plants and failing to deliver the clean
 energy future called for in [North Carolina] state law," Will Scott, Southeast climate and clean energy director at the Environmental Defense Fund, said in a Feb.
 1 statement. "We're hopeful the NC Utilities Commission will require Duke to pursue a path that controls costs for customers while meeting North Carolina's
 2030 carbon emission reduction goal on time."

 Duke's proposal also includes continued support for a new advanced nuclear facility in North Carolina, with an additional site to be determined in 2025.

 "These breakthrough nuclear technologies are not anticipated to be available for deployment ... until the mid-2030s," Duke said in its filing.

 Duke proposed 2,400 MW of wind generation offshore North Carolina by 2035 in its updated plan and 1,200 MW of new onshore wind for the Carolinas by
 2033.

 "Duke's updated modeling and supplemental portfolio clearly illustrates how important offshore wind will be to providing the large quantities of carbon-free
 electricity needed to reliably run the grid," Southeastern Wind Coalition President Katharine Kollins said. "Offshore wind is an incredible resource that will
 diversify power generation and provide electricity when we need it most — winter mornings and summer afternoons."

 In North Carolina, Duke's newly amended plans include options for paths to 70% carbon emission reduction by 2030, 2033 and 2035. North Carolina state law
 mandates a 70% reduction in carbon pollution in the electric power sector by 2030.

 North Carolina regulators ordered Duke to file with its updated plan a state-mandated portfolio that meets a 70% reduction in carbon emissions by 2030. Duke's
 preferred resource plan, however, targets a 70% reduction by 2035 or 2037. Offshore wind and advanced nuclear are key to reaching even those goals, Duke
 said.

 That plan, according to the utility, would be the "most reasonable, least cost and least risk pathway to inform the near-term reasonable steps required to progress
 the reliable and orderly transition of the Carolinas system."

 Future updates to North Carolina regulators will continue to include portfolio options targeting a 70% reduction by 2030, according to the law, Norton said.

 A decision from the North Carolina Utilities Commission on the updated plan is expected by the end of 2024, according to Duke. Hearings for the plan are
 scheduled for spring and summer 2024.

 South Carolina

 South Carolina had the fastest-growing population in the US in 2023. To prepare for the load that will accompany future growth, Duke is proposing a new
 combined-cycle gas plant for South Carolina.

 "This additional demand for energy is unprecedented — historic in both size and speed," Callahan said. "To meet customer needs, we're proposing more of
 nearly every resource available to us as we maintain our diverse 'all of the above' approach to maintain reliability and affordability."

 The Carolinas plan includes advanced nuclear, gas, pumped hydro storage and renewables along with energy efficiency and demand-side management programs
 to target peak energy use reduction of nearly 2.8 GW by 2038, according to Duke.

 At least one of several new gas plants Duke proposed is expected to be located in South Carolina.

 Duke's amended Carolinas plans also include more solar energy than prior proposals, with a forecast for 17,500 MW of additional solar across both states within
 the next 15 years.

 "We need power — a lot of it — and we need to take action today," Callahan said.

 On Jan. 25, the South Carolina Public Service Commission approved Duke's proposal to provide additional information on its resource plan and reset the
 previously approved regulatory schedule. The new schedule now plans for a hearing in mid-September. Regulators are expected to decide on the plan as early as
 November.

    Section 1.1 – Provision for Peak-Period Load Ratios: This article of the Manual establishes and

    provides for the annual derivation of Peak-Period Load Ratios (“PPLRs”) that are utilized in

    energy and capacity transactions and in other allocations as provided for in the IIC. These ratios

    are shown on Informational Schedule No. 1.


    Section 1.2 – Methodology for Determining Peak-Period Load Ratios: The Contract Year in the

    IIC is defined as January 1st through December 31st. The peak period is defined as the fourteen

    (14) hours between 7:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m. (Prevailing Central Time) of each weekday,

    excluding holidays.


    The Peak-Period Load Ratios for the Contract Year are based upon the prior year’s actual peak

    period energy in the months of June, July, and August for each OPERATING COMPANY. The

    system peak period energy is equal to the sum of all the OPERATING COMPANIES’ peak

    period energy, excluding: (i) opportunity transactions with OTHERS that would begin and end

    during the period from the current hour through Friday (midnight) of the following week; and (ii)

    any energy sales transactions that are settled on a financial basis.


    The Peak-Period Load Ratios are determined by dividing each OPERATING COMPANY’s

    summation of the June, July, and August actual weekday peak-period energy by the total system

    June, July, and August actual weekday peak-period energy.

    efficiencies during normal and low-flow periods is replaced during the 14-hour peak period since

Summary
qPlease summarize your findings and recommendations.
AGiven the extraordinary circumstances surrounding the IRP update, specifically, the dramatic increase in load growth prompting GPC to accelerate future capacity additions, the Commission should be highly skeptical of GPC’s proposals on deferred accounting and an additional sum. My findings and recommendations are as follows:
Deferred Accounting
Deferred accounting is an extraordinary regulatory practice. 3. Deferred accounting
qwhat is deferred accounting?
ADeferred accounting is a process whereby a utility is authorized by the Commission to defer, rather than immediately expense, a specific cost for recovery in a future period.  If a cost is immediately expensed, it could have an immediate (and potentially material) impact on a utility’s earnings; whereas, deferring the expense would match the expense with future revenues while spreading the burden of that cost into the future.  
qis deferred accounting a normal regulatory practice?
ANo.  Deferred accounting is an extraordinary regulatory practice.  It is only used when it is necessary either to prevent material harm to the utility’s financial integrity, thereby placing its credit rating in jeopardy, or to provide more stable rates, thereby averting rate shock for the benefit of the utility’s customers.
qIs deferred accounting always appropriate?
ANo.  Deferred accounting shifts regulatory risk from a utility’s shareholders onto customers.  Thus, it is contrary to the regulatory compact in which a utility should have an opportunity — not a guarantee — to earn a reasonable return on its investment in the facilities used in providing electricity to its captive customers.  Therefore, deferred accounting should be used sparingly, such as when a utility incurs an extraordinary cost, such as storm damage, or it becomes necessary to spread recovery of that cost over a reasonable period to prevent harm to a utility’s financial integrity or avoid rate shock for customers.  
qis deferred accounting an accepted practice in Georgia?
AYes.

qYou previously stated that GPC could have procured capacity from mpc through the IIC.  What is the IIC?
AThe IIC is a FERC-approved tariff by and between APC, GPC, MPC and Southern Power Company (collectively, the “Operating Companies”) which operate as an integrated electric utility system and Southern Company Services, Inc.  A copy of the currently effective IIC is provided in Exhibit JP-2.  
qwhat is the purpose of the IIC?
AThe IIC provides a contractual basis for the continued operation of the electric facilities of the Operating Companies to achieve the maximum possible economies consistent with the highest practicable reliability of service, and to provide a basis for equitably sharing the costs associated with the operation of facilities that are used for the mutual benefit of all the Operating Companies.  Thus, the Operating Companies share all the benefits and burdens, including complying with operating, dispatch and reserve requirements, participating in opportunity sales transactions, and bearing responsibility for their portion of purchases.
qDoes the IIC also provide a mechanism for reviewing generation expansion plans of each of the operating companies?
AYes.  Article IV of the IIC provides that a designated representative from each of the
Operating Companies, together with a representative of Southern Company Services, Inc., shall constitute as an “Operating Committee.”  The Operating Committee is responsible for reviewing and recommending generation expansion plans for approval by the Operating Companies that relate to the overall coordinated operation of the Southern electric system.
qGPC asserts that the IIC provides a mechanism for temporarily sharing capacity surpluses or deficits among the operating companies.  Does the IIC define a “temporary” capacitY surplus or deficit?
ANo.  Under Section 7.1,  the IIC is premised on each of the Operating Companies having adequate resources to reliably serve its own obligations.  However, it also recognizes that in any given year one or more of the Operating Companies may have a temporary surplus or deficit of capacity as a result of coordinated planning or by virtue of load uncertainty, unit availability, and other such circumstances.  Given the extraordinary nature of the growth that GPC is now incurring, the IIC should not have been so narrowly interpreted, as the Company is now suggesting.  
qDo the operating companies compensate one another for the cost of capacity that is shared among them?
AYes.  Section 7.2 of the IIC provides that the costs associated with reserve sharing capacity are settled on a monthly basis whereby each deficit company purchases capacity from a surplus company at the rate that reflects the cost of the most recently acquired peaking plant resource that is available for year-round operation and scheduling. Thus, while the IIC requires each Operating Company to be self-sustaining, there is no stated limited on how often, or how long, an Operating Company can make reserve sharing purchases. Further, because generation expansion requires long lead times, and because capacity additions are “lumpy,” there will always be some level of reserve sharing purchases and sales by and among the Operating Companies.

This would include both the timing and amount of any planned capacity additions and reassessing any prior decisions to retire generation capacity.  Thus, when GPC became aware that MPC was planning to retire capacity that is now needed to maintain reliability for the Operating Companies, it could have arranged to procure this capacity through the IIC to assure that the resources would continue to be used and useful, while compensating MPC for the cost to keep the resources in operation.

ASchedule CL-1 would be an alternative to the Demand Plus Energy Curtailment (DPEC) Schedule and would apply to customers who agree to reduce their load to a designated firm-demand level during periods of extreme supply and demand conditions (i.e., a Demand Response Event) within no less than 30 minutes advance notice.  A CL-1 customer would receive an unspecified credit based on 75% of the capacity value provided over the contract and identified in the customer agreement.  Further, the demand credit would be fixed over the initial six-year term, but it would be updated during each subsequent annual renewal.  However, either GPC or the customer may decline to renew the agreement.
The customer would also be required to demonstrate its ability to curtail load each year and would be subject to substantial costs if it fails to comply during any Demand Response Event or demonstration.
qAre there any design elements common to all three of the proposed new schedules?
AYes.  In addition to not specifying the amount of the required credits/payments, GPC also reserves the right to terminate service in any related agreement without penalty or further obligation if:
Termination is approved by the Commission;
The participating customer fails to meet any obligations or requirements under the rate schedule or accompanying agreement; or
Where termination is otherwise consistent with GPC’s electric service Rules and Regulations.
qDo you have any concerns with the three proposed new rate schedules?
AYes.  GPC would have undue discretion in determining the amount of the credits/payments, renewal terms, penalties and other significant terms and conditions. 
For example, the credits/payments would be based on “system value” as defined by GPC.  Neither the term system value, nor the methodology for quantifying system value, is specified in any of the proposed new rate schedules.  This would give GPC sole authority in determining the amount of the payments and/or credits and the customer would have no opportunity to either vet the proposed credits/payments or seek a remedy from the Commission.  Without knowing the specific credit, or even the specific methodology and assumptions used in determining the credits, potential customers cannot properly assess the risks and benefits of opting for any of the proposed new rate schedules.  In essence, all the risks would be placed on the customers.
The risk-shifting is further exacerbated because GPC can terminate the DER/DR rate schedules without notice, and without further obligation, at its discretion.  This is especially unfair because to the extent a customer is required to pay for DER facilities, or may require additional equipment to comply with curtailments under Schedule CL-1, premature termination with short or no notice could result in “stranding” the customer’s investment.
qDo you have any specific concerns regarding proposed Schedule DCL-1?
AYes.  As previously stated, GPC will install, operate, maintain and control the Company-owned DER and related equipment as determined by GPC.  All of the costs will be recovered in base rates, and GPC will earn a return on the associated rate base.  However, there is no requirement for GPC to competitively procure the DER and related equipment to be installed at the premises of a participating customer, and the customer apparently has no input into the decision about the manufacturer and type of equipment to be installed at the customer’s facility.  
qDo you have any specific concerns regarding proposed schedule dco-1?
AYes.  First, as previously stated, GPC proposed limiting the maximum DER capacity to 10 MW, citing operational risk; whereas, there are no similar constraints in the proposed Schedule DCL-1.  However, there are operating risks associated with any generation equipment, regardless of the operating party.  Regardless of who will own/operate the DER, the enabling agreement should provide a clear set of standards consistent with generally accepted operating and maintenance practices to ensure compliance.  Further, GPC should be more than capable of conducting due diligence of a third-party owned and operated generator, including periodic inspections and operational reviews to minimize the risk of forced outages.  Ultimately, the customer will have to demonstrate compliance with the Company’s dispatch orders, or face either financial penalties or termination.  
Second, although the customer is required to own and install the equipment and provide several capabilities, which include providing a firm fuel supply, the Company would have full operational control, including dispatching the resource solely at its discretion.  It is unclear why any customer who may also require the thermal energy produced from the facility for heating/process use would be willing to give GPC full operational control without assurances that GPC cannot arbitrarily curtail the thermal energy supply.  
qDo you have any specific concerns regarding proposed schedule CL-1?
AYes.  First, the customer would be required to execute a six-year rolling term; that is, although the commitment will be renewed annually, unless GPC or the customer declines the renewal, the customer would have to wait for up to six years before returning to firm service.  GPC has not explained why a six-year term is necessary or why a customer must wait six years to return to firm service.  Second, it appears that GPC could penalize the customer for failing to comply with a curtailment based on a capacity value that could be higher than the credit that the Company is currently paying.
Third, the term Demand Response Event is not defined with any specificity.  Although it is understandable that GPC will want to curtail load during extreme weather events, such as Winter Storm Elliot or the Polar Vortex, the circumstances in which GPC should call for curtailments should be defined more specifically.  Examples of more specific circumstances requiring GPC to declare a Demand Response Event would include:
Projected shortage of available generation and/or transmission capacity to maintain service to firm native load customers.
Local area protection.
System emergency.
qwhat do you recommend?
AFirst, none of the three proposed new rate schedules should be approved as filed.  
Second, GPC should be required to publicly disclose the methodology and assumptions for determining how any credits/payments under the rate schedules are determined and provide a specific quantification of the currently projected system resource (capacity/energy) values, at least annually, during the term of the corresponding agreements.  Further, the methodology and assumptions should be subject to Commission review and approval, both initially and in each subsequent year. This would provide customers with sufficient information to assess their level of interest, prior to making a long-term commitment.
Third, customers should be given at least six months advance notice before service is terminated.  Further, upon terminating any of the proposed rate schedules, GPC should be obligated to compensate the customer for any costs incurred to qualify for service under each rate schedule. This would require compensating the customer for the remaining book-value of the equipment installed and paid for by the customer. This is only fair as GPC expects to be compensated when it retires a generating unit.  
qDo you have any recommended changes to Schedule DCL-1?
AYes.  Generation is not a natural monopoly.  Allowing a regulated monopoly to install generation behind a customer’s meter at a discounted price to the customer while recovering the remaining costs, including a return on rate base, from its customer base would confer a competitive advantage over other (non-regulated) generation providers who do not have captive customers to recover their costs.  To ensure that GPC does not disadvantage competitive generators, GPC should be required to competitively procure the DER and related equipment from third-parties.  Further, the customer should be fully involved throughout the RFP process, including the preparation of the RFP, identifying qualified providers, evaluating the proposals, and selecting the winning bidder.  
qdo you have recommended changes to schedule dco-1?
AYes.  Limiting Schedule DCO-1 to 10 MW is potentially anti-competitive.  The customer should have the ability to reserve the right to utilize the DER facility to provide any needed thermal energy when the facility is being dispatched to serve system needs.  Further, it should be clear that the customer will be allowed to outsource the design, build and fuel supply supporting the on-site DER provided, of course, that the customer is willing to certify that the equipment selection and the maintenance plan are consistent with generally accepted utility practice.  Finally, GPC has not justified placing a 10 MW limit on the size of a customer-owned DER.  To do so, would clearly discourage a customer from owning a DER in lieu of the Company ownership option, thereby limiting competition.  Thus, the Commission should reject the 10 MW limit.  Alternatively, if the 10 MW limit is retained, then it should also apply to Schedule DCL1.  
qDo you have any proposed changes to schedule CL-1?
AYes.  First, the specific circumstances that constitute a “Demand Response Event” should be identified (e.g., a shortage of available generation and/or transmission capacity, area protection, or a system emergency).  Second, the credit should reflect the full avoided cost of generation capacity.  In the event that a Demand Response Event can occur due to shortages or congestion with the transmission system, the credit should also reflect avoided transmission capacity costs.  The credit should not be discounted because to do so would underpay the customer and fail to recognize the benefit to firm customers who, as a result of increasing DR, will have more reliable service and less exposure to potentially costly outages.  
Third, customers should have the option to choose among a menu of different options, such as contract duration and maximum daily/monthly/annual curtailment hours, and the credit should reflect the value of the option selected.  For example, a longer contract term and/or more hours exposed to curtailments, should result in a higher credit.  This would allow a customer to better align its appetite for risk with the potential benefits.  
Finally, the compliance incentive should not exceed the capacity value reflected in the currently applicable agreement.
7. Conclusion

AThe Commission should make the following findings:
Reject GPC’s deferred accounting of capacity payments and development costs.  
Reject GPC’s proposed additional sums on the MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs.
Reject the proposed solar array at the ________ site.  
Reject the new DER/DR rate schedules (DCL-1, DCO-1, and CL-1) as proposed.  
Revise the DER/DR rate schedules to at a minimum:
Include a definition of system value.
Require GPC to seek Commission review and approval of the methodology and assumptions (by describing the methodology and providing a specific quantification) used to determine system value prior to approving the new rate schedules initially and in each subsequent year.

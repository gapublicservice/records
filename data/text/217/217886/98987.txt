Small Modular Reactors in Commercial Operation
There are no small modular reactors (SMRs) in commercial operation in the United States. The only identified Light Water Reactor SMRs in the United States are:
the NuScale US460, 77MWe design undergoing licensing with the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and
the NuScale US600, 50 MWe design which received a Standard Design Certification in 2020.
Neither of the projects above are currently commercially operational. Globally, two SMR designs are in commercial operation in Russia (KLT-40S, 35 MWe) and China (HTR-PM, 210 MWe).
NuScale Power recently installed a new simulation laboratory at Texas A&M University. The lab is the third of its kind built in the United States and will be used to help broaden the understanding of the company’s small modular reactor (SMR) technology. Numerous jurisdictions across North America are actively pursuing the development of SMR technology, such as the mid-2030s plan for GE-Hitachi BWRX-300s by Ontario Power Generation  or the  Tennessee Valley Authority’s efforts to develop up to 800 MWe of SMR capacity at the Clinch River Nuclear Site.

Staff Response #2:
In Staff’s filed Mix Study, Yates 8 and 9 were selectable in 2026 and Yates 10 in 2027. This assumption is consistent with the Company’s 2023 IRP Update filing in October 2023. 

If Staff’s study had allowed the flexibility for the Yates CTs to be selected in other years beyond 2026 and 2027, Yates 8 (357 MW) would have been selected in 2028, but Yates 9 and 10 would not have been selected.
This result supports Staff’s recommendation to deny the certification of Yates 8, 9 and 10 in this proceeding and to submit Yates 8 -10 in the 2029 – 2031 All Source RFP to determine whether these units are the most economic options for ratepayers.

Despite the fact that Yates 8 was selected in 2028 in Aurora for Southern Company, Staff’s plan, which includes the MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs, provides sufficient capacity for Georgia Power in 2028 without the need for the Yates 8 CT.

Staff Response #3:
During the hearing for Docket 55378 regarding Georgia Power’s 2023 IRP Update, Staff was asked for further information about utilities' and RTOs' use of Dynamic Line Ratings (“DLRs”) in their long-term transmission planning.


Executive Summary
Georgia Power may significantly improve short-term transmission through DLRs, allowing the company time to evaluate and invest in cost-effective transmission solutions. This approach to short-term investment will ensure that the Company’s transmission system functions reliably in the near-term while ensuring that customers are receiving a long-term solution that is least-cost and competitive. The document below provides background on DLRs, references to evaluations of the methodology, and how this technique would apply to the Company’s system.
History of Dynamic Line Ratings
In 1994, Portland GE effectively utilized DLR systems to postpone necessary upgrades on 230 kV transmission lines within their network. Portland GE found that by utilizing DLR that monitored line tension, they were able to defer projects initially slated for 2003 to 2010 and defer a transmission project from 2005 to 2012. Transmission line capacity is rated based on their thermal rating, sag and clearance, electrical rating, and mechanical rating. By monitoring line tension, one could determine the line’s thermal performance, sag behavior, and mechanical integrity.  Adopting a similar strategy could allow the Company to enhance the utilization of existing infrastructure, potentially delaying expensive transmission line upgrades.

Daymark is also aware of multiple utilities and regional transmission operators who have implemented ambient-adjusted ratings (AAR), which is a subset of the suite of grid enhancing technologies referred to as DLR. In addition, FERC Order 881 requires public transmission utilities to implement AAR over the next few years. Utilities that have already or are currently working to implement AAR include the New York Power Authority (NYPA) and National Grid in New York, PPL Electric in PJM, and many others.

The FERC clarified that Order 881 (and the implementation of AAR generally) does not require revisions to assumptions or methodologies for transmission planning, meaning that the order was not focused on long-term reliability but rather shorter-term transmission capacity (congestion avoidance) benefits. 

Efficacy of DLR
A study performed by AES evaluated the impact of Weather Adjusted Line Ratings (WALR), which is a type of DLR, on transmission line capacity using a test-bench WECC model. The study found that WALR can significantly mitigate thermal overloads by adjusting transmission line ratings based on historical and projected weather patterns. This allows for more optimized utilization of existing infrastructure, potentially delaying or avoiding costly upgrades and ensuring that the transmission lines are operated safely and reliably within their thermal limits.  
Application to Georgia Power
Georgia Power integrates both reliability and market efficiency concepts into its transmission planning, making AARs a potentially attractive solution in some cases. In addition to standard seasonal contingency cases required by NERC standards, Georgia Power also runs the “Area Max” cases, which are a form of market efficiency test designed to prevent all congestion for the generators of a particular area under contingency. Implementing AARs to solve Area Max contingency loadings, where appropriate, would present the Company and its ratepayers with the potential for attractive, cost-effective alternatives to more expensive transmission buildout in those situations.

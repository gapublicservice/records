 1   C.        Summary of the IRP Update and Initial Thoughts
 2   Q.        Please summarize your understanding of the circumstances that caused GP to file
 3             this IRP Update.
 4   A.        Updated demand projections relative to the Company’s 2022 IRP indicate that GP will
 5             have a capacity deficiency to serve its customers by the winter of 2025/2026. The
 6             Company states that capacity deficiency may impair GP’s ability to provide reliable firm
 7             service to its customers. As explained in the Direct testimony of the Company witnesses
 8             Mr. Grubb, Mr. Valle, Mr. Evans, and Mr. Bush (collectively the “Panel”), based on data
 9             and information at the time of GP’s 2022 IRP, the Company was projecting less than
10             400 megawatts (“MW”) of growth from the winter of 2023/2024 to the winter of
11             2030/2031.1 At the time of the filing of the IRP Update, GP now expects load growth of
12             approximately 6,600 MW through the same time period. This revised load growth has
13             accelerated when GP is projecting a capacity deficiency to 2025/2026 from 2030/2031.


14             GP also opines that there is limited time to procure or construct the capacity resources
15             that are needed to meet its capacity deficiency by the winter of 2025/2026. As such, GP
16             maintains that it must acquire additional capacity resources, largely outside typical
17             competitive solicitation processes, to meet the projected growing capacity and energy
18             demands in order to support the tremendous economic growth opportunities expected in
19             the Southeast in general, and Georgia specifically.


20   Q.        Will you please briefly describe what is driving this change in the company’s
21             projected customers’ demand and economic development to the Southeast in
22             general, and in particular, Georgia?
23   A.        At the hearing, the Panel testified that the overwhelming majority of the large economic
24             development growth (approximately 80% of the total gigawatt hours) is going to come
25             from data center load. In addition to data centers, the Panel also testified that the state is
26             seeing an influx of manufacturing growth for the manufacture of electric vehicles,
27             battery, and clean energy technology.

 7   E.   Deficiencies in the existing IRP Update filing
 8   Q.   Please highlight some concerns you have with the IRP Update filing.
 9   A.   Given the anticipated growth spurred by rapid economic development, the immediate
10        necessity to secure additional capacity resources within a constrained timeframe may
11        induce rushed analyses, decisions, and potential oversights in resource selection. The
12        urgency to match the accelerated pace of growth may limit the availability of time for
13        comprehensive assessments and thorough consideration of all relevant factors. In such
14        circumstances, critical details might be overlooked, risks underestimated, and the
15        Commission may find itself compelled to make decisions without the advantage of a
16        comprehensive analysis vetted by all stakeholders. This heightened sense of urgency
17        poses a risk to the decision-making process.


18        In addition, I have certain other concerns. They can be summarized as follows:


19        1. The Company’s resource option/selection process should not be constrained based on
20           a strict application of the 70/30 ownership rule, particularly in a time of exponential
21           growth in the region.
22        2. While the current IRP Update does include certain expansions of CFE resources, it
23           inadequately addresses customer-specific goals and needs.
24        3. The Company insists that there will be downward pressure on rates as a result of the
25           projected sales growth. However, GP stops short of guaranteeing rates are going to
26           realize that downward pressure.
27        4. Load forecast needs to be thoroughly vetted, including projected load growth for
28           serving new at-risk large customers.

 1   Q.   Will you please briefly address some of your concerns with the 70% ownership
 2        requirement GP references?
 3   A.   Yes. GP makes reference to a precedent that was last litigated and decided by this
 4        Commission in 2001, requiring that an electric utility own a minimum of 70% of its
 5        capacity. The restriction requiring an electric utility to own and operate a minimum of
 6        70% of its generation capacity, limiting alternative sources like PPAs to 30% or less, can
 7        be a concern for several reasons:


 8        1. Cost Efficiency: Depending too heavily on owned generation capacity may limit GP’s
 9           ability to benefit from potentially more cost-effective alternatives available through
10           PPAs. It might result in higher costs for ratepayers if the utility is unable to secure the
11           most economically favorable resources.
12        2. Technological Advances: Energy landscapes and technologies evolve over time.
13           Relying heavily on owned generation facilities might hinder the utility's ability to
14           swiftly adopt and integrate emerging, more efficient, and environmentally friendly
15           technologies that could lead to cost savings.
16        3. Market Dynamics: Restricting the use of PPAs may limit the utility's flexibility to
17           navigate market dynamics. Entering into agreements with external providers could
18           offer better terms and pricing, contributing to more stable or even reduced rates for
19           ratepayers.
20        4. Risk Mitigation: Owning and operating all generation capacity exposes the utility
21           and, consequently, ratepayers to the risks associated with maintaining and upgrading
22           these facilities. Sharing these responsibilities with external providers through PPAs
23           may distribute risks and ensure more efficient risk management.
24        5. Renewable Energy Integration: Limiting alternative sources might impede the utility's
25           ability to meet renewable energy goals or environmental commitments. PPAs for
26           renewable energy sources, for example, could help the utility transition to a more
27           sustainable energy mix.


28        The restriction on alternative capacity sources could potentially lead to missed
29        opportunities for cost savings, hinder the adoption of new technologies, limit flexibility in

This non-binding Memorandum of Understanding (•MOU") is made and entered into by and
between the United States of America, acting by and through the Administrator of General
Services (•GSA"), and Southern Company Services, Inc. ("SCS") (collectively the •parties").
SCS is the authorized agent for The Southern Company ("Southern Company').

Parties: GSA is an agency in the executive branch of the United States Government that
provides centralized procurement for the Federal Government, offering billions of dollars'
worth of products, services, and facilities that federal agencies need to serve the public.
Southern Company currently serves over 9 million electric and gas customers and is committed
to sustainability as reflected in its goal of achieving net-zero greenhouse gas emissions
company-wide by 2050 with an interim target of 50% reduction in emissions by 2030.

Effective Date and Term: This MOU will become effective upon execution by both Parties. The
date of execution by the last Party to sign will be deemed to be the effective date of this MOU.
This MOU will terminate two years from the effective date.

Authority: Pursuant to 40 U.S.C. S0l(c), GSA is authorized to represent certain executive
agencies in negotiations with public utilities. SCS is authorized to enter into this non-binding
MOU on behalf of the Southern Company.

Purpose: The Parties will work collaboratively and expeditiously towards developing a non­
binding carbon pollution-free electricity ("CFE") plan (the "Plan"). The Parties will seek for
the Plan to be implemented by 2030 and result In electricity that is 100% attributable to CFE
resources for Federal Government electricity customers ofthe electric utility subsidiaries of
Southern Company ("Southern Operating Companies"). Resources can be either owned or
contracted for by the Southern Operating Companies with at least 50% meeting the Federal
Government's hourly matching objectives for 24/7 CFE. Further, by pursuing their respective
sustainability goals, the Parties strive to provide benefits to the States of Alabama, Georgia, and
Mississippi.

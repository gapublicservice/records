STF-PIA-13-1

Question:
It appears the company analyzed several capacity expansion scenarios associated with serving this new anticipated customer load. Why did the company choose scenario MG0 (Mid-Gas and Zero Carbon) as its primary case? 

Please provide additional quantitative detail on the main differences between input assumptions used in these scenarios.   
Does MG0 align with gas price and utilized carbon cost assumptions in the last approved IRP?  If not, why? 
Did each scenario allow all resource options to be freely selected or are any resources “forced in” to the model? 
Which scenario does the company believe best reflects scope of requirements included in the new EPA Source Performance standards (commonly referred to as EPA 111 b and d)?


Response:
The Company’s moderate gas view is based on the “Reference View” in the Annual Energy Outlook produced by the United States Energy Information Administration. This view of future natural gas prices is widely cited and publicly available. The Company’s Lower CO2 pressure view reflects what current law requires regarding CO2 emissions, with the assumption of some increasing stringency of requirements over time through the periodic review of regulations under the Clean Air Act, and the indirect pressure applied by the tax incentives available under the Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”). The Company believes that its MG0 scenario (Moderate gas view, Lower CO2 pressure view) is appropriate for its base case as it reflects (1) a moderate fuel price forecast, and allows for consideration of higher and lower prices in the other scenarios, and (2) what current law requires with respect to CO2 emissions in the near term, and allows for consideration of moderate and higher pressures on CO2 emissions given the uncertainty with stringency, timing, and form of CO2 pressure.
The Company creates scenarios to aid in understanding some forms of uncertainty. Key uncertainties affecting planning include future environmental pressure—especially regarding carbon-dioxide, cost and performance of future generating technologies, future load growth, and the evolution of natural gas prices.
There are four main areas that involve input differences between scenarios: CO2 pressure, technology cost and performance, load growth, and fuel prices. Quantitative information for each assumption difference between scenarios can be found within the Supplemental Update Aurora files, provided December 4, 2023. The list below includes where to find specific input assumption details:
Carbon pressure assumptions can be found within various tables and change sets.
Emission price assumptions can be found in the Emission Price tables, labeled according to their associated scenario.
The last year CC without CCS is available can be found in the New Resources table.
CT capacity factor constraint assumptions can be found within the change set “B2024 Final IRP – CT Capacity Factor Constraint.” 
Technology cost and performance assumptions differ between scenarios based off the IRA end date assumption. These differences can be found within the New Resources table by utilizing the “Var Cost Mod1” column for production tax credit (“PTC”) assumption differences and utilizing the “Fix Cost Mod1 and Mod2” columns for investment tax credit (“ITC”) assumption differences. 
Load assumptions are found in the Time Series Hourly tables, labeled according to their associated scenario.
Fuel price assumptions can be found in the Fuels tables, labeled according to their associated scenario.
Yes, the methodology used to develop the MG0 scenario, and specifically the gas and carbon cost assumptions, is consistent with the last approved IRP. The Company updated its gas price forecast using the EIA AEO 2023 information, and there is no carbon emissions cost assumption for the MG0 scenario in either the last approved IRP or this 2023 IRP Update. 
No generic expansion resource options are forced into the model.
Because the rules are not yet final, it is still unclear what the revised guidelines that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (“EPA”) has proposed under section 111 of the Clean Air Act will require. For existing sources, state environmental authorities, including Georgia Environmental Protection Division (“EPD”), will each write their state’s plans for implementing the 111(d) rules, and these plans must then be submittal for approval by EPA. Under the proposed rule, this process may take up to three years. In the meantime, the Company continues to use the four views of future CO2 pressure considered in this IRP Update to capture a range of possible outcomes and has not identified a specific view as reflective of the proposed EPA 111(b) and 111(d) rules.

Question:
Is the proposed customer DER co-location program open to carbon-free electricity resources (such as solar+batteries or generators that run on carbon-free fuels)? What does “firm fuel dispatchable” resource mean in this context?

BASIS FOR THE ASSERTION THAT THE 
INFORMATION SUBMITTED IS A TRADE SECRET

As part of Georgia Power Company’s 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update filed in Docket No. 55378 (“2023 IRP Update”), Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) submits to the Georgia Public Service Commission its response to STF-PIA-13-11 (“Response”). In the Response, the Company has provided project specific data supporting the evaluation of resources proposed in the 2023 IRP Update (the “Information”). All of such Information constitutes trade secret information of Southern Company, Georgia Power, and its affiliates and is therefore protected from public disclosure under Commission Rule 515-3-1-.11. 
The Information derives economic value from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use. Specifically, the Information contains competitively sensitive details related to the timing and cost assumptions for the resources proposed in the 2023 IRP Update. If the Information were made public, competitors, bidders, and suppliers could use the Information to unfairly manipulate the request for proposals process and competitive market to structure future bids and set an artificial price floor to arbitrarily increase prices to the detriment of the Company and its customers. Public dissemination of the Information would undermine Georgia Power’s ability to conduct an unbiased solicitation and secure the best cost bids and resources for the benefit of customers. Lastly, the Company’s competitors are not required to reveal or publish similar information and to require the Company to do so would place it at an economic disadvantage.

The Information is subject to substantial procedures to maintain its secrecy. Only select Georgia Power and Southern Company Services personnel are granted access to the Information. Those personnel receive access only on a “need to know” basis. Parties outside the Company who have been granted access to the Information, if any, have been required to sign confidentiality agreements with respect to the Information.  


STF-PIA-13-11

Question:
How long does the company believe it takes to construct new utility-scale battery energy storage resources?  Is the timeline longer, shorter or approximately the same as constructing a new CT? Please provide answers to these questions for sites that do and do not require transmission upgrades to serve these generation facilities. 

Response:
New generic battery resources in the Resource Mix Study utilize a REDACTED construction/spend curve. This timeline is shorter than the REDACTED construction/spend curve utilized for the generic combustion turbine (“CT”) resources in the Resource Mix Study. It is worth noting that the accelerated construction/spend schedule for the Yates CTs is required to meet the needed CODs. It is not expected that future new CTs in the Resource Mix Study would be required to meet this accelerated schedule.

A full interconnection affected systems study for a given resource must be performed in order to understand the cost and need for transmission upgrades a specific generator would also have to accommodate. Given the timeline of affected systems studies and their site-specific nature, it is impossible to predict which generic resources in the Resource Mix Study would need to have transmission upgrades indicated by an affected systems study. In general, transmission upgrades for a given generator are installed in parallel with the construction of the generator and may take longer than the construction of the generator depending on the required transmission upgrades.

STF-PIA-13-13

Question:
Does the company earn an additional sum when it draws power from the “reserve sharing with the Southern Company pool” referenced on page 32 of the company's direct testimony?


Response:
No. The Company does not earn an additional sum on capacity reserve sharing transactions within the Southern Company pool. The Southern Company Intercompany Interchange Contract (“IIC”), which governs transactions within the Southern Company pool, provides for reserve sharing among the operating companies related to temporary surpluses or deficits of capacity. Reserve sharing does not provide for firm, long-term capacity. In contrast, power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) provide for firm, long-term capacity and energy. An additional sum is associated with certificated long-term power purchases and is intended to encourage long-term power purchases.

STF-PIA-13-6

Question:
Page 49 of the 2023 IRP update main document states: “each new large customer must pass financial and economic screening requirements, including the Ratepayer Impact Measure (“RIM”) test.  These screening requirements compare the projected new revenues that will result from the customer projects with the estimated new costs to serve those projects, resulting in projected net benefits to customers.”
Please provide all rules, policies, or requirements upon which the referenced financial and economic screening requirements are described, based on, or otherwise based.  
Please provide all rules, policies, or requirements upon which the referenced Ratepayer Impact Measure (“RIM”) test are described, based on, or otherwise based.
Please describe how the projected net benefits to customers are measured.
Please describe how the projected net benefits to customers are determined. 
How are transmission and distribution upgrades and interconnection costs addressed in the referenced financial and economic screening requirements?
How are transmission and distribution upgrades and interconnection costs addressed in the Ratepayer Impact Measure (“RIM”) test?

Response:
Customers who receive new business offers must meet the eligibility requirements of the tariff under which electric service is being offered. Each offer considers the customer’s credit rating as related to the payment of direct and local costs. Many customers are now required to pay 100% of local costs, regardless of credit rating. For customers on a Real Time Pricing tariff, a Customer Baseline Load (CBL) is determined based on the size of the project, the costs to serve the customer, and other customer specifics to ensure the project  passes the Rate Impact Measure (“RIM”) test and puts downward pressure on rates. 
The RIM test used by the Company for large load customer offers is conducted using the PRICEM model. This is the same analysis and model the Company uses to calculate RIM for load expansions, other new customers who are not eligible for a choice of electric service provider, and demand-side management (“DSM”) programs. The RIM test is a net present value (“NPV”) calculation of the total benefits and total costs over the life of the project. These economic evaluations align the customer’s load shape with the marginal costs they place on the system, including with respect to their method of service and the  costs to serve them. As with DSM and other analyses, a RIM calculation result above zero is considered passing. However, simply passing RIM is not the final threshold for an offer to be extended. The additional criteria are described above in STF-PIA-13-6 part (a).
Projected net benefits are measured by a positive RIM result. Projected net benefits are measured by comparing the NPV of projected revenues to the NPV of projected costs for each new business offer. The NPV of projected revenues must exceed the NPV of projected costs in order for the offer to pass the RIM test and provide a positive RIM result. A positive RIM result indicates net benefits for customers.
Projected net benefits for new business offers are determined by subtracting the NPV of projected costs from the NPV of projected revenues. Revenue projections are developed based on the monthly peak load (kW), monthly energy consumption (kWh), the customer load shape, and the respective tariff under which the customer being evaluated will take service. Projected costs to serve new business loads are assigned to each project and included in the new business offer. There are two types of costs that may be included in offers: local and upstream. Local costs are assigned directly to the project and may include both transmission and distribution investments where the project receives the sole benefit. Upstream costs include investments that provide system benefit to other customers in addition to the project. Upstream costs are allocated to each project based upon that project’s marginal use of the system over the life of the project. The Company prepares each offer to fully recover both local and allocated upstream costs required to serve the customer load over the life of the project. 
Transmission and distribution upgrades and interconnection costs are captured in the local costs assigned directly to each project. Even if there are no upstream investments incurred by Georgia Power at the time of initial service, upstream costs are allocated to each new business project based on that project’s marginal impact on the system, as described above in STF-PIA-13-6 part (d). 
Both local (direct assigned) and upstream (allocated) transmission and distribution upgrades are included as projected costs in the RIM test.

Question:
Does Georgia Power or its parent company have carbon reduction goals? If so, please describe them and discuss how they have been taken into account when developing the proposed expansion plan presented in this IRP?


Response:
Southern Company has a long-term greenhouse gas (“GHG”) reduction goal of net zero emissions by 2050 and an interim goal of 50% reduction in GHG emissions from 2007 levels by 2030. Southern Company’s climate approach includes the three pillars set forth in the 2050 net-zero goal: (1) of pursuing a diverse energy resource portfolio, (2) developing new technologies to lower GHG emissions, and (3) constructively engaging with stakeholders.

When developing the expansion plans presented in the 2023 IRP Update, the Resource Mix Study Supplemental, which was filed with the Commission on December 4, 2023, includes various carbon pressure scenarios to capture impacts and allow for long-term planning to mitigate risks to customers from the range of potential outcomes from legislative or regulatory action aimed at reducing carbon emissions.

STF-PIA-13-9

Question:
Does the company use publicly available sources to develop its generic unit cost estimates for new resources? If yes, please identify the sources utilized. If not, why not? 


Response:
Yes, the Company uses publicly available resources ranging from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (“NREL”) Annual Technology Baseline (“ATB”) to Lazard to benchmark the Company’s internal cost estimates for new resources. However, the cost estimates utilized for all generic resources included in the Resource Mix Study are the result of internal studies and models developed specifically for use in generic cost estimates. These studies frequently leverage outside contracted work with Engineering Procurement and Construction (“EPC”) firms, but also utilize the Company’s internal engineering services. The intended result is to create a generic new resource cost and performance indicative of a technology deployment in the Company’s regulated service territory versus solely using public sources, which are frequently broader in scope. The Company also uses certain non-public sources used from Bloomberg New Energy Finance that inform future cost assumptions for certain technologies.

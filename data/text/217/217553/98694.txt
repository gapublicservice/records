 5   Q:        DID GEORGIA POWER’S LOAD REALIZATION MODEL PROVIDE A SINGLE
 6             ESTIMATE FOR PROJECTED LOAD?
 7   A:        No. Georgia Power’s Load Realization Model is based on 100,000 simulations, with each
 8             individual simulation representing a different estimate of potential load growth based on
 9             the assumptions that Georgia Power assigned to the uncertain variables. The 100,000
10             simulations are then combined to give a range of possible load growth and to show how
11             likely it is for each load projected to actually occur.

12             As a matter of statistics, the most likely load growth scenario is the data point in the middle,
13             where 50% of the scenarios project more growth and 50% of the scenarios project less
14             growth. This data point is referred to as P50, or the 50th percentile. Similarly, probabilities
15             can be assigned to all the other data points as well. P05 (the 5th percentile) and P95 (the
16             95th percentile) describe load forecasts where just 5% of the scenarios run are lower or
17             higher than that amount of load. In other words, scenarios smaller than P05 or larger than
18             P95 are very unlikely to occur.

 6   Q:       HOW DID GEORGIA POWER DEVELOP ITS TRM FOR THE 2022 IRP?
 7   A:       Georgia Power used the SERVM model, which evaluates the ability of a system’s resources
 8            to meet load across thousands of iterations that include varying assumptions around
 9            weather, the load forecast error, and generator forced outages. In many cases, utilities will
10            develop a reserve margin based on meeting the industry standard metric of one loss of load
11            expectation (“LOLE”) in ten years or 0.1 events per year. To develop Georgia Power’s
12            TRM, Georgia Power takes any additional step to evaluate the expected costs of
13            maintaining reserve capacity, production costs, and reliability costs to determine the
14            economic optimum reserve margin (“EORM”). This evaluation considers the tradeoff
15            between the costs of additional generating capacity on the system versus the improvement
16            in resource adequacy from having those additional generating resources. Georgia Power
17            evaluated the EORM by comparing the production 39 and reliability 40 costs, as determined
18            by the SERVM model, to the incremental capacity cost of new generation over several
19            different winter planning reserve margin levels (ranging from 20% to 32%) to find the
20            point where the sum of the production costs, reliability costs, and incremental capacity
21            costs is minimized. The image below shows the winter EORM from the TRM Study.

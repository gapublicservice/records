Q. Why do you state that GA Power’s 2023 IRP should be rejected? GA Power indicates
that the large amount of new economic development is creating this need.

Georgia Power has a long history of submitting false growth projections that are used to justify

expanding capacity but which does not materialize. The “Averch–Johnson effect” is an economic

term used to describe the tendency of regulated utilities to engage in excessive amounts of

capital accumulation in order to expand their profit.1 This can happen when the allowed rate of

return exceeds the cost of capital, since the difference between the two represents pure profit.


Q: Has this happened in Georgia?

Yes. Not only does GA Power’s allowed rate of return exceed the cost of capital, GA Power’s

allowed rate of return exceeds that of nearly every investor-owned utility (IOU) in the United

States and is far above industry norms. Thus, GA Power has even more incentive than most

IOUs to overinvest capital in order to increase profits. During both the 2019 Rate case and the

2022 Rate Case, expert witness Michael P. Gorman testified that GA Power’s ROE requests

were not justified and that the utility can easily meet its needs for financial stability and investor

profits with a normal ROE. Mr. Gorman prepared a chart to accompany his testimony which

depicted the history and current status of utility IOU returns on equity in the United States.

Q. What evidence is there that GA Power’s reserves are too high?

There are national guidelines for appropriate electricity reserves, which is how much generating

capacity is available above what the grid is expected to need. These guidelines ensure vital U.S.

interests in electric stability are met. According to NERC, the National Electric Reliability

Corporation, the Southeast region, or SERC-Central, which includes Georgia Power, exceeds

winter guidelines two-fold and summer guidelines by nearly three-fold. NERC recommends

Summer and Winter reliability reserves for the SERC region of the U.S. to be 15% even before

the additions of capacity from Plant Vogtle and the 2022 IRP. This excessive capacity costs

costumers millions, if not billions of dollars.

Q. What evidence is there that GA Power’s load factor is too low and how does that relate

to reserve margin?

Reserve margin’s purpose is to make sure GA Power, or any utility, has enough reserve capacity

to meet peak demand. It says nothing about grid efficiency in terms of utilization of generation

capacity. For that you must look at load factor. Load factor is the amount of power required from

the grid over a period of time divided by the amount of energy that can be supplied by the grid.

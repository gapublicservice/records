As part of Georgia Power Company’s 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update filed in Docket No. 55378 (“2023 IRP Update”), Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) submits to the Georgia Public Service Commission its response to STF-PIA-9-1 (“Response”). In the Response, the Company has specific competitive trade secret pricing (the “Information). All of such information (the “Information”) constitutes trade secret information of Southern Company, Georgia Power, and its affiliates and is therefore protected from public disclosure under Commission Rule 515-3-1-.11.

 The Information derives economic value from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use. Specifically, the Information contains the names of counterparties and facilities currently under negotiation. Public dissemination of the Information would allow Georgia Power’s competitors, suppliers, and customers access to the Company’s confidential and strategic development opportunities. Prospective bidders, vendors, and service providers may set their bids and proposal prices artificially high based on the Company’s expected costs, which would prevent the Company from securing the best cost resources and services for customers. Finally, the Company’s competitors are not required to disclose this type of information and to require the Company to do so would put it at a competitive disadvantage to the detriment of Georgia Power and its customers.

The Information is subject to substantial procedures to maintain its secrecy. Only select Georgia Power and Southern Company Services personnel are granted access to the Information. Those personnel receive access only on a “need to know” basis. Parties outside the Company who have been granted access to the Information, if any, have been required to sign confidentiality agreements with respect to the Information.

Question:
Has the Company evaluated the feasibility and economics of energy storage sites located in “energy communities” in Georgia? If so, please provide the results of any analysis, including workpapers if applicable. 


Response:
Yes. Siting energy storage in “energy communities,” or Georgia facilities that have had a coal-fired electric generation unit retired after 2009, has been studied. This would lower the cost of Battery Energy Storage System (“BESS”) projects by roughly 10%. This is due to the incremental bonus Investment Tax Credits (“ITC”) that apply to sites such as these that meet the Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”) definition of being located in an energy community. Many retired coal sites are suitable for the footprint of BESS, but transmission deliverability timing and costs must also be considered. If the Company can identify an economical site with transmission deliverability by November 2027, it will bring the project to the Commission for approval.

STF-PIA-9-3

Question:
Please explain why there is a difference in customer size requirements between the DER Colocation Program and the DER Customer Owned Program. Provide any analysis conducted by the Company or on its behalf, to justify the 10 MW minimum size for the DER Colocation Program and the 1 MW minimum and 10 MW maximum for the DER Customer Owned Program.


Response:
There is a difference in the applicable resource sizes between the DER Colocation Program and DER Customer Owned Program due to the operational risk that third party ownership would present to reliability. Company ownership provides assurance that the DER will respond when needed by mitigating risks such as vendor selection, vendor oversight, weatherization, commissioning, maintenance oversight, etc. While this risk exists with DER in the DER Customer Owned Program, the smaller size of the DER mitigate it by reducing the size of a single point of failure. Should one or several of the resources in the DER Customer Owned Program fail to operate as needed, it would have less of an impact on the overall reliability for Georgia Power than larger engines 10 MW or greater would have.

STF-PIA-9-4

Question:
Please refer to the IRP Update Main document p. 22 regarding the DER Colocation Program.
Describe the Company’s definition of firm fuel supply and what specific technologies are eligible for this program. Specifically, are batteries considered an eligible technology for this program?
What types of fuel will be acceptable for this program? 

Response:
Many technologies are eligible for the program provided they meet the requirements (e.g. reciprocating engines, battery energy storage, combustion turbines, fuel cell). The Company is not limiting what current or future technologies are eligible beyond these requirements. A firm fuel supply indicates that the resource is dispatchable, and the energy source for the resource is available on demand. Batteries will be considered an eligible technology provided they can be charged from the grid coincident to the co-located load. 
The Company is fuel source agnostic, provided that a resource can meet the certification and permitting requirements and the firm availability. Additionally, the DER must be interconnected allowing the Company to transmit the energy they produce to the electric grid. Acceptable fuel sources in addition to the battery requirements described in part (a) include natural gas with firm transportation rights, diesel with firm fuel contracts in place, or other fuels that can be contractually guaranteed.

STF-PIA-9-5

Question:
Please refer to the Company’s response to STF-PIA-4-3 (d).  Describe all lessons learned from the DER Customer Program Pilot.

Response:
Several lessons have emerged from the DER Customer Program Pilot: 

Segments of customers increasingly have a resiliency need and are interested in ways to better utilize those investments.
Many of the customers with interest in resiliency assets take service on marginal rates and are thus ineligible to receive credits for assets covering the marginal portion of their load through a demand response program structure.  
Resiliency as a service requires an additional investment, which increases on a $/kW basis for smaller customers. 
Most existing backup generation is not certified and permitted for non-emergency operation. 
Programs and credits can help incentivize customers seeking new DER to select cleaner, more efficient forms of back-up generation. 
Those programs need to reach customers before they make asset decisions, which often occurs during initial site engineering plans. 
Many customers have easier access to funds for upfront expenditures rather than an ongoing service fee under a tariff.  

See TS STF-PIA-4-1 Attachment A for a full list of customer feedback.

STF-PIA-9-6

Question:
Please refer to the Company’s response to STF-PIA-4-3 (c)
Provide any documentation applicable to the referenced DER Customer Owned Program’s certification, permitting, interconnection and maintenance requirements noted.
Has the Company considered allowing existing resources to be included in a DER program?  If not, why?

Response:
Regarding certification and permitting, federal regulations, such as 40 CFR Part 60 Subparts IIII and JJJJ and Part 63 Subpart ZZZZ, severely restrict or prohibit the ability of customers to enter into agreements with the Company to use existing Distributed Energy Resources (“DER”) in non-emergency situations, such as to supply power to an electric grid. In contrast, new DER, certified pursuant to 40 CFR Parts 1039 or Part 1048 and permitted for non-emergency use, provide the operational flexibility needed to economically dispatch these resources and provide energy and capacity benefits to all customers. The “Operation of Distributed Energy Resources (DER) in Parallel with the Distribution System Policy” applies if the DER Customer Owned Program participating customer is served via the Company’s distribution system. Due to the complexity of the Company’s transmission system, interconnection requirements for participating transmission customers will be determined as part of the interconnection studies conducted for participating customers. Minimum maintenance requirements are based on Original Equipment Manufacturer (“OEM”) recommendations and requirements, industry codes and standards, Southern Company generation standards, and best practices. These apply to routine preventative maintenance, operational checks, startup/commissioning and functional performance testing, sequence of operations, environmental compliance, control system compatibility, and cyber security.
No. Most existing backup generation is not certified and permitted for non-emergency operation and would not qualify for the program. Additionally, to assess and ensure the reliability of a DER system, the Company must be engaged from the beginning of the project. Allowing existing systems in the program without such engagement could  create risk to Geogia Power’s customers. Critical activities Georgia Power would not have been engaged in during the development of an existing asset include:
Engineering of the system and related equipment including interconnection to the electric grid.
Weatherization of the DER
Equipment selection and procurement to ensure equipment is reliable and readily available from a spare part perspective.
Startup and commissioning to ensure the system was tested under all scenarios giving the Company confidence the system will operate as designed.
Planned and unplanned maintenance activities and our understanding of how the system has been both proactively maintained and reactively maintained to any issues.

STF-PIA-9-8

Question:
Please refer to the Company’s Direct Testimony of Jefferey R. Grubb, Francisco Valle, Lee Evans, and Michael A. Bush, p. 47, lines 17-18.  Explain in more detail the referenced capacity value difference for winter and summer months between the Curtailable Load Program and Demand Response Plus Energy Credit Demand Response Program. 


Response:
The existing Demand Response Plus Energy Credit (“DPEC”) program pays demand credits in each of the four summer billing months of June through September. The demand credit is based on the product of the demand credit rate and the potential demand reduction during the defined DPEC Peak Period of June through September, non-holiday weekdays, from 12:00 noon to 8:00 p.m. The proposed Curtailable Load Program will pay monthly demand credits based on an annual capacity equivalence of the potential demand reduction during projected periods of extreme supply and demand conditions.

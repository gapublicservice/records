  Arne Olson
44 Montgomery Street, Suite 1500, San Francisco, CA 94104 arne@ethree.com

ENERGY AND ENVIRONMENTAL ECONOMICS, INC.San Francisco, CA
Senior Partner

Mr. Olson has 30 years of experience in energy analysis. He joined E3 in 2002 and became a partner in 2010. Mr. Olson leads E3’s Bulk Energy Infrastructure practice area helping clients navigate changes to bulk electric system operations and investment needs brought about by increasing policy and market interest in clean and renewable energy production. He has led a number of landmark studies of the feasibility and cost of achieving deep decarbonization and high renewable penetration goals, including studies of 100% renewable and net zero energy systems in New England, California, the Pacific Northwest and the Midwest. He consults extensively for utilities, asset owners, project developers and electricity consumers in support of resource planning and commercial transactions involving renewable, conventional and energy storage resources. He also provides technical and strategic support to numerous utility regulators, state agencies and environmental organizations. He is a frequent conference speaker and has provided expert witness testimony in regulatory and legal proceedings in California, Oregon, Montana, Colorado, New Mexico, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Alberta, Nova Scotia and Ontario. Prior to joining E3 in 2002, he served for six years in the Energy Policy Division of the Washington State Energy Office and its successor agencies.

Mr. Olson has led the development of E3’s industry-leading resource planning software including the RESOLVE model that develops optimal portfolios of renewable, conventional and energy storage resources and the RECAP model that calculates Loss-of-Load Probability and related statistics to ensure that power systems can meet load reliably under high renewable penetrations. His clients have included most of the major utilities and market participants in the West including the California Independent System Operator, Pacific Gas and Electric, Southern California Edison, Puget Sound Energy, PacifiCorp, Arizona Public Service, Sacramento Municipal Utilities District, Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, the Bonneville Power Administration, Calpine, NextEra, NRG, TransAlta and many others. Eastern clients have included Xcel Energy – Northern States Power, Florida Power & Light, Tampa Electric Company, Nova Scotia Power, Hydro-Quebec TransEnergie, TransElect, Long Island Power Authority, and the Georgia Large-Scale Solar Association. He also works extensively with government agencies and industry organizations such as the California Public Utilities Commission, California Energy Commission, Oregon Public Utilities Commission, the Western Electric Coordinating Council, the Western Interstate Energy Board, and the New York State Research and Development Authority.

Electricity Resource Adequacy:

Mr. Olson is a prominent expert on resource adequacy issues and has led many projects that evaluate the need for effective capacity to ensure resource adequacy during the clean energy transition.
Examples include:

Advising MISO on need determination and resource accreditation as part of a package of
reforms to MISO’s Planning Resource Auction.

Advising the California Public Utilities Commission in developing a proposed Reliable Clean Power Procurement Program that would reform the way the state’s Load-Serving Entities procure reliability and clean energy attributes.
Advising the Public Utilities Commission of Texas on proposed reforms to the ERCOT market to address resource adequacy needs.
Advising Calpine Corporation on generator accreditation issues for capacity markets in ISO-NE and PJM including addressing thermal generator limitations associated with lack of fuel availability.
Developing new resource adequacy standards and models for the Hawaiian Electric Company’s
five island systems.
Advising the New York Independent System Operator on application of ELCC concepts to
renewable resources within the context of NYISO’s Installed Capacity (ICAP) market.
Prepared the study Resource Adequacy in the Desert Southwest on behalf of a consortium of Southwest utilities that concluded urgent action is needed to develop new resources to ensure the region can continue to provide reliable power supplies in response to returning electric load growth, planned and anticipated resource retirements, and a changing supply mix.
Led a team that was retained by NRG and Exelon to develop a concrete proposal for a Load- Serving Entity Reliability Obligation to provide forward price signals necessary to ensure resource adequacy in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) market.
Led a team including former U.S. Energy Secretary Ernest Moniz and the Energy Futures Initiative that published Net Zero New England, a study of electricity system reliability under economy-wide target of net zero carbon emissions. The study included significant electric load growth due to electrification of transportation and building sector end uses along with the incorporation of vast quantities of solar and wind generation.
Advised PJM Interconnection on application of ELCC concepts to renewable resources within the
context of PJM’s Reliability Pricing Model (RPM) forward capacity market.
Leads a multi-company team that supports the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) staff for the Integrated Resource Planning (IRP) Proceeding. The IRP proceeding is considering the need for new capacity resources to ensure resource adequacy in California.
Prepared a report on regional resource adequacy programs in support of the Northwest Power Pool’s effort to stand up such a program in the Pacific Northwest. The study evaluated regional programs operated by each of the major market operators in North America for their potential application to the unique circumstances of the Pacific Northwest.
Developed the Renewable Energy Capacity Planning (RECAP) Model, a loss-of-load probability (LOLP) model designed explicitly to measure the need for capacity on highly renewable electricity systems, as well as the contribution of dispatch-limited resources such as wind, solar, energy storage and demand response toward meeting those needs.
Has led numerous teams that utilized RECAP to calculate resource needs, Planning Reserve Margins (PRMs), and Effective Load-Carrying Capability (ELCC) values for dispatch-limited resources for clients including the California ISO, California PUC, Western Electric Coordinating Councils, Portland General Electric, Hawaiian Electric Company, Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, Sacramento Municipal Utilities District, NorthWestern Energy, Northwest Power Pool, Xcel Energy, NV Energy, El Paso Electric, Nova Scotia Power, Calpine Corporation, and others.
Evaluated the need for “clean firm” capacity to ensure reliable electric service under 100% carbon reduction scenarios for California, sponsored by the Environmental Defense Fund and the Clean Air Task Force.

Led a team that used RECAP to calculate ELCC values for solar, wind, storage and hybrid resources to support NorthWestern Energy’s evaluation of bids received in its 2020 capacity solicitation, and is providing expert witness testimony to the Montana Public Service Commission.
Reviewed Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress’s IRPs on behalf of Cypress Creek Renewables and Carolinas Clean Energy Business Association and provided expert witness testimony in North Carolina and South Carolina regarding Duke’s resource adequacy studies, the capacity value attributed to solar and battery storage resources, and their incorporation in Duke’s capacity expansion modeling.
Led a project team that evaluated alternative methods for calculating the capacity value of solar resources on behalf of the Oregon Public Utilities Commission staff.
Led a team in 2019-2020 that utilized RECAP to calculate the capacity contribution of demand response and energy storage resources in California using the ELCC method on behalf of the California ISO.
Served as expert witness in a wholesale market tariffs case for Nova Scotia Power, considering issues related to effective capacity determination for third-party resources.
Led a team in 2019 that evaluated what types of resources would be needed to maintain resource adequacy in a deeply-decarbonized, 2050 California electricity system, in a study sponsored by the Calpine Corporation.
For a group of 13 utilities in the Pacific Northwest, led studies in 2017 and 2018 that examined scenarios achieving 50% renewables and up to 100% carbon reductions across the region, focusing on policy mechanisms to achieve the goals at least cost and on the nature and quantity of complementary resources that are needed to maintain reliable electric service.
For the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, led a study that evaluated the reliability implications of closing three in-city natural gas-fired generating stations and replacing them with alternative resource portfolios including new gas, demand response, solar energy, energy storage and new transmission.
Evaluated flexible capacity needs under high renewable penetration across the Western Interconnection on behalf of the Western Electric Coordinating Council and the Western Interstate Energy Board. The team included technical contributions from E3, NREL and Energy Exemplar.
Performed analysis on behalf of Calpine and presented to the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) to recommend the use of the effective load carrying capability (ELCC) metric in the Resource Adequacy (RA) program for wind and solar resources. The CPUC ultimately adopted this recommendation along with several market design features proposed by E3.

Advising Manitoba Hydro in conducting their first integrated resource plan.
Advising the Salt River Project in conducting their first integrated system plan consisting of generation, transmission, distribution and customer resource planning. E3 helped SRP design and implement the ISP process.

The Net Zero New England study considers alternative scenarios for building decarbonization including High Fuels and High Electrification scenarios, as well as optimal portfolios of electricity resources to meet electric demand reliably while reducing carbon emissions to 2-3 MMT by 2050. Alternative resources considered include onshore and offshore wind, ground-mounted and rooftop solar, imported hydroelectric power, conventional and advanced nuclear power, fossil generation with carbon capture and sequestration, and hydrogen.

For the Colorado Public Utilities Commission, assisted in developing long-term scenarios to use across a range of energy infrastructure planning dockets.

Review of Best Practices for Resource Planning and Capacity Accreditation
Q. please breifly summarize industry best practices for resource planning.
A.Through extensive work with utilities across the United States and Canada, I have identified seven principles that represent best practices for resource planning. In brief, these best practices include: (1) Incorporate climate policy and impacts of climate change; (2) Include renewable energy and energy storage resources as candidate resources; (3) Determine capacity needs through robust, loss of load probability (LOLP) modeling; (4) Consider total resource cost (TRC) of demand-side resources; (5) Address operational flexibility and integration needs through detailed operational study; (6) Engage meaningfully with stakeholders; and, (7) Conduct all-source solicitations for procuring resource needs identified during planning. While I have enunciated these first six principles in prior testimony in front of this Commission with regard to integrated resource planning best practices, I add here the seventh principle to highlight the importance for utilities to act on their plans by procuring resources in a fair and competitive “all-source” process that results in least-cost outcomes for ratepayers. I describe this principle in greater detail later in this testimony.
HOW DOES GEORGIA POWER’S 2023 INTEGRATED RESOURCE PLAN UPDATE COMPARE TO THESE BEST PRACTICES? 
A.Georgia Power is now including renewable and energy storage resources as candidate resource options in its capacity expansion modeling for the IRP and capturing the dispatch benefits associated with adding solar and storage separately and in combination. This is an improvement from the 2019 IRP. However, Georgia Power does not appropriately capture the increased capacity value of solar and battery storage when added in combination, relative to the standalone values, and thus continues to undervalue solar and storage resources relative to other types of generation. By only partially recognizing the reliability contributions of renewable energy and energy storage resources, Georgia Power will essentially over-build non-renewable capacity at a higher cost to ratepayers. With substantial resource additions projected in Georgia Power’s 2023 IRP Update, it is critical for Georgia Power to employ a more accurate methodology for estimating the capacity contribution of these resources. I have identified this concern in prior testimony and detail my continued concerns later in this testimony.
WHAT IS THE INDUSTRY BEST PRACTICE FOR CALCULATING THE CAPACITY VALUE OF RENEWABLE ENERGY AND ENERGY STORAGE RESOURCES?
A.Widely accepted best practice for capacity accreditation of renewable energy and energy storage resources is to use a rigorous, reliability-based model using loss of load probability (LOLP) to calculate Effective Load-Carrying Capability (ELCC). ELCCs are increasingly used throughout the industry to calculate a more accurate value of the capacity contribution of variable resources such as wind and solar, dispatch-limited resources such as energy storage or demand response, and hydroelectric resources that have characteristics of both variable and dispatch-limited resources. 
In addition, an emerging best practice is to use ELCC to estimate the capacity contribution of “firm” resources such as natural gas. The ELCC of a gas unit may differ from its Unforced Capacity or UCAP value because loss-of-load events tend to occur during periods with an unusually high number of resource outages; that is, the combined outage rate of the fleet during loss of load hours tends to be higher than the expected loss-of-load for any individual resource, or the combined outage rate for the fleet during normal hours. This is due to the binary and asymmetric nature of the reliability challenge, where most resource outage states do not result in loss of load, but the “tail conditions” of a cumulative outage distribution that do result in loss-of-load represent conditions with higher-than-average resource outages. In addition, the generator may suffer from weatherization or fuel availability issues which prevent it from performing at its highest capacity during critical events. The ELCC methodology allows the reliability contribution of various resources to be compared on a level playing field.  
Q.What are portfolio effects as they relate to solar and storage elcc values?
A.The ELCC values of solar and storage have two key portfolio effects that must be captured accurately in IRP modeling in order to ensure selection of least-cost portfolios that minimize costs for ratepayers. First, solar and storage are subject to saturation effects – where the marginal capacity value of solar or storage declines as more of the resource is added. This phenomenon is well-understood and is captured by Georgia Power in its modeling. Second, there is a diversity benefit of adding solar and storage together. This diversity benefit accrues because as more solar capacity is installed, the summertime loss-of-load risk hours typically shift away from daylight hours when solar is generating into a shorter window in the evening hours between sundown and 10pm. These shorter loss-of-load events can more easily be avoided by limited duration (e.g., 4-hour) battery storage. Adding storage, in turn, improves the capacity value of solar by moving loss-of-load hours from the evening back into the shoulder hours, e.g., late afternoon, when solar production is low but non-zero.
Q.How is the diversity benefit of renewable energy and energy storage determined?
A.Figure 1 demonstrates the diversity benefits of installing both solar and storage in the Desert Southwest region, from a study that E3 completed for a consortium of the six largest electric utilities in the region. The left-hand panel shows that the incremental capacity value from new solar in the region on top of the 3.6 GW of solar that is already installed, represented in the yellow area, is already very low. The second panel shows that incremental additions of four-hour lithium-ion battery storage, represented in the purple area, have very high capacity value, over 95% of the nameplate value, until approximately 8 GW are installed. The value for standalone 4-hour storage additions beyond 10 GW is very low, as another 10 GW of installations yields only 1 GW of capacity value. The third panel shows that when solar and storage are added in combination, the capacity value of the combined portfolio, represented in the purple hashed area, continues to grow without any noticeable saturation effects.

Q.How are utilities addressing the capacity value of hybrid solar + storage projects?
A.Most utilities are now receiving bids from hybrid solar + storage projects with various levels of storage capacity and duration, in addition to standalone solar and standalone storage projects. If the utility does not use a two-dimensional solar and storage ELCC surface to reflect the combined contributions of projects that are not co-located, its calculations may unfairly favor co-located projects where synergies are more obvious. This would prevent the utility from achieving the least cost portfolio. 
Q.Are there examples of utilities that utilize this methodology?
A.Yes, the most advanced utilities are using portfolio modeling approaches that are designed to consider variable renewable resources, energy storage, demand response, and other dispatch-limited resources alongside conventional generation such as natural gas. Specific utilities that use an ELCC methodology include Arizona Public Service, NV Energy, Xcel Energy, and many others around the country.
To provide one concrete example, E3 supported El Paso Electric Company (EPE) in its 2021 IRP. E3 utilized tools similar to the SERVM and Aurora models used by Georgia Power to select reliable resource portfolios. E3’s RECAP model was used to establish a reliability target and to calculate a two-dimensional surface for solar and storage ELCC values. The surface was then used to select portfolios in capacity expansion modeling in PLEXOS LT to identify an optimal resource portfolio for EPE. This approach captures the portfolio effects of integrating higher levels of solar generation while also considering the diversity benefits of adding combinations of solar, wind and energy storage. EPE then used the PLEXOS model to evaluate bids received through an all-source RFP. This process enabled EPE to select a least-cost resource portfolio on behalf of its ratepayers in both the IRP and the procurement phases.  
Q.how Does georgia power determine the capacity value of renewable energy and energy storage resources?
A. Georgia Power continues to treat renewable energy resources as standalone, energy-only resources, assigning them zero capacity value in its capacity expansion modeling.  Georgia Power models energy storage as standalone resources with energy arbitrage benefits and capacity benefits. A capacity value equivalent to the full nameplate capacity of battery storage is credited to the earliest storage resources integrated into the system. Subsequent energy storage resources are progressively discounted as their ELCC is reduced due to saturation effects.  Georgia Power accounts for this diminishing effect by parsing future energy storage assets into several different tranches, each with different capacity values, in the capacity expansion modeling. 
Q. ARE THERE CAPACITY ACCREDITATION CONCERNS REGARDING THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED ASSETS IN THIS IRP?

ERIS-type service is a non-firm service that may be curtailed during system stress. ERIS is a less robust transmission service, often requiring less study time and fewer physical upgrades as opposed to Network Resource Interconnection Service (NRIS). As described in a paper by the Nicholas Institute for Energy, Environment & Sustainability, FERC describes NRIS as follows:
“Specifically, a transmission provider studying a generating facility for NRIS would study the transmission system at peak load, under a variety of severely stressed conditions to determine whether, with the generating facility operating at full output, the aggregate of generation in the local area can be delivered to the aggregate of load, consistent with reliability criteria and procedures”
With Georgia Power’s 2022 capacity power purchase agreements approved by the PSC, firm network transmission service was required. When asked about the ERIS/NRIS designation for the proposed self-own batteries, Company witness Grubb stated he did not know the difference between the two services. The full 1.4 GW of capacity from the new units at Plant Yates and the 1 GW of new capacity from company-owned battery systems (some of which have not yet been identified or potentially entered into the queue) effectively cannot provide firm capacity without sufficient transmission delivery capability. The NRIS transmission service is a firm transmission service in which upgrades needed for firm delivery are identified. In summary, GPC appears to be relying on transmission delivery capability that may not be available, which could materially impact those facilities’ abilities to provide capacity during peak periods.

Transmission Planning
Briefly describe the activities through which Georgia power identifies transmission needs and constraints.
Georgia Power maintains a ten-year transmission plan with updates for the trailing year provided annually to the Public Service Commission. Additionally, FERC requires Georgia Power to participate in the Southeastern Regional Transmission Planning (SERTP) process, which is intended to provide stakeholders with an open and transparent transmission planning forum for the region. These transmission planning initiatives appear disjointed from each other and it is unclear how their results are incorporated into resource planning.
Historically, Georgia Power has performed reactionary transmission screening in response to resources as they are proposed.  In its 2022 IRP, Georgia Power proposed to more proactively guide siting of resources through geographically targeted RFPs. 
how does Georgia power incorporate transmission into its integrated resource planning?
Georgia Power does not appear to consider any transmission planning information in its IRP process. The IRP model treats Georgia Power’s system as a “copper sheet”, where resources are not subject to explicitly defined transmission constraints that may inhibit their ability to deliver energy to load centers; nor are any transmission costs added to individual candidate resources or resource groups to reflect transmission investments needed to deliver the energy to load. 
This requires transmission planners to react to the results of the resource plans, rather than coordinating mutual solutions. Because the resources are selected without regard to the potential transmission costs, transmission analysis can occur only for specific resources after they have been selected. This may result in the selection of seemingly lower-cost resources that require significant transmission upgrades (and are therefore higher-cost from a combined system perspective) over slightly resources that may have higher energy costs but require no transmission investments and are therefore ultimately lower-cost.
What are common approaches for better integrating transmission planning with resource planning?
Transmission planning, as it relates to resource planning and interconnection reform, is becoming more important as high-quality renewable resources are sited in areas farther from quickly growing load centers, requiring additional solutions to deliver that power to loads. As such, several new practices are emerging to better manage the influx of new resource additions needed to meet the significant load growth anticipated in the coming decades. Generally, I would highlight three common practices that are being embraced across the industry: (1) integrating transmission and resource planning as part of a more holistic planning process; (2) conducting more detailed analysis of transmission expansion opportunities; and (3) providing transparency of transmission constraints with resource providers.   
Q.What examples can you provide for integrating more holistic transmission and generation analyses, as suggested by practice #1?
A.Several utilities, including Salt River Project in Arizona and Xcel Energy in Colorado, have begun transitioning from integrated resource planning (IRP), to a more holistic integrated system planning (ISP) framework.  Among other things, an ISP more proactively incorporates transmission planning, hosting capacity, and non-wires alternatives into the resource planning process.  
The topic of planning framework reform has also recently been taken up by the Task Force on Comprehensive Electricity Planning, which is sponsored by the National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners (NARUC) and the National Association of State Energy Officials (NASEO).  In its report, the Task Force underscored the need for planning reform, saying, in part, that a more comprehensive approach to electricity system planning is necessary to ensure that investments are cost-effective and meet the needs of the grid and its customers, as well as deliver against policy goals. It can be more efficient to incorporate variables such as transmission congestion into the planning process than it is to react to them in the interconnection process.
Within the southeast, Duke Energy has recognized that a more proactive approach to transmission planning and expansion is needed to meet load growth and clean energy policy targets in their 2022 Clean Energy Plan for the Carolinas. Duke has also adopted a new Integrated System & Operations Planning (ISOP) framework that optimizes capacity and energy resource investments across generation, transmission, and its distribution system. With respect to transmission, the utility brought over half a billion dollars of transmission projects to the North Carolina Transmission Planning Collaborative (NCTPC), detailing investments necessary to facilitate more solar interconnections in the near term.  In its filing with the North Carolina Utilities Commission (NCUC), Duke notes that “a significant number of generator interconnection studies point to the same transmission constraints.” Duke concludes “if the pathway for planning, development and construction of these transmission projects, which have repeatedly been shown to be needed to support renewable generation, is limited to the traditionally reactive generator interconnection process, the timeline… is likely to be adversely impacted.”
Q.What examples can you provide where more detailed transmission data is developed, as identified by practice #2?
A.As transmission is integrated earlier and more comprehensively in the planning process, Practice #2 suggests that a more granular assessment of system constraints is necessary. Using a single zone transmission scheme to model resources is insufficient for coordinating resource siting with available transmission capacity, resulting in non-viable resources advancing further in the evaluation process than necessary. In ERCOT, dedicated transmission planning studies have been completed focused on defining designated renewable energy zones and identifying the transmission needs to deliver those remote renewable resources to load centers. This resulted in the creation of competitive renewable energy zones (CREZs) which drove substantial investment in renewable resources in the areas with the best resource potential and gave clear signals to developers about where to site their projects. This study spurred other CREZ-like studies across the country like Regional Generation Outlet Study (RGOS) in the MISO territory and the Western Renewable Energy Zone (WREZ) study in the West. These processes have identified long-term transmission investments that unlock generation resources that support a least-cost portfolio inclusive of both generation and transmission costs. 
More recently the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) has been evaluating new reforms to their interconnection process, which includes development of transmission zones based on the available transmission capacity and least-cost major transmission buildout. For priority zones, generator interconnection requests will be ranked on their viability while projects not located in priority zones will be relegated to a separate, lower-priority study process. In 2023, CAISO also adopted a new strategic framework to coordinate transmission planning with the interconnection of new generation and energy storage, recognizing the interrelated nature of resource planning, transmission planning, the interconnection process, and resource procurement. CAISO’s zonal planning framework prioritizes transmission expansion to regions where new clean energy resources can be built.  By communicating these transmission system limitations and working closely with integrated resource planners, California’s organized market is working to interconnect projects more effectively and efficiently to the places on the system that offer the most value.
Q.How are entities within the industry ensuring transparency of transmission constraints with resource providers, as suggested by practice #3?
A.With a more comprehensive understanding of transmission capacity, as it relates to new resources, Practice #3 calls for the utility to work with external stakeholders.  Transparency and clarity are what allow improvements in transmission planning to translate into efficient interconnection of viable resources.  In support of this concept, FERC recently instituted improvements to standard transmission interconnection practices. In brief, FERC Order 2023 requires transmission providers, including RTOs and regulated utilities, to make several changes to the way generators are assessed in the interconnection process, including evaluating resources in clusters and publishing a heat map indicating hosting capacity across the system. By sharing timely information about transmission capacity and deliverability by geographic area, new resources can be developed to target appropriate regions with minimal transmission needs and non-wires alternatives, such as storage sited as a transmission resource, can address constrained parts of the transmission grid.   
Q.DOES gEORGIA poWER’S USE OF GEOGRAPHICALLY TARGETED RFPS ADDRESS YOUR CONCERN ABOUT THEIR TRANSMISSION PLANNING PROCESS?
A.No. While targeted RFPs may be a useful way to address a specific need that can’t be addressed in any other way, targeted RFPs by their nature seek a limited set of solutions that only address a portion of Company’s needs. Targeted RFPs limit the solution space and may exclude resources that are strong “all-around performers” because they are not the best resource to meet the specified need. In particular, targeted RFPs that focus only on generation resources only would be unable to address the tradeoffs between transmission and generation solutions to a location-specific need. 
What recommendations do you have for georgia power to improve the integration of transmission planning into resource planning?
I recommend that Georgia Power more proactively integrate transmission planning into the early stages of a broader planning effort, identify more granular transmission constraints and communicate those to external stakeholders, include zonal transmission constraints in its capacity expansion modeling, and reflect those insights in its integrated resource plan.  Sharing this information with independent power providers will encourage siting resources in more advantageous locations, possibly mitigating the need for investment in new transmission infrastructure and reducing costs for Georgia Power ratepayers.  
Resource Procurement
PLEASE DESCRIBE COMMON PRINCIPLES FOR BEST PRACTICES IN RESOURCE PROCUREMENT.
A. Resource planning and procurement are inherently linked, and the associated activities should be coordinated accordingly. Overall, procurement decisions should minimize ratepayer costs while managing demand, reliability, and long-term strategic goals of the utility, as overseen by the PSC. Specific principles that support this directive include: (1) transparency; (2) fair consideration; and (3) objective evaluation.  Transparency of data and processes allows for information to be shared effectively between the utility and stakeholders, which helps to improve the quality of decisions and compatibility of solutions. Fairness in considering candidate resources is necessary to understand all available options and is best accomplished through all-source competitive solicitations. Once resource bids are received, an objective evaluation can be conducted using accurate, impartial methods to select the most cost-effective solutions to address portfolio needs.
Q.What support do you have for these best practices?
A.Best practices in resource procurement are less established and continue to evolve; however, useful principles can be found in the “Edgar” standard, which was established in a 1991 FERC ruling. The three specific components of FERC’s Edgar standard provide for: 
“a competitive solicitation process was designed and implemented”; 
impartial bid evaluation, “particularly with respect to non-price factors”; and, 
resource selection “based on some reasonable combination of price and non-price factors.”  
FERC later elaborated on these criteria, articulating four principles to determine whether an RFP satisfied the Edgar test: 
Transparency: the competitive solicitation process should be open and fair; 
Definition: the product or products sought through the competitive solicitation should be precisely defined; 
Evaluation: evaluation criteria should be standardized and applied equally to all bids and bidders; 
Oversight: an independent third party should design the solicitation, administer bidding, and evaluate bids prior to the company’s selection.”  
It is important to note that these criteria were developed with specific regard for transactions between affiliates, in which extra care is required. While this federal standard is not binding for a state commission, I cite these principles as considerations for best practices that should be applied for all of Georgia Power’s resource procurements.  
Q.Do you have an example of a state that does a good job of implementing these best practices for utility-owned generation or affiliate transactions?
A.The Colorado Public Utility Commission has embraced the concept of fairness in considering candidate resources through all-source solicitations.  Like the Georgia PSC, Colorado requires resource needs to be procured through a competitive solicitation; however, the Colorado PUC further stipulates all-source RFPs are preferred and utilities must provide specific reasons, supported by a cost-benefit analysis, to conduct an alternative solicitation. This approach correctly recognizes that different resources offer combinations of benefits to the system; given the opportunity, renewable energy and energy storage resources can compete with alternatives and meaningfully contribute to a reliable and diverse portfolio as I discussed above. All-source solicitations ensure the diverse nature of candidate resources are evaluated together, and that the optimal blend of resources that meets a combination of system needs is selected, in contrast to solicitations for isolated system needs.
Arkansas, California, Illinois, and Texas are all examples of states with affiliate transaction rules for electric utilities. These rules are in place to ensure transparency and fairness to protect ratepayers, aligned with the intentions of the FERC’s Edgar ruling and standard. In California, the California Public Utilities Commission has conducted independent audits of the large, regulated utilities in the State with respect to affiliate transactions. Other jurisdictions have various approaches to regulation. 
how does georgia power’s procurement process compare with industry best practices?
There are several occasions in which Georgia Power deviates from best practice in resource procurement in its 2023 IRP Update. For example, Georgia Power has failed to meet transparency and objectivity expectations in its expedited contracts with Mississippi Power and Santa Rosa. The Commission should consider what standard to use for affiliate transactions, such as that which was executed on October 11, 2023, given the FERC Edgar ruling was written with the intent to provide guidance around affiliate transactions. It is worth noting that other state public service commissions have rules regarding affiliate transactions that incorporate competitive bidding requirements and provide procedures for Commission review and approval of such affiliate transactions prior to execution.
In the absence of a competitive process, the resources proposed in Georgia Power’s 2023 IRP Update should be benchmarked against comparable transactions to ensure that costs are prudent for Georgia ratepayers. Failure to comply with these standards may result in higher costs for Georgia Power ratepayers.

WHAT METHODOLOGY DOES GEORGIA POWER USE FOR EVALUATING RENEWABLE ENERGY RESOURCE BIDS?
A.Georgia Power uses a “best cost” methodology based on a renewable cost benefits (RCB) framework to evaluate renewable energy resources. The RCB framework includes components like avoided energy costs, deferred generation capacity costs, avoided transmission losses, deferred transmission investment, and avoided distribution losses to fully assess the value of renewable resources.  The best cost methodology is essentially an avoided cost method without a firm threshold for avoided costs that might limit resource selection.     
What are the limitations of Georgia Power’s selected methodology?
Avoided Cost methodologies are simplified analyses based on a “snapshot” of the projected utility portfolio at a given moment in time. They are commonly used by utilities in lieu of more sophisticated portfolio analysis in specific instances where portfolio analysis is impractical or where benefits might be realized that are not captured in portfolio analysis.  Marginal values are determined for each component based on that snapshot and are assumed not to change across the entire quantity of the resource being evaluated. For example, marginal energy values are typically based on either (a) a wholesale market price that is assumed to be independent of the addition of the resource being evaluated, or (b) the variable cost of the most expensive resource in a utility’s dispatch stack in each hour of the year, which again is assumed not to change with the addition of the resource being evaluated. This framework is appropriate for evaluating relatively small quantities of resources in portfolio that is otherwise fixed. In general, conducting a more comprehensive portfolio analysis is better suited for evaluating the full range of costs and benefits of significant quantities of additional resources.
Avoided Cost methodologies are not well-suited to evaluating resources that can interact with other resources in the portfolio in complex ways, for example resources that can be dispatched in response to changing marginal energy costs. Most DERs cannot be dispatched by the system operator and in fact are not even visible to the system operator, and as a result an Avoided Cost framework is appropriate for evaluating these resources. However, newer solar power plants have the capability to be optimally dispatched by system operators and to provide essential grid services. Valuation of these services is complex and may require dynamic modeling of system-wide production. 
Furthermore, Avoided Cost methodologies are not well-suited to evaluating energy storage resources. Energy storage can provide significant value by displacing expensive peaking resources during high load hours and reducing the quantity of thermal generation required to meet system peaks.  However, its energy value is dependent on the resources used for charging and its capacity contribution is complicated by considerations related to its limited duration and need to maintain a high state of charge. Valuation of energy storage may also require portfolio-level modeling.
HOW SHOULD GEORGIA POWER EVALUATE RENEWABLE ENERGY AND ENERGY STORAGE RESOURCES IN PROCUREMENT?
As with many other utilities, Georgia Power uses avoided cost methodologies in bid evaluation. As long as the amount of new resources under consideration does not fundamentally change system conditions, this can be methodologically sound. However, because Avoided Cost methodologies develop individual, static valuations for each potential benefit stream, there is a risk that the method will double count, not count, or inappropriately count certain value streams if the values aren’t combined in a way that preserves the appropriate portfolio-level value. 
A better method for bid evaluation is to use portfolio optimization tools. These models are well suited for bid evaluation: when given a list of resource options, the models will select the economically efficient outcome, i.e., the least-cost outcome for ratepayers. Although it is important that the correct inputs be given to the model, the analytical framework has been vetted by the developers and other utilities, reducing the likelihood of errors that lead to inefficient outcomes. I recommend that Georgia Power use portfolio modeling tools for bid evaluation. 
Q. HOW MIGHT GEORGIA POWER IMPROVE THEIR RESOURCE PROCUREMENT PROCESS TO ALLOW FOR MORE TIMELY RESOURCE ADDITIONS?
A. Georgia Power’s experience with resource procurement is not altogether unique.  Competitive solicitations are inherently time-intensive processes that are difficult to accelerate without negatively impacting the quality of the results.  Georgia Power can maximize the effectiveness of these solicitations by issuing all-source RFPs on a more standardized periodicity, stemming from the needs identified in the IRP cycle. Implementing this regularity can avoid one-off, ad-hoc solicitations, such as the RFI issued on September 26, 2023 and the executed PPAs with affiliate Mississippi Power for 750 MW on October 11, 2023, and with Santa Rosa for 230 MW on October 22, 2023, respectively. While the results of the RFI indicated that Georgia Power could potentially meet its near-term needs with energy storage or solar in the 2025-2028 period, including 1,200 MW by 2026, the lack of a standard, regular competitive bid solicitation process disallowed any opportunity for Georgia Power to explore the optimal solution to meet their needs by evaluating the Santa Rosa and Mississippi Power PPAs alongside bids from developers. Additionally, by ensuring the capacity expansion model can accurately optimize the desired portfolio, Georgia Power can fully leverage the time that is invested in the procurement process and eliminate the need to conduct resource-specific RFPs. Finally, by sharing zonal transmission data with resource developers, Georgia Power will improve the quality and viability of the proposals that are returned and eliminate the need to run location-specific RFPs. Increased transparency will also allow for a more streamlined transmission screening and eliminate the need for regionally focused RFPs.
Conclusion 
WHAT ARE YOUR KEY FINDINGS AND RECOMMENDATIONS? 
In response to Georgia Power’s 2023 IRP Update, I provide the following conclusions and recommendations:
Georgia Power is arbitrarily limiting renewable energy and energy storage resources in its procurement plan by: 
instituting unnecessary limits on how much the capacity expansion model can select;
assigning solar resources a zero capacity value under the Aurora model; and
failing to capture the diversity benefit of solar energy and battery storage in providing capacity.
Therefore, I recommend the Commission direct Georgia Power to update its treatment of renewable energy and energy storage resources in its capacity expansion analysis to do the following:
remove any artificial limit for specific resources;
assign solar resources a calculated capacity value in modeling, consistent with its 2022 IRP and the associated ELCC study; and
ensure the diversity benefits of solar and energy storage resources are captured in modeling.
Georgia Power’s transmission planning is insufficient for allowing coordination with resource planning. To correct this, the Commission should:
direct Georgia Power to provide more transparency in its transmission constraints to allow stakeholders to better understand how resources are valued in various zones and allow for non-wire alternatives to be more easily and cooperatively developed; and
incorporate transmission constraints and upgrade capacity information into its capacity expansion modeling to ensure that the resources selected can deliver energy to load when it is most needed. 
Georgia Power’s procurement practices are inefficient and do not follow industry best practices.  Therefore, the Commission should:
direct Georgia Power to follow industry best practices for resource procurement, including standards similar to the Edgar standards adopted by FERC, in its future resource procurement, including the upcoming 2025 IRP; and
direct Georgia Power to conduct all-source RFPs on a regular periodicity and develop a bid evaluation methodology that accurately captures the energy, capacity and transmission benefits and costs of candidate resources.
The Commission should revise its guidance to reduce the amount of utility-owned generation that is required to be maintained by Georgia Power and direct Georgia Power to procure the optimal, most cost-effective mix of resources, regardless of ownership type.

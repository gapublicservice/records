Question: 

Refer to the Companies response to STF-JKA-1-27, “In developing and applying the marginal cost models used in economic analysis, the Company verifies the underlying principle behind the capital substitution component. Peaker theorem states that the total marginal cost of serving incremental load in an optimally planned system can be reliably calculated as the marginal running cost in every hour (lambda) and the capital costs of peaking generation. This will be true regardless of the resources that are deployed to serve that load in an economically optimal matter. The economic justification for building a more fixed cost intensive form of generation and associated FT would be that the resulting energy benefit outweighs the additional capital costs.”

a.Provide the Company’s verification analysis of the capital substitution component. If the Company has not performed such analysis, please explain why not.
b.What cost of Peaker capacity has the Company relied on to determine the capital substitution component? Provide all supporting workpapers and explain assumptions made for the analysis.

Response:

Capital substitution is not a component of the real time pricing (“RTP”) price that is set or determined in advance through analysis. Rather, capital substitution reflects the delta between the marginal cost of energy (lambda), the average cost of energy (RTP fuel credits), and generation variable operations and maintenance (“O&M”) in each hour. That delta is credited to base where it offsets fixed costs beyond the peaking capacity priced and collected separately through the reliability component of RTP. These additional contributions to base from capital substitution reflect that in a least cost resource selection, the most economic resources to serve load are not always peaking units. Baseload or intermediate resources will be selected if the savings from lower operating costs over the life of the resource are greater than the additional capital costs. This is the foundation of resource planning and request for proposals (“RFP”) evaluations to select the resource plan with the lowest total production costs that provides the necessary reliability. This optimized resource mix puts downward pressure on fuel costs but results in higher fixed costs to be recovered through base, which are partially offset by the capital substitution contribution from RTP customers. 

Please see the response to subpart (a) above. The cost of peaking capacity is recovered through the reliability component of RTP prices and has no bearing on the capital substitution contribution nor the fuel cost recovery clause.

Refer to MFRH-3.1.xlsx which states, “The Company considers various operational, transmission, reliability, and fuel-related limitations and constraints to optimize the system to minimize total system production costs and maintain reliable service. Recently, there has been an increase in risk of supply availability due to less capability and flexibility from coal producers and transportation providers. When compared to the planned burn activities of years past, which were driven solely on each plant’s physical inventory limit, the Company now engages in monthly coal management activities to better manage inventories incrementally within established inventory limits, while ensuring availability and reliability of supply, and to manage coal burn economically given the operational constraints in place at the time. With the more frequent cadence of coal management, along with the overlapping impacts of transmission must-run requirements, minimum up-time requirements, and other operational constraints, the Company cannot track whether a specific coal unit was run due to coal inventory management, or to assign costs to specific operational constraints.”

a.Please explain what the Company means by “less capability and flexibility from coal producers and transportation providers.”
b.When during the review period did the Company decide to begin engaging monthly coal management activities? Please explain.
c.Please describe what monthly coal management activities are conducted, including any decision making processes, modeling tools utilized for such activities, or policies/procedures established through this process.

Response:

The coal industry has seen significant decline since its peak in the late 2000s, coinciding with the closure of coal units across many of the utilities around the country. As is the trend among utilities in the United States, the Company currently burns 60-70% less than it did at its peak. The result has been a rationalization of coal supply and a repurposing of railroad assets (locomotives and crews). Accordingly, there is less supply of coal and fewer assets dedicated to its delivery from the Company’s coal vendors.

The Company has always engaged in real-time management of the coal supply chain to ensure affordable and reliable delivery of coal. Given the decrease in supply chain flexibility, the Company added a formal monthly review of forecasted coal plant operation and expected coal deliveries in 2023.

Summary and Steps of Coal Inventory Management Process
Fleet Operations and Coal Services meet with key stakeholders to review the monthly operational forecast results from the ‘OpsView’ model, as well as the associated underlying assumptions and inputs. 

Key Inputs & Considerations
Plant Inventory Targets, Minimums and Maximums 
Minimums ensure continued unit reliability by maintaining adequate fuel supply.
Maximums ensure stockpile management activities – including stock-out operations - can be carried out safely and effectively.
Contracted Coal Volumes
Amount of coal contracted/purchased for upcoming year/s
Any coal scheduled to roll over from previous year
Contractual Delivery Flexibility
Degree of flexibility within the coal supply and transportation agreements (e.g., ability to adjust deliveries or redirect supply).
Monthly Burn Data
Monthly burn targets by unit and/or plant to keep train sets cycling smoothly.
Actual Monthly burn levels needed to support ongoing and safe stock-out activities.
Unit Outage Schedules
Planned unit unavailability and its impact on coal consumption patterns.
Historical Seasonal Load Patterns
Typical high-load and low-load periods inform the development of a base plan that aligns with the Ops View forecast and provides a realistic planning baseline.
Operational Flexibility Needs – The plan must preserve the ability for Unit Commitment and Real-Time Operations to:
Increase generation during peak or other reliability-driven periods.
Decrease generation to prevent bottoming out.
Adjust generation dynamically to respond to day-ahead and real-time requirements.
Economically commit and dispatch the units within system and contractual constraints.
 
Using the coal supply and delivery requirements alongside the ‘OpsView’ forecast, an annual year-end burn target is developed for each unit or plant.
An operational profile is developed to align unit operations with the monthly burn target (guideline).
The team meets monthly to review actual burn compared to target burn, along with any operational concerns or emerging risks stemming from weather, load, unit availability, fuel prices, delivery schedules, and other variables. 
Adjustments are made as needed to ensure targets are met and objectives are achieved at the lowest cost possible.

STF-JKA-3-13

Question: 

Refer to files provided in MFRP-1 deriving TOU-FCR-7 and TOU-FCR- TP-5 rates. Provide a narrative explanation regarding the derivation of TOU-FCR-7 and TOU-FCR-TP. Explain if the derivation relies only on a projected lambda, or if it relies on forecasted fuel costs including costs associated with hedging, FT, and sales, similar to or different from the derivation of FCR-27 rate.

Response:

TOU-FCR-7 and TOU-FCR-TP-5 are revenue-neutral to FCR-27 at each service level and thus have identical cost recovery over projected kWh sales in the test period. The peak and off-peak period rates used in the Time-of-Use (“TOU”) fuel tariffs are derived by applying a ratio to the proposed non-seasonal FCR-27 prices by service level. Those ratios are calculated using the weighted marginal cost of energy (i.e., lambda) by TOU period over the projected test period. Therefore, while the marginal fuel cost (lambda) determines the ratio of peak to off-peak price, that ratio is applied to a fully embedded fuel rate, which is set to recover all recoverable fuel costs in this filing. The design of the TOU fuel tariffs incentivizes shifting consumption to Off-Peak and Super-Off-Peak periods such that any reduction in revenue collection as a result of behavioral change would reflect lower fuel expense incurred.

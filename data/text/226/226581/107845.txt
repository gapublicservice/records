                                            INTRODUCTION


        Georgia Power’s premises in this case are that it is entitled to 100% recovery of fuel costs
and that it is under no obligation to prove that its coal dispatching practices are economic. Both
premises are invalid. First, O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26 allows for disallowance when the Company’s
actions were “illegal or clearly imprudent” — but not exclusively in those cases. Since the
Commission has disallowed $14.5 million over the last fifteen years without a specific finding of
illegality or imprudent activity, 1 then the statute clearly allows for cost sharing between the
Company and its ratepayers. Although $14.5 million is a trivial amount in comparison to a
conservative estimate of $29 billion dollars spent by Georgia Power on fuel costs during the
same period, the Commission’s past practice demonstrates that it has statutory authority to limit
the Company’s recovery.
        Second, Georgia Power’s testimony regarding its uneconomic dispatch of coal suggests
that the Company wants to have its cake and eat it too: it fails to meet its burden to show that its
dispatch was in fact economic, while simultaneously seeking to discredit testimony that uses the
best available alternative methodology to evaluate whether that dispatch was in fact economic.
Intervenors had to use an alternative methodology because the Company does not allow other
intervenors (nor Staff) to use its proprietary software to confirm Georgia Power’s claims that
there were no cheaper available options when it dispatched coal. 2 Using system lambda, which
“reflects the marginal cost of the highest cost unit dispatched across the Company’s system to
meet electricity demand in that hour” and is “the best metric of the marginal cost of producing
electricity in that hour,” Sierra Club, SACE and NRDC’s Witness Michael Goggin found that the
Company incurred in $152 million in net losses due to the imprudent dispatch decisions at plants
Bowen and Scherer.3 Disallowance of this amount “will protect ratepayers from shouldering
these imprudently incurred costs, and more importantly will incentivize the Company to avoid
uneconomic dispatch going forward.”4

                                                   ARGUMENT


      1. The Commission Should Implement a Fuel Cost Sharing Mechanism
               A. Fuel Cost Sharing in Georgia
           The Commission should direct Georgia Power to share a percentage of the fuel costs that
the Company seeks to recover in this proceeding. One-hundred percent fuel cost recovery is not
mandatory nor set in stone under Georgia law. While Georgia’s fuel cost recovery statute allows
Georgia Power to pass along all of fuel costs to ratepayers, it does not require it. O.C.G.A. § 46-
2-26(c) merely directs the utility to propose “base rate tariffs to recover [fuel] costs,” but does
not mention that costs must be 100% passed through to customers. While O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h)
establishes that the Commission shall disallow fuel costs that are “the result of illegal or clearly
imprudent conduct on the part of the utility,” it does not preclude the Commission from
disallowing fuel costs on other bases, i.e., as part of a fuel cost sharing mechanism.
Commissioner Hubbard correctly identified that the Company and the Commission regularly add
and remove costs from the fuel tariff, such as firm transportation or Power Purchase Agreement
(PPA) costs. Therefore, the Commission can likewise implement a fuel cost sharing mechanism
where shareholders pay a portion of the costs. There is plenty of room under the statute to
implement a fuel cost sharing mechanism and, for the reasons discussed over the course of this
proceeding, the Commission should do so in this case.
           As Sierra Club, SACE and NRDC’s Witnesses Michael Goggin and Jeremy Kalin
testified in this proceeding, a “moral hazard” occurs when one party is incentivized to take more
risk than they normally would because that party is insulated against that risk. Here, “the
Company is the agent who makes the decision, but the ratepayer is the principal who pays the
cost of that decision.”6 When the consequences of a decision are so disconnected from the

             C. The Commission Should Open a Fuel Cost Recovery Modernization
        In addition to that RTP-specific proceeding supported below, the Commission should
open a Fuel Cost Recovery modernization docket to explore fuel-cost-sharing and fuel-risk-
sharing by the utility rather than continue the current moral hazard policy of passing through
100% of fuel costs to ratepayers. This Fuel Cost Recovery modernization docket should address,
among other factors, the proper amount of fuel cost and fuel risk sharing to be assigned to
Georgia Power, transparency of key contract terms, and reliance on forecasts versus actual
historic pricing.25 This docket would explore mechanisms to fairly share the risk of fuel cost
changes between Georgia Power and ratepayers, and analyze at what percentage Georgia Power
is sufficiently incentivized to reasonably manage its fuel costs.

     4. RTP proceeding
         Sierra Club, SACE and NRDC support the Stipulation’s paragraph 6 creating a separate
proceeding in which “[t]he Commission shall further review the methodology by which the
Company credits RTP revenue to the FCR revenues and the allocations that the Company is
making under such methodology in this Docket.” As Staff testified, there are “discrepancies in
the treatment of RTP revenue used to offset FT costs recovered in fuel rates” as well as in take-
or-pay contracts.45 Consequently, Staff recommended that the Commission “open an
investigation docket to consider if the RTP fuel credit methodology and the Company’s
application of the methodology going forward is sufficient to protect non-RTP customers from
cost-shifts associated with future load growth.”46 While it has been the Company’s recurring
position that data centers will pay for their fair share of the costs of bringing more generation
online to meet their needs, “[t]he Company’s FCR-27 application appears to require that a
significant amount of new data centers be built in order to achieve the claimed benefits or
general ratepayer protections,”47, and:
              the Company has not clearly stated the extent to which additional fuel costs
              will be incurred regardless of whether large loads are realized (in other
              words, whether large data centers are actually built and begin operating), or
              if some fuel costs will be incurred by the company and cannot be curtailed
              or avoided should the large load growth not be realized, or fall short of the
              forecasted load growth.48

         With FCR-27, the Company would have committed significant sunk costs into serving
these large loads, including hedging premiums and firm transportation costs incurred years in
advance of new loads coming online: “All of the Company’s ratepayers will face these increased
hedging premiums if any project large load is not realized within the FCR-27 period, or at all.”49

                                                CONCLUSION


         Georgia Power has asked this Commission to continue a framework in which the
Company bears none of the consequences of its own fuel‑related decisions while ratepayers bear
all of them. The record in this proceeding demonstrates why that framework harms ratepayers.
The Company’s uneconomic coal dispatch, its persistent over‑reliance on gas and its repeated
procurement of above‑market coal, demonstrate that when fuel costs are 100% passed through to
customers, the utility has no meaningful incentive to minimize them. O.C.G.A. § 46‑2‑26 does
not require the Commission to accept that result.
         For these reasons, Sierra Club, SACE, and NRDC respectfully request that the
Commission (1) disallow $152 million in imprudently incurred coal‑dispatch costs, 51 (2) reject
the Company’s proposed expansion of the IFR mechanism and maintain the current cap (or,
alternatively, adopt paragraph 5(c) of the proposed Stipulation), (3) adopt paragraph 7 of the
Stipulation denying the Company’s request to expand its hedging program, and (4) open a Fuel
Cost Recovery modernization docket to evaluate appropriate fuel‑ and fuel‑risk‑sharing reforms,
in addition to adopting paragraph 6 of the Stipulation.

Respectfully submitted this 14th day of May 2026.

DEREK

Derek HasBrouck advises utility industry clients and investors on topics ranging from utility strategy to operations
improvement. He is an expert in benchmarking electric and gas utility businesses, utility regulation, and network
reliability. He has led a number of after action reviews of restoration activities following major storms. He is currently
leading the Winter Storm Fer after action review for Nashville Electric Service. For Vermont Electric Power Company,
he led the planning and creation of a new electric transmission company, VT Transco LLC. Derek served as the Chief
Financial Officer of VT Transco as it raised and deployed $750M to construct new transmission lines throughout
Vermont. He has served as a Third-Party Neutral for construction disputes on a major transmission construction
project, advised un-insured bondholders in the financial restructurings of the Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority
and has led due diligence activities for the purchase or sale of interests in numerous electric, gas and water utilities.

RESPONSE TO PROPOSED RATE OF RETURN REDUCTIONS AND DISALLOWANCES

A.PIA Staff and GIPL collectively suggest that the Commission reject the Company’s request for return on the storm cost regulatory asset at its grossed-up weighted average cost of capital (“WACC”). Specifically, the PIA Staff panel recommends that the Commission authorize a debt-only rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset. GIPL witness Barnes recommends that, if the Commission approves an amortization recovery mechanism for under-recovered storm damage costs, then the Commission should eliminate the Company’s return entirely. Alternatively, if the Commission were to opt for anything other than a full elimination of the Company’s return, Witness Barnes suggests that the Commission set the return rate at the Company’s cost of long-term debt. GAM witness Pollock testifies that using debt financing, including the potential possibility of securitization, would be a more appropriate alternative to address affordability.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH THE PIA STAFF AND INTERVENORS’ RATE OF RETURN PROPOSALS?
A.No. The Company does not agree with these proposals because they are fundamentally inconsistent with the purpose of this docket, which is to provide recovery of the full revenue requirement associated with the extraordinary storm costs the Company incurred to maintain safe and reliable service for customers. A full revenue requirement necessarily includes both (1) timely recovery of prudently incurred storm restoration costs and (2) an appropriate return on the related regulatory asset at the Company’s WACC, reflecting the actual mix of debt and equity used to finance those costs.
The Company does not project finance any of its retail rate base assets with specific equity issuances or debt obligations. Deferred storm costs, just like any other retail rate base item, are financed by the Company as a whole with debt and equity based on the capital structure currently approved by the Commission in the previous base rate case. Considering that, providing a debtonly return, or eliminating the return entirely, would not provide the appropriate revenues to cover for the financing costs that were appropriately incurred by the Company to pay for the storm costs used to restore service to the customers it is obligated to serve. This would unduly penalize the Company for performing its service to the best of its ability. As demonstrated by its actions during the storms—and as confirmed by the utility industry—the Company performed effectively, if not admirably, given the significant devastation and impacts caused by the storms, especially Hurricane Helene. Likewise, there is no finding of any imprudent actions recommended by the PIA Staff or any Intervenors. The Company should not be denied recovery of the cost of financing by having its authorized return on its storm restoration efforts reduced. 
Accordingly, the Company continues to support recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset at its grossedup WACC, consistent with the treatment of other regulatory assets and with the Commission’s historic approach of allowing a fair opportunity to earn the authorized return on prudently incurred investments made to provide reliable service.
Q.HAS THE COMPANY PREVIOUSLY BEEN ALLOWED ITS FULL WACC ON ITS STORM RESERVE?
A.Yes. The Commission has allowed the Company a return based on its full cost of capital, including debt and equity, on the deferred storm balance regulatory asset or liability in prior base rate cases. Q.HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO GIPL WITNESS BARNES’ CHARACTERIZATION OF DEFERRED STORM COSTS AS O&M EXPENSES?
A.There is a fundamental difference between O&M expenses versus costs deferred to the regulatory asset balance, such as storm costs. Under typical utility ratemaking, O&M expenses are recovered immediately from customers as the Company incurs them. In that circumstance, there are no, or very limited, financing costs incurred by the Company since costs are recovered on a timely basis. On the other hand, if costs are no longer expensed as O&M and deferred to a regulatory asset, the Company incurs financing costs on the unrecovered balance. In that respect, for purposes of ratemaking, the deferred costs in a regulatory asset are no different from capitalized costs for a plant asset in terms of the financing costs that are incurred and the need to be recovered from customers. 
However, a deferral cost mechanism benefits customers from both a timing and an accounting perspective. For timing, compared to an immediate recovery of O&M expenses, the recovery of a deferred regulatory asset can be extended for a much longer period of time, such as the four-year recovery period proposed by the Company in this filing. From an accounting perspective, a deferred regulatory asset ensures that customers only pay for actual costs of the storms incurred by the Company. 
Q.WHAT DOES GAM WITNESS POLLOCK SUGGEST REGARDING DEBT FINANCING?
A.Witness Pollock testifies that using debt financing, including the potential possibility of securitization, would be a more appropriate alternative to address affordability.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO WITNESS POLLOCK’S DEBT FINANCING PROPOSAL?
A.The Company does not agree that using debt financing is a more appropriate way to address affordability. The Company maintains that a balanced capital structure, including both debt and equity, is necessary to preserve strong credit quality and ensure access to capital on reasonable terms. The Company does not finance its deferred storm costs with 100% debt but rather with a combination of equity and debt using a capital structure previously approved by the Commission. The Company believes affordability should be managed through prudent ratemaking and regulatory mechanisms, not by materially increasing debt leverage. In this particular case, the Company’s request takes into account affordability and leverages both PTCs and the reduction in fuel rates to ensure that customers’ overall rates will be lower as a result of this request being approved. We will discuss this further below. Moreover, a disallowance of a key component of the Company’s financing would adversely impact the utility and could place upward pressure on affordability.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO WITNESS POLLOCK’S SECURITIZATION PROPOSAL?
A.Securitization is neither permissible under current Georgia law, nor does the Commission need this tool to ensure that costs are recovered appropriately. There are currently adequate tools within the existing statutory framework and the Commission rules and regulations to address rate impacts for customers in this proceeding. 
Q.HOW HAS THE COMPANY ADDRESSED AFFORDABILITY FOR CUSTOMERS IN THIS FILING?
A.The Company considered and made numerous adjustments in this filing to reduce rate impacts for customers. As mentioned in the Company’s direct testimony, the Company voluntarily included a portion of the 45U PTCs for the benefit of customers as a reduction to the revenue requirement requested in this filing. To note, the ARP Extension Stipulation did not call for any PTCs to be applied to the storm revenue requirement in this proceeding. 
In addition, as discussed in further detail below, the Company set the proposed amortization period of the prior under-recovered balance to four years. In a typical base rate case, the Company has traditionally filed for a three-year amortization period for its recovery of prior storm costs to align with the occurrence of the next expected base rate cycle. In this case, the Company also considered the option of an amortization period of two years and seven months to match the end of the ARP Extension period. While this would have aligned with previous practice by aligning with the next expected base rate case cycle, the Company understood that this would have caused greater affordability concerns for customers. Q. PLEASE EXPLAIN IN DETAIL HOW THE COMPANY CALCULATES CAPITAL PLANT TRANSFERS FOR DISTRIBUTION LABOR INCURRED DURING STORMS. 
A.As part of the storm capitalization process for distribution, and in accordance with the Company’s established capitalization policy, capital estimates for storm-related assets are based on actual materials installed and labor estimates required to install those materials. Total labor costs are initially recorded to base and storm reserve accounts rather than directly to capital accounts, and labor capitalization is subsequently recorded through journal entries based on labor estimates.

These labor estimates used for capitalization are derived from the Job Estimating and Tracking System (“JETS”) tool, which does not differentiate between straight-time and overtime labor rates. During storm restoration events, Company crews routinely work significant overtime hours, which are compensated at a rate of 150% of straight-time pay. As a result, the use of unadjusted standard straight-time labor rates would not reasonably reflect the actual labor costs incurred to restore and install storm-related assets.

To address this fact, and to ensure that capitalized labor amounts reasonably approximate actual labor costs incurred during storm restoration, an incremental adder of 25% is appropriately applied within JETS to standard labor rates for distribution mass assets associated with storm events. This adder reflects the increased proportion of overtime labor worked during restoration activities, assuming the mix of straight-time labor and overtime labor is approximately equal, and serves as a reasonable and consistent proxy for overtime rates that cannot be directly captured within the estimating system. The use of a fixed percentage adder provides a consistent, systematic, and reasonable approach to approximating overtime labor premiums within the capitalization process without requiring project-specific labor tracking.
Q.WHY IS THE COMPANY’S CALCULATION A MORE REASONABLE APPROACH THAN PIA STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION? 
A.The Company’s calculation is consistent with the Company’s historical treatment of storm restoration costs. Under this longstanding approach, initial labor costs associated with storms are recorded to both base O&M (for straight-time labor) and the storm regulatory asset. Once a JETS estimate is developed, the Company reviews the amounts charged to base O&M and the storm reserve and transfers a proportional share of labor costs to capital plant from both sources. The Company’s 25% adder, therefore, is appropriately intended to capture a mix of both straight-time labor recorded to O&M and the internal labor overtime recorded to the regulatory asset. PIA Staff’s recommendation of using a 150% adder associated entirely with overtime labor rate ignores the capital plant estimate in total, a portion of which is transferred from O&M. The Company’s process for crediting base O&M and the storm regulatory asset is grounded in historical experience and operational expertise and represents a reasonable and accurate approximation of the actual costs incurred during storm restoration activities.
The Company correctly calculated the external contractor capital costs to be excluded from the storm cost regulatory asset.
Q.PLEASE DISCUSS PIA STAFF’S PROPOSAL REGARDING THE COMPANY’S CAPITALIZATION OF EXTERNAL CONTRACTOR COSTS.
A.According to PIA Staff, the Company’s methodology incorrectly excludes all external line contractor costs, all external vegetation management contractor costs, and all staging costs from capital plant transfers. Based on these arguments, PIA Staff concludes that the Company overstated the costs in the regulatory asset and understated the costs recorded to plant in service. To address this, PIA Staff recommends that the Commission move a minimum of 20% of these costs to capital.
Q.IS PIA STAFF’S JUSTIFICATION FOR CAPITALIZING 20% OF EXTERNAL CONTRACTOR COSTS ARBITRARY? 
A.Yes. PIA Staff unreasonably relies on storm cost capitalization percentages of a non-jurisdictional utility without context, analysis, or other relevant information. According to PIA Staff, the recommended 20% capitalization percentage is roughly half of the percentage that Duke Energy Carolinas capitalized. However, PIA Staff provided no contextual or empirical evidence demonstrating either the appropriateness of this comparison or the recommended capitalization percentage beyond bringing the Company’s capitalization level closer in line with a non-jurisdictional utility that PIA Staff handpicked for comparison. Such logic demonstrates the arbitrary nature of PIA Staff’s recommendation. 
Q. WHY IS PIA STAFF’S RELIANCE ON THE CAPITALIZATION LEVELS OF OTHER UTILITIES MISGUIDED AND UNREASONABLE? 
A.As stated above, PIA Staff recommends that the Commission consider the capitalization level of a non-jurisdictional utility without supporting empirical evidence or context. PIA Staff relies upon the fact that Duke Energy Carolinas capitalized 42% of its costs related to Hurricane Helene. This simple analysis is misguided and fails to look behind a particular percentage to better understand the impacts of the storm and the assets being replaced or removed that could vary significantly based on each storm event. Thus, a simplistic analysis of percentages, without more in-depth consideration of the factors making up that percentage, provides little value to this Commission. 
Q.ARE THERE OTHER REASONS WHY PIA STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION IS FLAWED?
A.Yes. The Company has historically treated storm support costs, such as staging costs or the cost of temporary housing for contractors, as incremental costs and has therefore not capitalized these costs. Under the capitalization policy, staging costs incurred during storms are not directly associated with the construction of capital assets. In addition, the Company excludes vegetation management costs incurred during storms from the capital plant transfers to be consistent with FERC guidance, which only allows a utility to capitalize the initial clearing for new construction, not secondary clearing or cleanup work associated with storm restoration activities. Thus, PIA Staff’s recommendation to capitalize 20% of external contractor costs—costs that substantially include both staging costs and vegetation management costs—ignores the Company’s capitalization policy and supporting regulatory guidance. Accordingly, the Commission should reject PIA Staff’s recommendation.
RESPONSE TO PIA STAFF AND INTERVENORS’ RATEMAKING PROPOSALS
The Company’s amortization period should be adopted.
Q.WHAT DO INTERVENORS RECOMMEND REGARDING THE RECOVERY PERIOD FOR THE STORM DAMAGE REGULATORY ASSET?
A.PIA Staff recommends that the Commission: (1) extend the amortization period through December 31, 2034, a 103-month period; and (2) levelize the revenue requirement over this 103-month period. Additionally, PIA Staff suggests that if the storm cost regulatory asset is fully amortized before the effective date of new base rates from the Company’s next base rate case proceeding, the Company should defer excess recoveries to a regulatory liability. GAM witness Pollock also suggests a longer amortization period, such as eight years, or 96 months.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE THAT A LONGER AMORTIZATION PERIOD WOULD BENEFIT CUSTOMERS?
A.The Company disagrees that a longer amortization period is appropriate. In determining the proposed recovery period of four years, the Company carefully considered the balance of a reasonable recovery period of costs already spent by the Company and the impact on affordability for customers. Given that most of the under-recovered storm costs in this filing are related to Hurricane Helene, which occurred in 2024, most of the deferred costs in consideration in this filing will have been incurred for approximately 20 months by the time cost recovery is set to begin when the rate adjustments take effect on June 1, 2026. The Company has absorbed the financing costs needed to support those costs in the interim period and, based on its proposal, the storm costs will be recovered in approximately six years from the date they were incurred. 
In contrast, PIA Staff and GAM’s proposed recovery periods are far too excessive. Based on their proposals, customers would continue paying for most of the storm costs for nearly a decade after they were incurred, even as new storms occur and associated costs are incurred and deferred to the storm reserve. Historically, the Commission has never approved a recovery period longer than six years in prior base rate cases. 
Q.WHAT IS THE RISK OF ADOPTING THESE PROPOSALS TO EXTEND THE AMORTIZATION PERIOD?
A.Spreading recovery over such a long horizon increases the risk that multiple layers of storm-related costs and amortizations will overlap. This would in turn potentially put persistent upward pressure on rates rather than allow rates to normalize once a particular event has been fully recovered. While lengthening the recovery period may reduce the revenue requirement in the short term, extending the recovery over such a prolonged period of time as proposed by PIA Staff and GAM is unreasonable, unprecedented, and will burden rate impacts for customers in the long-term.
PIA Staff’s proposal to levelize the revenue requirement would harm customers.
Q.ARE THERE ANY ISSUES WITH LEVELIZING THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT AS PROPOSED BY PIA STAFF?
A.Yes. PIA Staff’s proposal is onesided because it levelizes stormrelated revenue requirements without also considering the impact of the PTCs beyond the test period. The Company proposed the revenue requirement in this filing based on the test period for both recovery of costs related to storms and the PTC benefits provided to customers. While it is true that the revenue requirement of the deferred storm cost balance will decline beyond the test period as the costs are recovered and the balance decreases over the four-year period, the PTCs included as a reduction to rates do not exist beyond the test period. Nevertheless, the Company opted to provide the full benefit of the PTCs across the four-year period. Therefore, under the Company’s current proposal, customers will continue to receive the benefit of the PTCs in rates over the next four years, or until rates are reset, even though these PTCs will technically be fully amortized by the end of the test period with no remaining PTCs to be credited beyond the test period. PIA Staff’s attempt to levelize one discrete component of the Company’s request—i.e., the revenue requirement related to the deferred storm balance—should also levelize the revenue requirement impact of the absence of PTCs beyond the test period. Treating both components symmetrically would more than offset the impact of a levelized revenue requirement, meaning that PIA Staff’s proposal, when considered appropriately, would result in a higher rate increase for customers compared to the Company’s proposal in this filing.
The Company’s accrual of future storm cost should be adopted
Q.SHOULD THE PROJECTED STORM COSTS REMAIN AT THE CURRENT APPROVED AMOUNT AS RECOMMENDED BY PIA STAFF?
No. The Company has historically updated its projected storm costs based on a historical average of actual storm costs incurred in setting the appropriate storm accrual. In both the 2019 and 2022 base rate cases, the Company used a previous 10-year average of actual storm costs incurred to project future storm costs, which was adopted by PIA Staff and approved by the Commission. Using a historical rolling average of storm costs is the most accurate forecast of projected storm costs considering that any forecast of storm costs for years out in the future is an uncertain exercise at best. Failure to reflect an updated forecast of projected storm costs in the accrual used in setting rates will potentially increase the storm regulatory asset balance in the future to be recovered from customers. 
In addition, PIA Staff mischaracterizes the Company’s storm accrual as forecasted storm costs. The storm accrual includes both of these components—the recovery of previously deferred storm costs as well as the projected storm costs. The current storm accrual approved by the Commission in the 2022 base rate case includes a return of the projected over-recovery at the time of the filing, which reduced the annual storm accrual by $33 million over a three-year period ending December 2025. Therefore, the projected storm costs approved by the Commission in the 2022 base rate case is $65 million and not the current storm accrual of $31 million. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH PIA STAFF’S ALLEGATION THAT THE COMPANY IS SEEKING RECOVERY OF COSTS INCURRED AFTER DECEMBER 31, 2025?
A.No. The Company is not requesting recovery of any storm costs incurred after December 31, 2025. As clearly presented in its exhibits and MFRs, the Company is only seeking recovery of the deferred storm balance of $912 million as of December 31, 2025. In the projected balance of the storm regulatory asset, the Company appropriately included the forecasted change in the asset balance, which includes the projected future storm costs based on the 10-year historical average as well as the declining balance pursuant with the recovery of the storm costs during the test period. This is consistent with the forecasted regulatory asset or reserve balances associated with storm costs filed in every base rate case. The Company must present forecasted balance projections; otherwise, simply limiting the regulatory asset balance to the set date as of December 31, 2025, would not only remove the projected storm costs from the balance but also limit the balance from the projected decline in the deferred storm costs based on its recovery during the test period. Considering both factors together, this would actually result in higher financing costs for customers. The Company’s proposal is more appropriate because it reflects a better forecast of the deferred storm balance going forward.
A separate storm cost recovery rider is unnecessary.
Q.WHAT DOES GAM WITNESS POLLOCK PROPOSE REGARDING A SEPARATE STORM COST RECOVERY RIDER?
A.Witness Pollock suggests that the Commission require the Company to recover storm damage costs through a separate storm cost recovery (“SCR”) rider, rather than recovering these costs through base tariff rates. He argues that the Company would be able to track the remaining storm damage balance more accurately and adjust the rate to recognize projected sales and base revenue growth.
Q.IS A SEPARATE SCR RIDER NECESSARY OR APPROPRIATE?
A.No. Storm costs have always been recovered through traditional base rate tariffs. Therefore, the introduction of a new rider to separately recover storm costs in this filing is unnecessary and adds administrative burden. Furthermore, applying the increase as a rider would have the same effect as increasing each base rate component by the same percentage. Paragraph 3 of the ARP Extension Stipulation already provides for an equal percentage adjustment for storm cost, stating: 
The rate increase shall be applied to each traditional base rate on an equal percentage basis. The energy, demand, and basic service charge components shall all be adjusted equally; provided that the Company shall not apply any increase to the basic service charge for the domestic and small business rate groups. 
Finally, deviating from the Commission-approved approach of recovering costs through traditional base rates would likely infringe on this Commission’s order to have “new rates effective the 1st of the month following 90 days after the request for recovery.” Developing a separate rider tariff and changing the billing system to accommodate the separate rider would necessarily extend the Company’s implementation timeline by several months given the change required in the billing system and the Company’s transition to the new customer information system, thereby delaying the timely recovery of the storm costs. 
Nuclear PTCs should be included as a base rate component.
Q.WHAT DOES GAM WITNESS POLLOCK PROPOSE REGARDING THE USE OF THE COMPANY’S NUCLEAR PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS?
A.Witness Pollock argues that the Company’s nuclear PTCs are unnecessary, assuming that the Commission deploys his proposed alternatives. Therefore, he argues that the Commission should instead use PTCs to offset fuel expense in the Company’s FCR-27 rates.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO THIS PROPOSAL?
A.Income tax credits, including PTCs, have always been a base rate component. The Company does not earn a return on fuel costs and therefore, does not pay income taxes on fuel costs. The purpose of the income tax credits are to offset the Company’s income tax obligation, so Witness Pollock’s proposal to use these credits to offset fuel expenses—expenses that do not incur any income tax obligation—would be inappropriate. Accordingly, the PTCs should rightfully be included as a base rate component and not included in this case.
The Company’s storm threshold does not require modification.
Q.WHAT IS PIA STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE COMPANY’S MAJOR STORM THRESHOLD?
A.The PIA Staff panel believes that the Company’s major storm threshold is “inordinately low” and references thresholds for other utilities as the basis to recommend that the Commission set the major storm declaration threshold to a minimum of $2 million.
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY RESPOND TO THIS RECOMMENDATION?
A.The Company’s major storm threshold is appropriate, has served customers well, and does not need to be modified. The purpose of the major storm threshold is to determine when and whether certain incremental costs are tracked as storm expenses. The Company’s current threshold ensures that most of its incremental storm costs are deferred to the storm reserve rather than expensed as O&M. As explained earlier, O&M expenses are recovered timely from customers as incurred, compared to costs deferred to a regulatory asset, which can be recovered over a longer period thereby dampening rate impacts for customers. Raising the threshold would require the Company to amend its O&M budgets in future base rate cases to include additional projected expenses for incremental storm-related costs. Those increased O&M amounts would be collected from customers, which would increase rate impacts for customers. The Company did not budget for these incremental storm O&M costs in prior base rate cases given the threshold level that was in effect at that time.
In addition, the major storm threshold’s current level benefits customers because it ensures that storm costs are precisely accounted for and that customers only pay for costs that are related to storms that actually occur. Therefore, the threshold the Company uses appropriately balances between the timely recovery of forecasted O&M expenses and the accurate recovery of real incremental storm costs over a longer period to mitigate rate impacts. While PIA Staff may point to other utilities’ thresholds as support for a higher threshold for Georgia Power, this information is particular to those utilities, their specific experience with storms and storm restoration, their recovery mechanism of all incremental storm costs above or below their threshold, and their respective regulatory environments. The storm threshold at issue here is appropriate and does not require modification.

Despite the significant volume of information requested and the short turnaround period, the Company made good-faith efforts to produce all responsive documentation. Following its review, PIA Staff ultimately concluded that the Company’s invoicing was generally satisfactory and identified only a limited number of minor issues. PIA Staff likewise raised no concerns with the contract support provided by the Company, instead offering only processoriented improvement suggestions. While PIA Staff expressed a preference for a different organizational format, the documentation produced contained the substantive information requested and was provided within the compressed schedule established during discovery. 
Q.DID PIA STAFF RECOMMEND ANY DISALLOWANCES RELATED TO THE COMPANY’S INVOICE AND CONTRACT REPORTING?
A.No.
CONCLUSION
Q.Please summarize the company’s request in this filing.
A.The Company has an obligation to serve our customers, which we take seriously and responsibly. The costs requested by the Company for recovery in this case are prudently incurred costs to provide and restore service to our customers as safely and efficiently as possible. Without the extensive efforts of the Company to restore service to customers following Hurricane Helene, many communities across Georgia would have endured sustained periods without electric service. The steps the Company took and the corresponding costs incurred were necessary costs of service and, as such, the Company’s request should be approved. 

As stated in our Direct Testimony, the Company has timed this request to recover storm damage along with its fuel cost adjustment request. The net result of both requests is a reduction of $1.34 a month for the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh, and 1% decrease in overall rates for total retail customers. The outcome of this request will be consistent with the steps already taken by the Commission and the Company to provide customers with the continued benefit from stable electricity rates, all while providing safe and reliable service.

11   A.   There is a fundamental difference between O&M expenses versus costs deferred to
12        the regulatory asset balance, such as storm costs. Under typical utility ratemaking,
13        O&M expenses are recovered immediately from customers as the Company incurs
14        them. In that circumstance, there are no, or very limited, financing costs incurred
15        by the Company since costs are recovered on a timely basis. On the other hand, if
16        costs are no longer expensed as O&M and deferred to a regulatory asset, the
17        Company incurs financing costs on the unrecovered balance. In that respect, for
18        purposes of ratemaking, the deferred costs in a regulatory asset are no different
19        from capitalized costs for a plant asset in terms of the financing costs that are
20        incurred and the need to be recovered from customers.

21        However, a deferral cost mechanism benefits customers from both a timing and an
22        accounting perspective. For timing, compared to an immediate recovery of O&M
23        expenses, the recovery of a deferred regulatory asset can be extended for a much
24        longer period of time, such as the four-year recovery period proposed by the
25        Company in this filing. From an accounting perspective, a deferred regulatory asset
26        ensures that customers only pay for actual costs of the storms incurred by the
27        Company.

A.Utilities nationwide have demonstrated that investments in grid hardening, grid automation, selective undergrounding, and proactive vegetation management are leading practices that deliver reliability benefits to customers every day and resiliency benefits to customers during inclement weather.  In addition to these grid management practices, the development of ERPs, training on the use of those plans, and the disciplined execution of those plans in the time, if any, leading up to an emergency event, during the event, and through the restoration activities following the event is a critical industry best practice.  
Within each of these best practices, there are many more detailed supporting practices and technologies.  Often, there are multiple ways to execute one or more of these supporting practices.  It is frequently the case that a specific supporting practice or technology may be more attractive to a particular company for a specific reason, even though it may deliver little difference in overall performance for customers.
The moral of this best practice story, and the thing that leading performers consistently do, is to keep their eye on the forest, and not get distracted by looking at every tree.  An example of the “don’t lose the forest with your eye on a tree” principle in emergency preparedness is in emergency response organization design.  The critical best practice is to have unified command for the totality of emergency response, with a single person clearly in command.  Georgia Power’s ERP lays out a clear, unified command structure and this is the organization that executed the planning for and recovery from Hurricane Helene.  Anyone raising a concern about the fact that Georgia Power’s unified command structure is not completely parallel to the Incident Command organization favored by the Federal Government is missing the best practice forest and attempting to focus the best practice discussion on a single tree.

And, for major storms, this effort often includes representatives from third-party resources that participated in the restoration, providing yet another valuable perspective.
Another great source are utilities that have been repeatedly subjected to emergencies and demonstrate success in handling those emergencies, whether hurricanes, tornados, winter storms, wildfires, floods, or other natural disasters.
A third excellent source are industry collaboration groups, such as the AEIC.  In fact, in 2025, AEIC’s Storm Team published a Storm Preparation and Response best practices guide, and the guide specifically thanks Georgia Power for its contribution to the guide for its experiences and lessons learned from Helene.

EXTERNAL RESOURCE ACQUISITION PROCESS
Q. HOW DO LEADING UTILITIES PREPARE FOR A MAJOR EVENT SUCH AS A HURRICANE?
A.To prepare for major events, leading utilities take a couple of different paths.  As a part of the routine preparations, leading utilities will periodically review and update their ERPs, carry out exercises and drills to ensure the ERP is updated with prior lessons learned, and ensure there is familiarity with the latest ERP.  Georgia Power does all of these things.  Georgia Power holds annual storm restoration exercises aimed at familiarization with storm restoration strategy as well as providing meaningful challenges for participants to test how they will react.
As the storm system forms, utilities start to track the storm to see if their service territory will be impacted.  If there is a chance of impact, utilities will start to develop a picture of the anticipated damage and resources required to recover from the anticipated damage.  This is followed by outreach to various organizations (contractors, mutual assistance organizations, etc.) to coordinate resource requests with other utilities that are also anticipating damage from the same weather system.  All of these tasks and activities are part of Georgia Power’s ERP and were executed during the lead up to Hurricane Helene.
Q. HOW DO UTILITIES DETERMINE HOW MANY RESOURCES ARE NEEDED?
A.The question of how many resources are needed to recover is one of the more impactful decisions the utilities need to make in the days before a storm’s impact.  At the heart of the restoration challenge is twofold – 1) identify the anticipated damage from the expected storm track, and 2) determine the resources required to restore from the anticipated damage in a timely fashion.  
Rarely does anticipated damage prior to storm impact match exactly with the amount of damage observed after the storm ends.  In addition, local field conditions, such as the amount of tree damage, are highly variable and differ from storm to storm, leading to a degree of uncertainty around the total amount of work required to recover.  As recognized in the AEIC best practices paper, one of the ways to determine how many resources are needed is based on historical information—such as track of storm, amount of damage, or how much time and effort were previously needed to address the particulars of the utility’s electrical system—and must account for local work practices.  There is no industry standard for translating a set of standard inputs in terms of storm forecasts into accurate counts and mixes of resources.  The best source of how many resources are needed still comes from historical data obtained from dealing with local conditions and storms.  There are certainly additional considerations beyond the local damage that would drive the overall restoration resources and timing, but historical information remains the best source.
More resources can lead to faster restorations, but at a higher cost.  Utilizing fewer resources will lead to lower restoration costs, but at the expense of worsening customer experiences.  The balance between number of resources and overall expected restoration times is a judgment call of the responding utility and can be the most challenging aspect of any storm planning effort. 
Once the estimated damage (often in ranges) and required resources are determined, utilities can determine the number of available internal resources (crews and contractors), then derive the resource requests for the number of outside resources that will be needed to support the restoration effort.
Q. ARE UTILITIES ALWAYS SUCCESSFUL IN OBTAINING THE REQUESTED OUTSIDE RESOURCES TO SUPPORT RESTORATION EFFORTS?
A.No.  Storm tracks are rarely fixed in place.  As can be expected, large storms such as Hurricane Helene will likely impact multiple utilities.  All of the utilities that are anticipating damage from the storm will be making independent, concurrent determinations as to how many resources they will need to recover from the storm.  This leads to a pool of demand for resources from multiple utilities prior to the storm impacting. 
This demand is often greater than available resources, so there is often an allocation process run by the regional mutual assistance organization to determine how the available mutual assistance resources should be shared amongst all the utilities expecting to be impacted by a storm.

This assertion, from a PIA Staff panel without any notable emergency response and storm management expertise or experience and without having done a prudency or operations review of any of the Georgia Power storm responses, is, at best, an “ivory tower” assertion.

Q.DO YOU HAVE COMMENTS ON SPECIFIC ASSUMPTIONS THE PIA STAFF PANEL USED TO CALCULATE THEIR RECOMMENDED DISALLOWANCES?
A.Yes.  I would like to address several specific points.  
First, the PIA Staff panel claims a disallowance should be made to exclude from storm costs 20% of all Georgia Power employee overtime labor costs, as they assert these are non-incremental costs.  The totality of these costs are typically treated as storm costs across the industry.  As many, if not all, employees typically work sixteen-hour days during major storm responses, this cost dwarfs the “business as normal” overtime level, both in terms of overtime hours per employee and the number of employees working overtime.  The standard practice in the industry is to charge all employee overtime during the storm period to storm restoration.  I believe it is reasonable and appropriate to treat all employee overtime during a storm period as a storm cost.
Second, the PIA Staff panel claim a disallowance should be made to exclude from storm costs 20% of all external contractors that are under contract for routine work on the Georgia Power system, as they assert these are non-incremental costs.  Storm restoration work done by these “native” contractors is 100% incremental to their ongoing baseline work in support of the Company’s annual work plan.  Their baseline work will still be completed and paid for under the agreed contractual terms, although the completion schedule for that work is obviously impacted by the storm.  To suggest that re-assigning a contract crew to storm work for the restoration period is not incremental to that commercial relationship represents a fundamental misunderstanding of how external resources are managed in the industry.  I believe it is reasonable to consider all “on property” contract resources that are redirected to storm restoration duty as 100% incremental costs for storm accounting purposes.
Third, the PIA Staff panel’s recommendation to stretch out cost recovery of the incurred storm costs to 103 months (over 8 ½ years) is bad policy in my experience.  A primary purpose of the storm cost accrual is to align customer benefit from storm restoration with the customer cost for same.  The Company’s proposal to update the annual expense going forward is made with an eye to better match likely costs incurred with revenues for same.  Recovering restoration costs for Hurricane Helene in 2024 from Georgia Power customers through 2034 is, in my opinion, not wise.
Fourth, the PIA Staff panel recommends the Company be ordered to perform post-storm studies and reviews.  Because costs associated with these studies and reviews are not in “business as normal” costs, if that were to be ordered, those costs should be accrued to the storm cost regulatory asset.

 2   Q.   IN GENERAL, WHAT DOES THE TERM “BEST PRACTICES” REFER
 3        TO?

 4   A.   “Best Practices,” or “Best Demonstrated Practices,” refer to the application of
 5        policies, procedures, work processes, and technology solutions that participants in
 6        an industry have successfully applied to achieve improved performance.

 7   Q.   FOR THE MOST RELIABLE AND RESILIENT ELECTRIC UTILITIES,
 8        WHAT ARE THE PRACTICES THAT DRIVE TRANSMISSION AND
 9        DISTRIBUTION RELIABILITY AND STORM RESILIENCY?

10   A.   Utilities nationwide have demonstrated that investments in grid hardening, grid
11        automation, selective undergrounding, and proactive vegetation management are
12        leading practices that deliver reliability benefits to customers every day and
13        resiliency benefits to customers during inclement weather. In addition to these grid
14        management practices, the development of ERPs, training on the use of those plans,
15        and the disciplined execution of those plans in the time, if any, leading up to an
16        emergency event, during the event, and through the restoration activities following
17        the event is a critical industry best practice.

18        Within each of these best practices, there are many more detailed supporting
19        practices and technologies. Often, there are multiple ways to execute one or more
20        of these supporting practices. It is frequently the case that a specific supporting
21        practice or technology may be more attractive to a particular company for a specific
22        reason, even though it may deliver little difference in overall performance for
23        customers.

24        The moral of this best practice story, and the thing that leading performers
25        consistently do, is to keep their eye on the forest, and not get distracted by looking
26        at every tree. An example of the “don’t lose the forest with your eye on a tree”
27        principle in emergency preparedness is in emergency response organization design.

 3   Q.   HOW DO LEADING UTILITIES PREPARE FOR A MAJOR EVENT SUCH
 4        AS A HURRICANE?

 5   A.   To prepare for major events, leading utilities take a couple of different paths. As a
 6        part of the routine preparations, leading utilities will periodically review and update
 7        their ERPs, carry out exercises and drills to ensure the ERP is updated with prior
 8        lessons learned, and ensure there is familiarity with the latest ERP. Georgia Power
 9        does all of these things. Georgia Power holds annual storm restoration exercises
10        aimed at familiarization with storm restoration strategy as well as providing
11        meaningful challenges for participants to test how they will react.

12        As the storm system forms, utilities start to track the storm to see if their service
13        territory will be impacted. If there is a chance of impact, utilities will start to
14        develop a picture of the anticipated damage and resources required to recover from
15        the anticipated damage. This is followed by outreach to various organizations
16        (contractors, mutual assistance organizations, etc.) to coordinate resource requests
17        with other utilities that are also anticipating damage from the same weather system.
18        All of these tasks and activities are part of Georgia Power’s ERP and were executed
19        during the lead up to Hurricane Helene.

20   Q.   HOW DO UTILITIES DETERMINE HOW MANY RESOURCES ARE
21        NEEDED?

22   A.   The question of how many resources are needed to recover is one of the more
23        impactful decisions the utilities need to make in the days before a storm’s impact.
24        At the heart of the restoration challenge is twofold – 1) identify the anticipated


                               Rebuttal Testimony of Derek HasBrouck
                                On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                          Docket No. 44280
                                            Page 20 of 28

 1   damage from the expected storm track, and 2) determine the resources required to
 2   restore from the anticipated damage in a timely fashion.

 3   Rarely does anticipated damage prior to storm impact match exactly with the
 4   amount of damage observed after the storm ends. In addition, local field conditions,
 5   such as the amount of tree damage, are highly variable and differ from storm to
 6   storm, leading to a degree of uncertainty around the total amount of work required
 7   to recover. As recognized in the AEIC best practices paper, one of the ways to
 8   determine how many resources are needed is based on historical information—such
 9   as track of storm, amount of damage, or how much time and effort were previously
10   needed to address the particulars of the utility’s electrical system—and must
11   account for local work practices. There is no industry standard for translating a set
12   of standard inputs in terms of storm forecasts into accurate counts and mixes of
13   resources. The best source of how many resources are needed still comes from
14   historical data obtained from dealing with local conditions and storms. There are
15   certainly additional considerations beyond the local damage that would drive the
16   overall restoration resources and timing, but historical information remains the best
17   source.

18   More resources can lead to faster restorations, but at a higher cost. Utilizing fewer
19   resources will lead to lower restoration costs, but at the expense of worsening
20   customer experiences. The balance between number of resources and overall
21   expected restoration times is a judgment call of the responding utility and can be
22   the most challenging aspect of any storm planning effort.

23   Once the estimated damage (often in ranges) and required resources are determined,
24   utilities can determine the number of available internal resources (crews and
25   contractors), then derive the resource requests for the number of outside resources
26   that will be needed to support the restoration effort.

 1   Q.   ARE UTILITIES ALWAYS SUCCESSFUL IN OBTAINING THE
 2        REQUESTED OUTSIDE RESOURCES TO SUPPORT RESTORATION
 3        EFFORTS?

 4   A.   No. Storm tracks are rarely fixed in place. As can be expected, large storms such
 5        as Hurricane Helene will likely impact multiple utilities. All of the utilities that are
 6        anticipating damage from the storm will be making independent, concurrent
 7        determinations as to how many resources they will need to recover from the storm.
 8        This leads to a pool of demand for resources from multiple utilities prior to the
 9        storm impacting.

10        This demand is often greater than available resources, so there is often an allocation
11        process run by the regional mutual assistance organization to determine how the
12        available mutual assistance resources should be shared amongst all the utilities
13        expecting to be impacted by a storm.

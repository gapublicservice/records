The Company’s Planning For And Management Of Its Response To These Storms Failed To Employ Industry Best Practices
The Company’s planning and management of its response to these storms failed to employ industry best practices and planning and resource management technology tools to determine the optimal quantities and mix of resources acquired, manage the resources acquired to restore service in a reasonable timeframe against reasonable performance metrics, and minimize the costs of those resources. The Company’s failures in these areas likely led to excessive costs due to unnecessary and inefficient resourcing, although Staff was not able to quantify the excessive costs. The Staff did not perform a comprehensive prudence or operations review, but reviewed the Company’s emergency response plan, planning and resource management systems and technology that were in place when these storms occurred, the Company’s limited critique of its performance in response to Hurricane Helene, changes that have been implemented and changes that are in progress in these areas since Hurricane Helene, among other information.
We recommend the Commission direct the Company to study industry practices to identify the best of those industry practices, integrate those best practices into its planning, resource acquisition, and resource management, and adopt and use modern information technology and modeling tools, optimize the quantities and mix of resources, and manage those resources to reasonably balance the resources and time required to restore service with the costs incurred to restore service. We recommend the Commission direct the Company to complete this initial study, develop an action plant to implement these industry best practices, begin implementing those best practices by the end of 2026, and adopt and begin implementing modern information technology and modeling tools by the end of 2026. We recommend the Commission direct the Company to file quarterly detailed status reports with the Commission describing its progress on these activities starting in September 2026.
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to critique its performance after each major storm to identify areas for improvement and then apply the lessons learned to continuously improve its restoration and cost performance in its responses to future storms. We recommend the Commission direct the Company to file these critiques, action plans, and detailed status reports with the Commission starting three months after each storm and continuing each quarter thereafter for at least one year after each storm.
In addition, we recommend the Commission consider these facts in conjunction with our other recommendations, including the amounts that Staff had to estimate because of the Company’s inability and/or unwillingness to quantify and exclude non-incremental costs already recovered through base revenues and the amounts that Staff had to estimate because of the Company’s flawed accounting and failure to correctly calculate the costs capitalized and recorded to plant in service instead of to the storm cost regulatory asset.
The Company Overstated The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset Because It Failed To Exclude Costs Already Recovered Through Base Revenues
The Company’s accounting for storm costs is based on incorrect assumptions that do not accurately differentiate between incremental costs and non-incremental costs. The accounting for storm costs is important and relevant because it ultimately affects how these costs are recovered from ratepayers. The Company’s accounting incorrectly assumes that all overtime labor costs and all external contractor costs are incremental, despite the fact that some of these internal and external resources were redeployed from their activities under “business as normal” (also referred to as “blue sky”) conditions and the fact those costs already were recovered in base revenues collected from ratepayers. The costs of the redeployed resources under “business as normal” conditions were and are not incremental, were and are already being recovered in base revenues, and should not have been and should not be deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset for recovery a second time. In addition, due to limitations in its accounting and reporting systems, the Company claims that it cannot readily identify and quantify the cost of the internal and external resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” activities to storm response activities necessary to avoid recovering these costs twice through the ratemaking process.
We recommend the Commission disallow the overtime labor and all outside services contractor costs for resources redeployed from “business as normal” activities that were not incremental and already were recovered through base revenues.
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to improve its accounting and reporting systems so that it can accurately differentiate and quantify the incremental and non-incremental costs and exclude the costs for redeployed resources that are already recovered through base revenues, including overtime labor and external contractor costs, from the costs deferred for future storms.
The Company Overstated The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset Because It Failed To Remove All Capital Costs That Instead Should Have Been Recorded to Plant In Service
The Company overstated the storm cost regulatory asset because it did not remove all capital costs and record those costs to plant in service. Initially, the Company records all storm costs to the storm cost regulatory asset, and then credits (reduces) the regulatory asset to remove costs that should be capitalized to plant in service. The Company uses a formulaic standard cost methodology to quantify the costs capitalized to plant in service. The Company’s methodology is flawed and failed to correctly remove all relevant capital costs, including external line crew, vegetation management, and staging contractor costs. This fundamental flaw significantly overstated the amounts remaining in the storm cost regulatory asset and significantly understated plant in service. In addition, the Company made other errors in the calculation of the capital cost credit that further overstated the storm cost regulatory asset and understated plant in service. 
We recommend the Commission include additional capital costs in the capital cost credit, reduce the storm cost regulatory asset, and increase plant in service. The increase in the capital cost credit does not result in a disallowance of storm costs; rather, it correctly excludes the costs from the storm cost regulatory asset and instead includes the costs in plant in service. The costs correctly included in plant in service will be recovered consistent with the manner in which it recovers all other capital costs included in plant in service. The Company may fully recover these costs regardless of whether they are recorded in a storm cost regulatory asset or plant in service, provided they were reasonable and prudent.
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to modify the formulaic standard cost methodology it presently uses to remove all capital costs from the storm cost regulatory asset and record the additional capital costs in plant in service.
The Company’s Documentation, Review, and Approval Process Was Generally Adequate; However, Improvements Are Necessary
Staff performed an audit using stratified statistical sampling of invoice costs and related contracts for each of the storms. The audit was focused primarily on invoices submitted by external line and vegetation management contractors, external mutual assistance company contractors, staging site contractors, and other contractors, as these contractor costs made up the vast majority of the storm costs incurred. The sample size of invoices for Hurricane Helene was larger than that for the other storms due to magnitude of the costs incurred to respond to that storm.  
With certain exceptions that we subsequently describe, the invoice costs associated with external contractors were incurred to repair damages and restore service for storm events and properly deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset, before reductions for capital cost credits, the line item cost rates matched contract pricing when applicable, the total invoice costs matched the amounts recorded to the general ledger, and there were no duplications of invoice costs included in the storm cost regulatory asset and various storm cost summaries. We also concluded that the sample invoice support requested by Staff in discovery was complete and well organized for review. We recommend no disallowance of costs related to our audit of the external contractor invoices. 
In addition, we reviewed the contract provisions and rates for each vendor. We found there were numerous unorganized files for multiple vendors provided in a single folder. In many cases, the Company failed to provide copies of the vendor contracts. We recommend the Company improve the access and organization of these vendor files to ensure all costs are properly verified on a timely basis prior to approving invoices for payment. 
The Commission Should Deny The Company’s Proposal to Increase The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset Included In Rate Base For Estimated Storm Costs After December 31, 2025
The Company improperly increased the storm cost regulatory asset to reflect estimated increases in storm costs after December 31, 2025. The Company’s request violates the Stipulation to Extend the Alternate Rate Plan approved by the Commission in Docket 44280 approved by the Commission in its Order dated July 31, 2025 (Stipulation) wherein the recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset in this proceeding was expressly limited to the costs incurred through December 31, 2025. The Company should only be allowed to recover actual costs incurred rather than estimates of costs that have not yet been incurred.
In addition, the Company’s methodology used to calculate the return on the storm cost regulatory asset in this proceeding improperly commingles the regulatory asset at December 31, 2025, the amortization and recovery of the regulatory asset at December 31, 2025, the estimated storm costs after December 31, 2025, the requested storm expense accruals for future storms, and the calculation of the requested return on rate base. 
We recommend the Commission deny the Company’s request to recover a return on estimated storm costs incurred after December 31, 2025. We recommend the Commission limit the return on the storm cost regulatory asset to the costs incurred through December 31, 2025, consistent with the Stipulation approved by the Commission last year. 
The Commission Should Deny The Company’s Request To Recover A Return On The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset At Its Grossed-Up Weighted Average Cost of Capital 
The Company requests a return on the storm cost regulatory asset at its grossed-up WACC. One alternative to the Company’s request is to deny any return on the storm cost regulatory asset. That would maintain the status quo. The Company presently earns neither a current return nor a deferred return on the storm cost regulatory asset. Another alternative is to authorize a debt only rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset.
We recommend the Commission authorize a debt only rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset. This is an approximate midpoint between the status quo of no return on the storm cost regulatory asset and the Company’s request for a grossed-up rate of return. We note the Company has the option of actually financing the asset with debt only, such as term loans, which is lower cost and could be structured to match the limited amortization and recovery period for the storm cost regulatory asset. In addition, the debt only rate of return reflects the lower financial and regulatory risk from recovery of a defined financial asset and amount compared to physical assets and operating expenses. Further, the debt only rate of return redresses, albeit indirectly, the Company’s overrecovery of the storm cost regulatory asset due to growth in sales and other billing determinants between base rate proceedings. 
We also recommend that the return on be applied only to the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025 less accumulated amortization and related ADIT as the regulatory asset is amortized and recovered from customers. 
The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset Recovery Period Should Be Extended And The Revenue Recovery Should Be Levelized 
The Company’s calculation of the proposed base revenue increase and the proposed recovery will result in excessive recovery of the storm costs. The Company’s requested recovery is based on the revenue requirement calculated for a forecast test period from June 1, 2026 through May 31, 2027, representing only the first 12 months of the Company’s proposed amortization and recovery period of 48 months. The Company’s request fails to reflect the decline in the storm cost revenue requirement as the regulatory asset is amortized and the return on the regulatory asset continues to decline each month after May 31, 2027. In addition, the Company’s proposed amortization and recovery period is unnecessarily and unduly short. Further, the Company’s proposed amortization and recovery period is not aligned with the Commission’s recent history of setting the Company’s base revenues every three years pursuant to a sequence of alternative rate plans.
We recommend the recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset be calculated on a levelized (annuitized) basis, similar to the calculation of payments for a home mortgage loan, using an amortization and recovery period of 103 months timed to coincide with the Company’s historic pattern of base rate proceedings every three years. The recovery will remain the same for each of the 103 months until the storm cost regulatory asset is fully amortized and recovered. The recovery should remain a separate component within the base revenue requirement in future base rate proceedings to facilitate review by Staff and other parties.
The Company’s Definition of A Storm Event Reflects An Inordinately Low Threshold of $50,000
The Company’s definition of a storm event reflects a dollar threshold of $50,000, which results in the deferral of costs to respond to minor and unnamed storms that are both frequent and recurring. These costs are more appropriately considered as routine operating expenses to streamline the response to and accounting for these events, in contrast to less frequent and more severe storms with greater physical, customer, and cost impacts. The $50,000 threshold is extremely low.  A more appropriate dollar threshold would be $2 million. 
We recommend the Commission modify the definition of a storm event to reflect a minimum dollar threshold of $2 million per storm event before the Company is allowed to defer the costs over that threshold to the storm reserve/storm cost regulatory asset.
The Commission Should Deny the Company’s Request to Increase The Storm Expense Accrual For Future Storms By $69.835 Million In This Proceeding And Address Changes In The Storm Expense Accrual for Future Storms In Future Comprehensive Base Rate Proceedings
The Company seeks to expand the limited scope of this proceeding approved by the Commission last year to increase the storm expense accrual for future storm costs and the base revenue recovery from the $31.419 million presently recovered in base revenues to $101.254 million effective June 1, 2026, an increase of $69.835 million annually, or an increase in base revenue recoveries from customers of $180.407 million from June 1, 2026 through December 31, 2028 when the present three year alternative rate plan expires.
We recommend the Commission deny the request to increase the storm expense accrual for future storm costs and the base revenue recovery in this proceeding. This request is outside the limited scope of this proceeding and is properly addressed in the context of a future comprehensive base rate proceeding wherein all revenues and all costs are considered on a comprehensive basis, rather than seeking to increase the recovery of a single expense on a selective basis.
II. The Company’s Planning for and Management of Its Response to These Storms failed to employ industry best practices
Q.Describe briefly the Company’s planning for and response to severe weather events to repair damage to its system and restore service.
A.The Company’s planning for and response to severe weather events is described in its Storm Response Plan, its responses to Staff discovery, and in the Direct Testimony of Company witnesses Cleve Fann, Josh Stallings, and Matthew Berrigan. 
The process starts with the identification of a potential storm event based on meteorological data. The process continues with the identification of the potential location(s) and timing of the geographic areas that will be affected, assessment of potential impacts, continuous monitoring, acquisition and positioning of resources (line crews, vegetation management crews, damage assessors, flaggers, stating, etc.) in the geographic areas that likely will be impacted, post-storm assessment of actual damages, clearance of vegetation and other debris, repair of the system and restoration of service to customers, demobilization of external contractor resources, and return of internal resources and external resources temporarily redeployed to storm activities back to their “business as normal” activities.
Q.Describe how the Company determines the resources necessary to respond to a storm event.
A.The Company provided the following description in response to Staff discovery.
For pre-storm planning, the Company uses meteorological data from both contract meteorologists and the National Weather Service (NWS). The information is then compared to previous storms and historical data is used from the Distribution Resource Management tracking application to determine resource needs. For post-storm response, the Company uses intelligence from field and regional resources including events, customer outages, and the causes of outages to determine the necessary resources.
Q.Describe the information and other technology tools used by the Company in this process.
A.In response to Staff discovery, the Company stated: 
The Company currently does not have storm modeling tools. However, as described and referred to in later data request responses, the Company is in the process of developing and evaluating multiple tools to aid in storm preparation, resource allocation, and mobilization. 
These tools will utilize the following inputs: 
Historical outage data
Historical forecast data 
Geospatial data 
Customer location and density 
Asset location data 
Historical vegetation management data 
Crew resource data
The outputs from these tools will include: 
Predicted incident counts 
Predicted customer impacts 
Hourly timing predictions 
Geospatial impact predictions (with varying resolutions down to workgroup level) 
Forecasted weather metrics

The Company stated in response: 
The Company currently does not have a probability distribution or matrix developed. However, the Company is in the process of developing and evaluating multiple tools to aid in storm preparation.
Q.Did the Company utilize a decision matrix or probability distribution to measure and/or compare potential resources against outage times in order to make its resource and mobilization decisions?
A.No. Staff asked the Company in discovery if it utilized a probability distribution or matrix to measure and/or compare potential resources against outage times and, if so, to describe how the Company factors this distribution or matrix into its resourcing and mobilization decisions, including, but not limited to, the various storm response logistics modeling tools. The Company stated in response: 
The Company currently does not have a probability distribution or matrix for this issue. However, it does have an Estimated Restoration Time (ERT) measure, which will be incorporated into the tools it is developing and evaluating to aid in storm preparation, resource allocation, and mobilization. Q.Did the Company utilize target metrics or quantitative goals for outage restoration times that were factored directly or indirectly into the number and type of resources acquired and/or the various storm response logistics modeling tools?
A.No.  Staff asked the Company in discovery if it had one or more target metrics or quantitative goals for outage restoration times that was factored directly or indirectly into the number and type of resources acquired and/or the various storm response logistics modeling tools and, if so, to identify and describe each such target metric or quantitative goal and describe the manner in which it is used to determine the number and type of resources acquired, including, but not limited to, the various storm response logistics modeling tools. The Company stated in response:
The Company does not have specific metrics or quantitative goals for outage restoration times that factor into the number and type of resources needed for initial preparations of the storm response. Once the initial storm event has occurred and damage for various areas has been assessed by the Company, it assigns an outage event level from 1 through 4, which can be used as an input for resourcing decisions.  The outage event level also helps determine an Estimated Restoration Time (ERT) to track restoration times for customers.
The Staff also requested a listing and description of each goal or measure used by the Company to assess the effectiveness of its storm planning and response process and to provide documentation of these goals and measures and how they were used during and after Hurricane Helene, including in the various storm response logistics modeling tools. In response, the Company stated: 
There are no specific metrics or documented goals for Hurricane Helene restoration efforts; however, the primary aim during any storm event is to safely and efficiently restore electric service to all affected customers as quickly as possible.
Q.Does the historic lack of decision tools, including technology tools, to assist in planning and executing the response to the storms included in the storm cost regulatory asset support the Company’s claim that it utilized “industry best practices” or demonstrate a focus on optimizing resourcing and minimizing costs in response to those storms?
A.No. The Company failed to use industry best practices to plan or execute its responses to the storms included in the storm cost regulatory asset. This led to inefficiencies and likely excessive costs. The Company relied on its historic practices, but such practices have no inherent benefit other than as guides to rough estimates of resourcing based on prior resourcing decisions for other storms. Of course, those prior storms were resourced in the same manner and without the benefit of modern decision tools, including technology tools, essentially a circular process that embedded and perpetuated historic rule of thumb practices and the inherent inefficiencies in those practices. 
The Company also lacks an overall approach to storm response that incorporates quantitative performance objectives and metrics and allows it to assess the tradeoffs among the number and mix of resources available compared to outage restoration times and costs in order to achieve reasonable restoration times at the least reasonable cost. This led to inefficiencies and likely excessive costs. An overall approach that incorporates various performance objectives and metrics allows the utility to make decisions based on data and to test the outcomes using technology tools.
Generally, the more resources that are acquired, the shorter the time, on average, required to restore service, but the greater the cost to do so. The use of decision matrices and technology tools would allow the Company to more efficiently deploy resources so that restoration times are reduced and less cost is incurred.  
Other utilities are much further developed in their planning and execution and have implemented technology tools that allow them to more precisely forecast potential damages based on system asset inventories, vegetation coverage, other potential causes of damage, and that allow them to acquire the resources correlated to the expected potential damages, and to manage the resources and transitions from higher to lower priority locations, to timely demobilize resources as damage is repaired and service is restored, and to minimize the costs to respond to the storm or other emergency event.
The Company has been slow to identify and implement best practices. After Hurricane Helene, the Company recognized that improvements in planning and execution were necessary to optimize resources and minimize costs, including the use of technology tools in this process. The Company’s failure to implement industry best practices and to integrate relevant technology tools to assess and set performance objectives and outage metrics necessarily led to excess resourcing, inefficiencies, and excessive costs imposed on customers, although the excessive costs cannot be directly quantified in retrospect.
Q.Since Hurricane Helene has the Company acquired or developed new technology tools to improve its performance?
A.Yes. The Company’s response to Staff discovery previously cited included its assessment of the functionality and the use of new technology tools. The Company’s response to additional Staff discovery identified new technology tools it has implemented and described the functionality of those tools as follows:
The Company implemented a storm modeling tool, Storm Prediction, Estimated Restoration Times and Reporting (“SPEAR”), in July 2025. This storm analytics tool delivers:

Predicted incident counts;
Forecasted customer impacts;
Hourly timing estimates;
Geospatial impact forecasts, both at the state and region levels; and
Weather metric predictions.
As part of the SPEAR application, the Company also recently implemented a manpower tracking tool called GridCritical, which will be used to efficiently onboard, track, and accommodate resources assigned to restorations efforts.  This information will feed directly into the SPEAR application to assist in customer restoration times.

In addition to SPEAR, the Company is currently testing two additional modeling tools (StormImpact Prediction Models and WeatherSentry) to use for benchmarking purposes.

StormImpact: Provides proprietary weather and hurricane models that are customized with Georgia Power’s historical storm effects to predict infrastructure damage and outages for any scale event.  
WeatherSentry: Robust platform that provides insights from proprietary weather prediction models on storm impacts using historical data to show damage, outages, and flooding. Among other capabilities, this system also provides data on traffic and evacuation routes to route restoration crews if necessary. 
Q.Did the Company critique its performance after each of the storms to determine its strengths and successes as well as its weaknesses and areas to develop and apply lessons learned for improved performance in response to future storms?
A.No. The Company did not perform any documented critique of its performance after any storm, except for a limited critique after Hurricane Helene. In response to Staff discovery, the Company described this critique as follows.
Following the Company’s response to Hurricane Helene, a cross functional team was brought together to review the Company’s storm response process. As a result of this qualitative review, the Company identified eight areas for assessment and, where appropriate, further action. The Company is currently addressing these areas and anticipates completion later in 2025. 

Develop a base camp playbook for leaders to utilize to successfully run a base camp.
Update the Significant Event Level Estimated Restoration Time Management document to better accommodate needs for restoration events with a 1 to 2 week duration. 
Determine a process for assigning storm roles to every new and existing employee at the Company and providing necessary training for the newly assigned roles. 
Develop a process for hosting an annual Company-wide storm drill and include a scenario of a severe hurricane impacting the whole state, including Metro Atlanta, in the first drill.
Standardize the Company’s approach to local (county/city) communication during major storm events.
Evaluate and update the safety onboarding process for off-system personnel.
Develop a standardized process for damage assessment data collection and delivery. 
Refresh storm preparedness checklists included in all region Storm Emergency Response Plans.
Q.Is that limited effort sufficient to identify areas of improvement through lessons learned and to apply those lessons to improve performance, including reduced outage times at lower costs?
A.No. None of the eight areas reviewed addressed performance metrics, quantities and mix of resources, effective management of the resources acquired, reductions in outage times, or reductions in costs, all of which are at the very core of the Company’s storm response and the costs incurred.  Such self-critiques are essential to achieving improvements in the areas that matter the most to customers.
In addition, the lack of any specific performance metrics or documented goals for restoration efforts limits the opportunities for the Company and its leadership to assess performance and intentionally and continuously improve performance using those metrics.

We recommend the Commission direct the Company to complete this initial study, develop an action plant to implement these industry best practices, begin implementing those best practices by the end of 2026, and adopt and begin implementing modern information technology and modeling tools by the end of 2026. We recommend the Commission direct the Company to file quarterly detailed status reports with the Commission describing its progress on these activities starting in September 2026.
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to critique its performance after each major storm to identify areas for improvement and then apply the lessons learned to continuously improve its restoration and cost performance in response to future storms. We recommend the Commission direct the Company to file these critiques, action plans, and detailed status reports with the Commission starting three months after each storm and continuing each quarter thereafter for at least one year after each storm.
In addition, we recommend the Commission consider these facts in conjunction with our other recommendations, including the amounts that Staff had to estimate because of the Company’s inability and/or unwillingness to quantify and exclude non-incremental costs already recovered through base revenues and the amounts that Staff had to estimate because of the Company’s flawed accounting and failure to correctly calculate the costs capitalized and recorded to plant in service instead of to the storm cost regulatory asset. 
III. The COMPANY OVERSTATED THE STORM COST REGULATORY ASSET BECAUSE IT FAILED TO EXCLUDE COSTS ALREADY RECOVERED THROUGH BASE REVENUES
Q.Describe briefly the Company’s accounting for storm costs.
A.The Company describes its accounting for storm costs in “Accounting for Storm Damage & Other Emergencies,” which it provided in response to Staff discovery. These accounting guidelines apply to “major storms” and other emergency situations, including localized storms not meeting the requirements to be classified as a major storm, among others. 
The costs for each storm or emergency situation are charged to a unique project activity code. The costs charged to the project activity code are debited to the “storm reserve” account, which if it is an asset balance, is referred to as a storm cost regulatory asset. There may be multiple storm cost regulatory assets in the future depending on the form of recovery adopted in this proceeding. At this time, the Company simply aggregates all storm cost regulatory assets. However, other utilities segregate their regulatory assets based on the form of ratemaking recovery and the timing of that recovery. We address this latter issue in the ratemaking section of our testimony.
The Company’s accounting guidelines specify which costs are to be charged to the project activity code. For example, all straight-time labor and related costs are considered “base charges,” meaning these costs are not incremental and already are recovered in present base revenues. These costs are not charged to the project activity code and are not included in the storm cost regulatory asset. All overtime labor and related costs and all contractor costs are considered “reserve charges,” meaning these costs are considered by the Company to be incremental. These costs are charged to the project activity code and are included in the storm cost regulatory asset.
The Company’s accounting guidelines also require the transfer of capital costs from the storm cost regulatory asset to plant in service accounts. The Company describes its accounting for the capital costs in other accounting guidelines provided in response to Staff discovery.
Q.What are the threshold requirements for deferral of storm costs to a storm cost regulatory asset and recovery of those costs?
A.First, the costs must be incremental, meaning they are costs that would not have been incurred but for the storm and are not already recovered through present base revenues. Second, the costs deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset should exclude all costs that are capital in nature and properly recorded in the plant in service accounts. The capital costs are incurred for the replacement of physical assets that have physical service lives and should be recovered from customers over their service lives in the same manner the costs of all other physical assets are recovered.
Q.Does the Company agree conceptually with these threshold requirements?
A.Yes. The Company agrees that only incremental costs should be deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset. It also agrees that capital costs should be recorded to the plant in service accounts and excluded from the storm cost regulatory asset, which it records through the capital cost credit. We address the capital cost credit in more detail in the following section of our testimony.
Q.Did the Company actually restrict the costs included in the storm cost regulatory asset to only incremental costs? 
A.No. The Company improperly included costs that were not incremental and that already were recovered through present base revenues (rates). More specifically, the Company deferred all overtime labor costs for the internal resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” activities to storm activities and charged to the storm activity code for accounting purposes. The deferral of these costs to the storm cost regulatory asset is based on the assumption the entirety of these costs was incremental. That assumption is incorrect. The Company already recovers overtime labor costs for these redeployed resources through base revenues for “business as normal” activities. It does not incur those costs a second time for resources that were or are redeployed from those “business as normal” activities to storm response activities. 
Similarly, the Company deferred all external contractor costs for external resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” activities to storm activities and charged to the storm activity code for accounting purposes. The deferral of these costs to the storm cost regulatory asset is based on the assumption that the entirety of these costs was incremental. That assumption is incorrect. The Company already recovers external contractor costs for these redeployed resources through base revenues for “business as normal.” It does not incur those costs a second time for resources that were or are redeployed from those “business as normal” activities to storm response activities. 
Q.Did Staff request the Company provide the labor and external contractor costs already included in and recovered through base revenues under “business as normal” activities for internal and external resources that are redeployed to storm response activities? 
A.Yes. The Company refused to provide this information in response to multiple Staff discovery requests or in the Minimum Filing Requirements (MFR) schedules despite Staff comments requesting that this information be provided for the costs of each storm included in the storm cost regulatory asset. 
In response to one of the Staff discovery requests, the Company asserted that it “does not maintain a breakdown of contract labor and other outside services” into “electric line contractors and equipment,” “vegetation management contractors and equipment,” and “other contractors, e.g., flaggers, hydraulic pumping, etc., and equipment.” In response to Staff comments regarding the Company’s draft MFR schedules, the Company stated “During a storm, all outside services are charged to the respective storm number using a single resource type. The Company's reporting system does not track costs by vendor type.”
Q.Regardless of the Company’s refusal to provide the information requested by Staff, is this information available from the Company’s records?
A.Yes. The Company maintains accounting records that include the information necessary to quantify the information requested by the Staff necessary to correctly calculate the incremental and non-incremental costs. These records include the Company’s Contractor Application for Time and Expense Entry (CATEE) and Job Estimating and Tracking System (JETS) reporting systems and the invoices from each vendor for the specific external contractor resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” or activities to storm response activities, and the Company’s scheduling records. 
Since Hurricane Helene, the Company has developed and/or implemented other technology tools that could provide this information. Staff was able to obtain a list of invoices by external contract providers from the Company and sort the providers into “vegetation management contractors and equipment,” “staging contractors,” and all other contractor and equipment for each of the storms based on the contract and invoice data. The Company could have done the same thing Staff did for the other named and un-named storms, but would not. We note the Company subsequently provided additional detail in response to Staff discovery for Hurricane Helene. Again, the Company could have provided this additional detail for the other named and un-named storms, but would not. 
Q.Are other utilities able to provide this information or proxies for this information?
A.Yes. 
Q.In the absence of information that only the Company can provide, is there another approach the can use to quantify the costs of internal and external resources already recovered in base revenues?
A.Yes. The Commission can use informed judgment to approximate the percentage of internal and external resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” activities to storm response activities and the related costs already included and recovered in base revenues as a rebuttal presumption. The Company then can decide whether to do its own analyses or not.
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission disallow all overtime labor and all outside services contractor costs for resources that were redeployed from “business as normal” activities that already are recovered through base revenues. In the absence of more detailed information that only the Company can provide, we recommend that 20% of the daily overtime labor and external contractor costs incurred during the post-storm restoration period be disallowed from the storm cost regulatory asset, which the Company can refine on rebuttal if it chooses to do so, subject to Staff review and Staff supplemental testimony as appropriate.
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to improve its accounting and reporting systems so that it can differentiate between and correctly quantify the incremental and non-incremental costs and exclude the non-incremental costs for redeployed internal and external resources already recovered through base revenues, including overtime labor and external contractor costs.
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $3.173 million in the requested base revenue increase due to a reduction of $10.083 million in the storm cost regulatory asset. The reduction in the storm cost regulatory asset for this disallowance is less than 1% of the total storm costs incurred by the Company.                                                                                                    
IV. The Company Overstated The Storm cost Regulatory Asset BECAUSE IT FAILED TO REMOVE ALL CAPITAL COSTS THAT INSTEAD SHOULD HAVE BEEN RECORDED TO PLANT IN SERVICE
A.Overview Of Capital Cost And The Errors That Overstated The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset And Understated Plant In Service
Q.Describe the physical damage to the system from these storms using the Company’s description of the damage caused by Hurricane Helene.
A.The Company’s witnesses describe the extent of the physical damage to the system from Hurricane Helene as follows:
Hurricane Helene was the most damaging storm in the Company’s history, impacting nearly 1.6 million customers and causing extreme infrastructure damage, including 12,239 broken poles and more than 20,000 spans of wire downed. For comparison, Hurricane Helene had more broken poles and downed spans of wire than Hurricanes Matthew, Irma, Michael, Zeta, and Idalia combined. In response, Georgia Power mobilized more than 20,000 personnel, including crews from over 35 partner companies, to repair or replace poles, lines, and transformers, and in some cases, rebuild portions of the power grid from the ground up.
Q.Describe the two categories of costs incurred to respond to a storm.
A.The first category of costs are the costs to replace and/or substantially rebuild physical assets that have been damaged. These costs are accounted for as capital costs because they are costs incurred for physical assets that have physical lives and provide service to customers over many decades. 
The second category of costs are the costs to repair minor damage to assets and restore service to customers, such as retensioning conductor or replacing hardware items that are not units of property. These costs are accounted for as operation and maintenance (O&M) expenses and deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset, which generally are recovered over a period much shorter than the service lives of physical assets. 
In response to storm events, costs are incurred that cannot be directly categorized as either capital or O&M expense because they were incurred for both activities. All such costs should be properly allocated between capital (plant in service) and O&M expense (storm cost regulatory asset), not simply assigned to O&M expense.
Q.Describe the Company’s accounting for the storm cost regulatory asset and how capital amounts are removed from regulatory asset and recorded to plant in service.
A.The Company initially records all storm costs to the storm cost regulatory asset. It then determines the costs that should be removed from the storm cost regulatory asset and instead recorded to plant in service using a formulaic standard cost methodology. The standard cost methodology for capital costs is triggered only for the removal of damaged existing plant assets and the installation of new plant assets that are eligible and/or required to be capitalized to the plant in service accounts for accounting and reporting purposes.
The standard cost methodology for each asset starts with a calculation of the capital costs under “business as normal” conditions. It includes the inventory cost; an inventory stores cost load; straight time labor based on a standard number of manhours times a straight time average hourly rate; a standard labor cost load; a standard fleet cost load; a supervision and engineering (S&E) cost load; an administrative and general (A&G) cost load; and other cost loads. 
The Company then modifies the standard cost methodology for each asset to include additional costs in the capital cost credit under “storm event” conditions. More specifically, the Company applies a 125% “multiplier” to the standard straight time labor cost under “business as normal” conditions to calculate the labor cost included in the capital cost credit under “storm event” conditions.  The Company developed the 125% multiplier based on the assumption that the labor cost consists of straight time labor at 100% for half of the standard manhours and the overtime labor cost at 150% of the straight time labor cost for the other half of the standard manhours. 
The Company does not directly measure either the straight time or the overtime labor charged to the storm cost regulatory asset for the capital cost credit. As we noted previously, the Company does not record straight time labor to the storm cost regulatory asset, except for excess straight time labor and affiliate straight time labor.
The Company calculates the equipment costs included in the capital cost credit as 25% of the labor costs. 
The Company calculates the cost of inventory included in the capital cost credit based on an average of the standard costs under normal site conditions and the higher standard costs under inaccessible site conditions, and switches and switching cubicles based on the higher standard cost under inaccessible conditions. 
The Company calculates the cost of certain contractor costs included in the capital cost credit at 100% for hydrovac contractors, 25% for traffic control contractors, 15% for data analytics and technology (DTA) contractors, 100% for crew support contractors, and 25% for miscellaneous repair contractors, but does not calculate the capital portion of other external contractor costs. 
Q.Did the Company correctly capitalize and include in the capital cost credit all capital costs related to the removal and replacement of damaged physical assets?
A.No. The Company incurred significant other external contractor costs to remove and replace damaged physical assets. Yet, the Company did not and does not capitalize any of these other external contractor costs, exclude these costs from the storm cost regulatory asset through the capital cost credit, or correctly record the costs in the plant in service accounts. More specifically, the Company did not and does not capitalize and include in the capital cost credit any of the external line contractor costs, the single largest category of external contractor costs. Nor does the Company capitalize and include in the capital cost credit any of the external vegetation management contractor costs. Nor does the Company capitalize and include in the capital cost credit any of the staging costs. The entirety of these external contractor costs remains in the storm cost regulatory asset after the capital cost credit for other external contractor costs removes those costs from the storm cost regulatory asset.

A.The following table shows the percentage of the storm costs capitalized to plant in service for each of the named storms and for the un-named storms by the Company.

Q.Are these capitalization amounts and percentages reasonable?
A.No. They are remarkably low for the majority of the named storms, but especially low for Hurricane Helene, especially given the magnitude of the physical damage to the system for that storm described by the Company’s witnesses in their direct testimony that we previously cited and quoted in part. 
In addition, we are aware from publicly available information that Duke Energy Carolinas, LLC. capitalized approximately 42% of its Hurricane Helene storm costs, or approximately five times more than the 8.2% the Company capitalized for the same storm.
Q.Explain why it matters whether the costs are recorded to capital or remain in the storm cost regulatory asset.
A.It matters from an accounting and ratemaking perspective. As we previously noted, capital costs incurred for physical assets are properly recorded in plant in service accounts, included in the base revenue requirement, and recovered from customers over the service lives of the assets. In contrast, the costs recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset are included in the base revenue requirement and recovered from customers generally over a shorter period than the capital costs, at a lower rate of return, and/or in a different form if the recovery is levelized.
Q.Describe the Staff’s investigation into the Company’s accounting and methodologies that it used to calculate the capital cost credit. 
A.Given the Company’s extremely low percentages of storm costs capitalized to plant in service for the majority of the named storms and the fact the Company capitalized only 8.2% of the costs of Hurricane Helene compared to the 42% capitalized by Duke Energy Carolinas, LLC. for the same storm, we carefully reviewed and assessed the Company’s accounting and the methodologies it used to calculate the capital cost credits recorded to plant in service. 
In our review and assessment, we identified conceptual and calculation errors that caused the capital costs to be understated, not only for Hurricane Helene, but also for the other storms. 
We subsequently address each of these conceptual and calculation errors. The errors include a conceptual and calculation error the Company acknowledged in response to Staff discovery that understates the labor cost included in the capital cost credit, a conceptual error that further understated the labor cost included in the capital cost credit, and a conceptual error that understated the external contractor costs included in the capital cost credit.
As we previously noted, an increase in the capital cost credit does not result in a disallowance of storm costs; rather, it transfers the costs from the storm cost regulatory asset to plant in service costs, which then will be included in the Company’s base revenue requirement in the next base rate proceeding and recovered in that manner.
B.Calculation Error That Understated Labor Cost Included In Capital Cost Credit
Q.Describe the calculation error that understated the labor cost included in the capital cost credit for Hurricane Helene.  
A.The Company’s original calculation of this removal was incorrectly based on a percentage of costs applied not only to its own labor, but also to specialized external contract labor for such things as hydro-vac services, special equipment operation, assessor fees, and engineering services. In response to Staff discovery after a Teams meeting with the Company’s subject matter experts, the Company confirmed there was an error in its calculations and that approximately $8 million more should have been capitalized for Hurricane Helene as follows:
   In responding to this request, the Company recognized that the specialized contract labor in the estimated labor basis from JETS was not deducted prior to the application of labor adder deduction since contractors are not applied labor adders. This correction is reflected in the tab ‘STF-SCR-1-17_Recalculation’ within “PSC Staff Follow Up – 4.3.26” and results in an increase of approximately $8 million to the amount that were previously capitalized. The Company will make this adjustment during its final true-up to reflect this change.
The Company utilizes its JETS application to calculate the capital cost credit for plant in service costs using a standard cost methodology. It uses a standard cost methodology for “business as normal” conditions and the same standard cost methodology with modifications for storm conditions.  One difference in the standard cost methodology under storm conditions is the addition of a 25% adder, applied as a 125% multiplier, to the standard cost labor rates used under “business as normal” conditions in an attempt to recognize that it includes only overtime labor costs in the storm cost regulatory asset. 
The Company provided examples of the JETS output used to record distribution capitalized costs for Hurricane Helene in response to discovery, that appeared to reflect errors resulting in an inordinately low level of capitalized costs for Hurricane Helene. Staff participated with Company subject matter experts in a Teams meeting on March 26, 2026 to review the various inputs and components of the JETS output for capitalization and to explain its concerns with the Company’s calculations.
According to the Company, the JETS labor rates also factor in all labor overhead loaders such as benefits, incentive costs, and payroll taxes. These overhead loaders in JETS amounted to 99.86% of in-house labor applicable to Hurricane Helene and represent the overhead percentages applicable to straight-time pay. Since only overtime pay was included in the Company’s incremental storm costs to be capitalized, the overhead loader amounts had to be removed from the JETS output to determine the incremental distribution costs to be capitalized.  
Q.Does this error in the calculation of the capital cost credit for Hurricane Helene extend to the other storms?
A.We believe it does, but were not able to confirm this on the expedited schedule for this proceeding. We believe the Company used the same calculation methodology for the other storms in the absence of any information to the contrary. 
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission correct this error that understated the capital cost credit for all storm costs in this proceeding. As we noted, the Company agrees the error should be corrected for Hurricane Helene. We recommend the Company provide the amounts necessary to correct this error for the other storms or provide evidence that the error did not extend to the other storms. We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to correct this error going forward for all future storms.
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $2.578 million in the requested base revenue increase based on a reduction of $8.192 million in the storm cost regulatory asset, solely related to the Hurricane Helene costs after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend. We were not able to quantify the effects on the requested base revenue increase related to the other storms. However, the Commission should incorporate the correction of this error related to the other storms after the Company confirms this error exists as well for those storms and provides the quantifications necessary to correct those errors.
C.Conceptual And Calculation Error That Understated Labor Cost Included In Capital Cost Credit
Q.Did the Company correctly calculate the labor cost included in the capital cost credit?
A.No. The Company’s calculation understates the capital cost credit because it incorrectly assumes that the labor costs are half straight time labor and half overtime labor. This assumption is inherently incorrect because the Company does not and did not record any straight time labor to the storm cost regulatory asset. Indeed, the Company recorded and continues to record for this purpose only overtime labor, all of which has a 50% premium over straight time labor, under the assumption that the manhours are the same under storm conditions as under business as normal conditions. The capital cost credit should not reflect in any manner the lower straight time labor costs that were not recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset. The capital cost credit should reflect only overtime labor because only overtime labor is recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset. 
Q.How can the Commission correct this error?
A.The error can be corrected by increasing the straight time labor cost “multiplier” to 150% from the Company’s present multiplier of only 125% so that the labor costs excluded from the storm cost regulatory asset to plant in service through the capital cost credit are properly matched to the labor costs recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset.
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission correct this conceptual and calculation error that understated the capital cost credit for the storm costs in this proceeding. We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to correct this error going forward for all future storms.
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $1.279 million in the requested base revenue increase based on a reduction of $4.066 million in the storm cost regulatory asset balance after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend. 
D.Conceptual And Calculation Error That Understated External Contractor Cost Included In Capital Cost Credit
Q.Does the Company’s standard cost methodology used for the capital cost credit correctly calculate the external contractor capital costs that should be excluded from the storm cost regulatory asset and instead recorded to plant in service? 
A.No. The Company’s methodology also understates the external contractor costs in the calculation of the capital cost credit. The Company’s methodology incorrectly excludes all external line contractor costs, all external vegetation management contractor costs, and all staging costs from the capital cost credit, thus overstating the costs in the storm cost regulatory asset and understating the costs recorded to plant in service. 
Q.Is it reasonable to exclude all external contractor costs from the costs included in the capital cost credit?
A.No. These external contractor costs were incurred to repair all damage to the system necessary to restore service, which include both the capital cost activities necessary to remove existing assets that were damaged and install replacement assets (units of property) and O&M expense cost activities (not tied to units of property) necessary to restore service. External contractor costs were the single largest category of costs incurred in response to each of the storms.
The portion of the costs necessary to remove existing assets that were damaged and install replacement assets are capital costs and should have been included in the capital cost credit, excluded from the storm cost regulatory asset, and included in plant in service. 
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission correct the Company’s standard cost methodology used to calculate the capital cost credit and increase the capital costs recorded to plant in service to include external line contractor costs, external vegetation management contractor costs, and external staging contractor costs. 
We recommend that a minimum of 20% of these external contractor costs be included in the capital cost credit in the absence of more detailed information that only the Company can provide, but refused to do so in response to Staff discovery. We note this 20% is less than half of the 42% capitalization percentage calculated by Duke Energy Carolinas for Hurricane Helene. The Company can refine this percentage on rebuttal if it chooses to do so in response to our recommendation, although we reserve the right to review and supplement our direct testimony in response to any Company information provided for the first time in rebuttal testimony. 
We also recommend the Commission direct the Company to utilize the corrected standard cost methodology to calculate the capital cost credit for storm costs incurred in the future. 
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $68.948 million in the requested base revenue increase based on a reduction of $219.119 million in the storm cost regulatory asset after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend.

VI. The Commission Should deny The Company’s PROPOSAL to Increase The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset INCLUDED IN RATE BASE FOR ESTIMATED STORM COSTS AFTER December 31, 2025
Q.Describe the Company’s calculation of the storm cost regulatory asset and the related rate base, storm expense amortization, storm expense accrual for future storms, and estimated storm charges for future storms reflected in the storm cost revenue requirement. 
A.The Company included a return on the storm cost regulatory asset less accumulated amortization less related ADIT included in rate base in the first component of its request to increase base revenues to recover the storm cost regulatory asset. The Company started with the $911.591 million storm cost regulatory asset recorded at December 31, 2025. It then added another $8.438 million in estimated future storm costs based on its requested storm expense accrual for future storms for each month starting on January 1, 2026 and continuing through May 31, 2027. It subtracted $2.618 million for the storm expense accrual for future storm costs presently recovered in base revenues for each month starting January 1, 2026 and continuing through May 31, 2026, then subtracted $27.429 million for each month, calculated as the sum of its proposed annual $227.898 million in storm cost regulatory asset amortization expense and its proposed annual $101.254 million in storm expense accrual for future storms divided by 12. 
The Company then calculated the return on the storm cost regulatory asset starting with the estimated $940.689 million storm cost regulatory asset at June 1, 2026 instead of the actual $911.591 million storm cost regulatory asset recorded at December 31, 2025.
 The following table shows the Company’s calculation of the rate base for each month starting with the $940.689 million in June 2026 through May 2027 and the 13-month average used to calculate the return on rate base amount included in the base revenue requirement and requested increase.

Q.Are the Company’s calculations of the return on component of the base revenue requirement correct?
A.No. First, the calculations are not based on the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025. The calculations include additions for estimated storm costs after December 31, 2025 and subtractions for presently authorized recoveries for future storm costs through base revenues from January 2026 through May 2026 and then its requested recoveries for future storm costs from June 2026 through May 2027. 
The Company’s calculations violate the requirements set forth in paragraph 3 of the Stipulation approved by the Commission in Docket 44280 in its Order dated July 31, 2025, which states:
The Company will be allowed to recover actual reasonable and prudent storm damage cost incurred through December 31, 2025, that exceeds the amount of the annual storm damage accrual approved in the Order Adopting Settlement Agreement as Modified.
None of the Company’s adjustments to the storm cost regulatory asset after December 31, 2025 are allowed pursuant to the Stipulation, except the reductions related to recoveries of the storm cost regulatory asset authorized in this present proceeding. We subsequently address this issue in more detail. 
Second, the Company’s calculations include estimated storm costs greater than the storm expense accrual for future storm costs presently recovered in base revenues for January 2026 through May 2026. This error resulted in an increase of $29.098 million in the storm cost regulatory asset from the $911.591 million at December 31, 2025 to $940.689 million at May 31, 2026. Although this error did not affect the Company’s requested amortization expense included in the storm expense accrual, which was based on the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025, it overstated the rate base used to calculate the return on the storm cost regulatory asset included in the requested base revenue requirement.
Third, the Company’s methodology presumes that it is entitled to a rate of return, not only on the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025, but on any increases in the regulatory asset after December 31, 2025 for storm costs that exceed recoveries of the storm expense accruals for future storms. This would represent a significant change in policy for the Commission, one the Company did not expressly request in this proceeding, but appears to have implicitly requested by incorporating the change in methodology into its calculations. The Commission historically has not allowed a return on storm costs incurred and deferred to a storm cost regulatory asset in the interval between the effective dates when base rates are reset or on a deferred basis.

A.We recommend the Commission deny the Company’s request to recover a return on the estimated storm costs incurred after December 31, 2025 in this proceeding. The Commission’s Order and the Stipulation last year limited recovery in this proceeding to costs incurred through December 31, 2025. 
In addition, we note that the Company’s request to recover the “return of” (amortization) of the storm cost regulatory asset is, in fact, correctly limited to the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025. The return on the storm cost regulatory asset should be based on the same date that is used to calculate the return of the storm cost regulatory asset, and more specifically, the December 31, 2025 date specified in the Commission Order.
 Further, we note that, as a practical matter, the Commission cannot review estimated costs that have not yet been incurred for the five months after December 31, 2025. To the extent costs actually were and are incurred and correctly recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset after December 31, 2025, then the Company will be eligible to seek recovery of these additional costs in its next base rate proceeding to the extent that the recoveries for such costs in present base revenues are not sufficient to cover those actual costs. 
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $2.138 million in the requested base revenue increase based on a reduction $29.098 million in the storm cost regulatory asset after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend. 
VII. The Commission Should deny The Company’s Request To Recover A Return On The Storm cost Regulatory Asset At Its grossed-up Weighted average Cost of Capital
Q.Describe the Company’s request to recover a grossed-up rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset.
A.The Company proposes to recover a grossed-up WACC return on the storm cost regulatory asset net of the accumulated amortization and net of the related ADIT for the twelve months ending May 31, 2027. The Company used a 13-month average to calculate the rate base for this purpose. The Company calculated the WACC based on a long-term debt ratio of 44.0% and an average cost of 4.41% and a common equity ratio of 56.0% at a cost of 10.50%.
Q.Is it reasonable to include a rate of return at the Company’s WACC on the storm cost regulatory asset in the revenue requirement?
A.No. The Company does not presently recover or defer any rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset. If the Commission includes a rate of return in the revenue increase, it will incrementally increase the Company’s earned return, all else equal. 
The Company has provided no evidence in this proceeding that a rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset is necessary for its financial integrity or to earn the lower end of the earnings deadband set in the Stipulation approved by the Commission last year. 
In addition, the Company has presented no evidence in this proceeding that it financed the storm cost regulatory asset using long-term debt and common equity. Further, even if it had or could provide such evidence, it, nevertheless, could modify its actual financing to reflect only debt, either short-term debt or intermediate term debt, or a combination of both.
Further, even with the Staff recommendation to levelize (annuitize) the recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset to minimize the overrecovery of these deferred costs, the Company inherently will overrecover between base rate proceedings due to increases in the kWh sales, kW demand, and customers used to calculate rates with the storm cost recovery costs included resulting in actual revenues greater than the revenue requirement reflected in the rates using those billing determinants.
Q.What are your recommendations?
A.We recommend the Commission authorize a debt only rate of return on the storm cost regulatory asset. The debt only rate of return reflects the lack of any evidence from the Company that it financed the storm cost regulatory asset through equity and long-term debt financing. This is an approximate midpoint between the status quo with no return on the storm cost regulatory asset and the Company’s request to recover a grossed-up rate of return It also reflects the lower financial and regulatory risk from recovery of a financial asset compared to the recovery of the costs of operating assets and activities. The Company has the option of actually financing the asset with debt, either short-term or long-term. Finally, it recognizes the Company will overrecover the storm cost regulatory asset even with the Staff recommendations to levelized recovery due to growth in sales and other billing determinants between base rate proceedings when the actual rates using those billing determinants are developed.
We also recommend that the return on be applied only to the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025 less accumulated amortization and related ADIT as the regulatory asset is amortized and recovered from customers. 
Q.What are the effects of your recommendation?
A.The effect of using the Company’s debt only return is a reduction of $20.037 million in the requested base revenue increase.
VIII. The Storm Cost Regulatory Asset Recovery PERIOD Should Be EXTENDED AND THE REVENUE RECOVERY SHOULD BE LEVELIZED
Q.Will the rate base amounts for the storm cost regulatory asset continue to decline in the months after May 2027?
A.Yes. The Company calculated the rate base amounts for the twelve months ending May 2027. The rate base amounts for the storm cost regulatory asset will continue to decline each month as the amount recorded at December 31, 2025 is amortized and recovered from customers starting on June 1, 2026. This will result in a reduction in the revenue requirement each month from June 2026 through May 2027, which the Company reflected in its requested increase. This also will result in a reduction in the revenue requirement each month after May 2027 until the storm cost regulatory asset at December 31, 2025 is fully amortized and recovered from customers.
Q.Did the Company reflect these declines in the rate base and the reduction in the revenue requirement each month after May 2027 in its requested increase in base revenues?
A.No. This is an error because the Company will continue to recover the return on rate base amounts at the amounts included in its requested increase in base revenues even as the underlying costs and revenue requirement continue to decline. The Company will retain these excess recoveries absent a form of recovery that expressly addresses these ongoing reductions in the underlying costs and revenue requirement. 
We note that the return on the declining rate base is a concern only if the Commission authorizes a return on rate base. If there is no return on rate base, then the revenue requirement consists only of the return of, or amortization of, the storm cost regulatory asset and the revenue requirement will remain the same throughout the recovery period until the storm cost regulatory asset is fully amortized.
QWhat alternatives are available to the Commission to reflect these declines in the rate base and the return on if a return on is included in the revenue requirement as the rate base declines each month?
A.Other than the use of a rider to recover these storm costs, there are two practical base revenue approaches that ensure the Company recovers and customers pay only the Company’s actual costs, no more and no less, except for the inherent overrecovery due to the growth in sales, demand, and customer billing determinants between base rate proceedings. 
The first and best approach is to calculate the revenue requirement on a levelized (annuitized) basis over the recovery period in the same manner as a home mortgage payment is calculated. This first approach is designed to avoid excessive recoveries that either are retained by the utility or subject to true-up and refunded at a later date. 
The second approach is to calculate the excess base revenue recoveries compared to the declining revenue requirement and defer these amounts and a return on the deferred amounts at the grossed-up WACC as a regulatory liability for refund in a future base rate proceeding. This second approach is inferior to the first approach because it unnecessarily requires customers to pay excessive amounts only to have them refunded at a later date. 
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission calculate the revenue requirement on a levelized basis over the recovery period. This will occur without any further decision required by the Commission if it denies a return on rate base. However, it will require the Commission to authorize the recovery on a levelized basis if it includes a return on rate base, whether the Company’s request for a grossed-up WACC or our alternative recommendation for a debt-only return
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $12.929 million in the requested base revenue increase after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend. 
Q.Describe the Company’s proposed amortization and recovery period.
A.The Company proposes to amortize and recovery the storm cost regulatory asset over four years.
Q.Is there any compelling reason to amortize and recover the storm cost regulatory asset over four years?
A.No. The requested amortization and recovery period of four years is unduly short and does not align with the timing of the Company’s historic three-year alternative rate plans approved by the Commission in the last several base rate proceedings. 
The amortization and recovery period is a matter of informed judgment for the Commission to decide. The shorter the amortization and recovery period, the greater the base revenue increase and the longer the amortization and recovery period, the lesser the base revenue increase.
Factors that should inform the judgment of the Commission are the magnitude of the storm cost regulatory asset, the timing of future rate proceedings, the form of recovery, and the financial integrity of the utility. 
In this case, the storm cost regulatory asset is significant, caused in large part by the unprecedented damage and costs caused by Hurricane Helene. This argues for a longer amortization period to mitigate the effects on customer rates. 
In this case, the Company’s base revenues were set through December 31, 2028 via the Stipulation approved by the Commission last year, subject to change only for recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset costs and refund of the PTC regulatory liabilities. This argues for a longer amortization period, properly timed so that it is consistent with the timing of the three-year alternative rate plans the Commission has approved in the last several base rate proceedings. If the three-year pattern is maintained, then the amortization period could be extended beyond May 31, 2030 (based on the 48 months requested by the Company) to coincide with that three-year pattern to December 31, 2031 (67 months), December 31, 2034 (103 months), or December 31, 2037 (139 months). The reason to set the amortization period to coincide with the three-year pattern is to ensure that the base revenue recovery does continue beyond the end of a three-year recovery period and result in excessive revenues to the Company and harm to customers. 
In this case, the Company is financially healthy and does not require an unduly short amortization and recovery period to restore or maintain its financial integrity.
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission extend the amortization period through December 31, 2034 to 103 months from the requested 48 months and to coincide with the timing of the historic three-year rate case pattern ending on that date. 
We also recommend the Commission adopt a condition that if the storm cost regulatory asset is fully amortized prior to the effective date of new base rates reset in base rate case proceeding, then the Company is required to defer the excess recoveries to a regulatory liability for future refund to customers.
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $89.016 million in the requested base revenue increase after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend.
IX. The Company’s Definition of A Storm Event Reflects An Inordinately Low Threshold of $50,000
Q.Describe the Company’s definition of storm event and how the dollar threshold affects the storm reserve and/or storm cost regulatory asset.
A.The Company’s definition of a major storm event involves the repair/restoration of its system for incremental costs exceeding only $50,000 and even for other emergency situations. The full definition is found in the Company’s Storm Accounting Guidelines and replicated below:
The following procedure should be followed in the event of a major storm that damages Georgia Power’s Distribution, Transmission, or Generation systems, and other emergency situations described below. 

A major storm is defined as a storm where the cost to repair / restore the systems to normal operating conditions will exceed $50,000 of incremental costs including labor, materials and other directly related incremental costs. These procedures will be followed by all Georgia Power Company personnel involved in accounting for major storm costs. Questions regarding Major Storm Declarations should be directed to the Storm Center Operations Manager. 

Other emergency situations covered by this procedure include localized storms not meeting the requirements to be classified as a major storm; holding personnel over as a contingency for possible storm activity; load shed contingency actions in the event of major system problems; and other emergency situations determined by the Southern Company Security Council. For each of the situations described above, the costs associated with them should be charged to the applicable project (will be provided via storm declaration), as well as the business units organization code.

Once a major storm is declared by the Company, 100% of the incremental costs associated with the repair/restoration of the system for that storm are recorded directly to the storm reserve/storm cost regulatory asset. The net balance associated with all major storms is defined as a storm reserve if the net balance in the storm reserve is a liability, while it is defined as a storm cost regulatory asset if the net balance in the storm reserve is an asset. If the total costs for a storm event were $45,000, the storm would not be declared as a major storm event and none of those costs would be recorded to the storm reserve/storm cost regulatory asset. If the total costs for a storm event were $100,000, the storm would be declared as a major storm event and the entire $100,000 would be recorded to the storm reserve/storm cost regulatory asset. 
Q.Under the Company’s definition of a major storm event, does the storm have to be a named storm?
A.No. In fact, there were numerous storms during the 2022-2025 review period declared as major storms that were unnamed. The Company’s application details the total costs for such storms during this period. Total costs recorded to the storm cost regulatory asset for these storms were $191.770 million at December 31, 2025. The Company did not identify or provide the costs for each declared, but unnamed, major storm event that comprise the amount deferred to the storm cost regulatory asset. 
Q.Is the $50,000 threshold for a storm event to be declared a major storm event inordinately low?
A.Yes. The $50,000 threshold can be met very quickly and is extremely low for such a large utility. Assume four outside line contractor crews of four workers each from one vendor are involved with storm restoration for one full day. Assume also that the standard 16-hour storm event day is required at an average billing rate of $230 per hour (including labor and equipment rates). The incremental storm costs from this restoration event would be $58,880 (16 workers x 16 hours x $230 per hour). That amount is before the addition of material costs and Georgia Power labor costs needed to supervise the restoration event. Storm events such as these are both frequent and recurring and are more akin to routine expenses. They might only impact a few hundred customers in one small region of Georgia Power’s large service territory and involve a relatively small number of customer minutes of interruption.  
Q.How does the Company’s $50,000 threshold compare to other utilities?
A.It is extremely low. The Company’s threshold is much lower than other utilities that are much smaller. The Company has approximately 2,700,000 customers and a $50,000 threshold. In contrast, Kentucky Power Company has only 163,000 customers and a $2 million threshold. New York State Electric & Gas Corporation (NYSEG) has approximately 894,000 customers and a $750,000 threshold. Rochester Gas and Electric Corporation (RG&E) has approximately 378,500 customers and a $500,000 threshold. 
Q.What do you conclude about the Company’s definition of a major storm event?
A.The Company’s definition of a major storm event should reflect a higher threshold to reflect the fact that minor and unnamed storm events are frequent and recurring. These events should be considered and managed as routine expenses to streamline and limit the costs deferred to the storm reserve to more significant weather events that require enhanced response activities and incur higher costs. They definitely should not be considered extraordinary or nonrecurring costs and should be considered as part of the base rate planning process. In addition, only incremental costs above that threshold amount should be allowed to be deferred.  
Q.What is your recommendation?
A.We recommend the Commission set the major storm declaration threshold to a minimum of $2 million per storm event before the Company is allowed to defer the costs over that threshold to the storm reserve/storm cost regulatory asset. The Company would record all costs below that threshold as operations and maintenance (O&M) expenses and recover those costs in base revenues. This is a prospective change and does not impact the storm cost regulatory asset recovery requested in this proceeding. 
X. The Commission Should Deny The Company’s Request to Increase The Storm Expense Accrual For Future Storms By $69.835 Million In This Proceeding And Address ChangEs In FUTURE COMPREHENSIVE BASE RATE PROCEEDINGS
Q.Describe the Company’s request to increase the storm expense accrual for future storms in this proceeding.
A.As we noted previously, the Company seeks to expand the scope of this proceeding to increase the storm expense accrual for future storm costs from the present $31.419 million recovered in base revenues to $101.254 million, an increase of $69.835 million.
Q.Did the Stipulation approved by the Commission last year include consideration of the storm expense accrual for future storm costs in this limited storm cost recovery proceeding?
A.No. The Stipulation addressed only the recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset. In the Commission Order approving the Stipulation under Findings of Fact 2, the Commission described its understanding of the scope of this limited storm cost recovery proceeding as follows:
The Company agrees to no adjustment to base rates, with any under-recovered storm costs considered in a separate proceeding to be filed in 2026.

The Settlement Agreement states that base rates will not be adjusted in 2026, 2027, and 2028. The rate freeze ensures that customer rates are predictable and stable. The Company agrees to continue the amortization of liabilities and regulatory assets approved by the Commission in its 2022 Rate Case Settlement Agreement. Joint Petitioners agree to allow the Company to recover its reasonable and prudent under-recovered storm damage costs that result from major storms, such as from Hurricane Helen. The Settlement Agreement allows for consideration of the recovery to be considered through fully litigated and narrow proceedings, which are to take place in 2026.
The Stipulation (referred to as the “Settlement Agreement” in the Commission Order) itself approved by the Commission addressed only the recovery of the storm cost regulatory asset. The Stipulation does not address changes in the storm damage expense accrual for future storm costs. It addresses only the recovery of storm damage costs incurred through December 31, 2025 and the related changes in the storm damage accrual to recover those costs. The relevant portion of the Stipulation states:
The Company will be allowed to recover actual reasonable and prudent storm damage cost incurred through December 31, 2025, that exceeds the amount of the annual storm damage accrual approved in the Order Adopting Settlement Agreement as Modified. The Company will file for the recovery of under-recovered storm damage cost no sooner than February 1, 2026, and no later than July I, 2026, with new rates effective the 1st of the month following 90 days after the request for recovery. The Company's filing will include at a minimum pre-filed direct testimony and the documentation supporting the request for changes in the storm damage accrual as well as the proposed period over which to allow recovery of the under-recovered storm damage balance. The Commission shall determine the rates, the period over which underrecovered storm costs will be recovered, and any other issues the Commission deem necessary to address the limited issue of storm damage cost recovery.
Q.What is your recommendation?
A. We recommend the Commission deny the request to increase the storm expense accrual for future storm costs in this proceeding. The Company’s request to increase the storm expense accrual for future storm costs is separate and apart from the Company’s request to recover the “actual reasonable and prudent storm damage cost through December 31, 2025” and is outside the express scope of this proceeding reflected in the Commission’s Order and the Stipulation itself. 
The request to increase the storm damage expense accrual for future storm costs is properly addressed in the context of a base rate proceeding wherein all revenues and all costs are considered on a comprehensive basis rather than on a selective basis. The Company is not harmed or benefited as a result of this recommendation. If actual storm expenses are more or less than the storm expense accrual presently reflected in the base revenues, then the under or over recovery, respectively, will be captured in the storm reserve for Commission consideration recovery or refund in a future base rate proceeding. 
Q.What is the effect of your recommendation?
A.The effect is an incremental reduction of $69.835 million in the requested base revenue increase after consideration of the prior adjustments that we recommend.

A.Yes.

QON WHOSE BEHALF ARE YOU TESTIFYING IN THIS PROCEEDING? 
AI am testifying on behalf of the Georgia Association of Manufacturers (GAM).  The members of GAM are customers of Georgia Power Company (GPC).  They consume large quantities of electricity, primarily for manufacturing, under a variety of rate schedules.  
QWhat issues are you addressing in your direct testimony?
AFirst, I provide an overview of GPC’s request to recover $912 million of storm damage expenses over a four-year period ending May 31, 2030.  I then discuss alternatives to GPC’s proposed recovery of these storm damage expenses.  Specifically, I address the amortization period over which these costs should be recovered, how these costs should be financed, and the appropriate cost recovery mechanism through which they should be recovered.  Finally, because I have identified more affordable ways to recover storm damage costs than proposed by GPC, I address why nuclear production tax credits (PTCs) authorized under Section 45U of the Internal Revenue Code should not be used to offset storm damage costs.
qDoes the fact that you are not addressing all issues in this proceeding constitute an endorsement of the positions of georgia power company or other parties on these issues?
ANo.  The fact that I am not addressing every issue should not be interpreted as an endorsement of proposals made by GPC or other parties in this proceeding.
qAre you sponsoring any exhibits with your direct testimony?

Summary
qPlease summarize your findings and recommendations.
AMy findings and recommendations are as follows:
GPC is proposing to increase base revenues by $270 million, which includes $360 million in (incremental) storm damage expenses ($390 million of annual costs less $31 million currently recoverable in base rates) offset by $90 million of Section 45U nuclear PTCs earned in 2024 and 2025.  
The $390 million revenue requirement assumes cost recovery using traditional ratemaking practices and a four-year amortization.  GPC would accrue carrying charges on the unamortized storm balance at its current 7.82% weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which includes a 10.5% return on equity (ROE) with equity comprising 56% of total capital.  
GPC is proposing to increase all base tariff rates by the same percentage.  
The proposed base rate increase would be offset by GPC’s separate Fuel Cost Recovery (FCR-27) filing in Docket No. 56765, in which the Company is seeking to reduce annual fuel revenues by $388 million.  The net impact of these concurrent filings is a reduction in total revenues of $118 million.
Although GPC proposes to use the $90 million of nuclear PTCs to offset these storm damage expenses to address affordability concerns, there are more appropriate alternatives to address affordability, such as:
Extending cost recovery over a longer amortization period; and
Using debt financing.
Both alternatives would be more cost-effective than GPC’s proposal.  
Extending the amortization period to eight years, for example, would reduce the annual revenue requirement by approximately 28%.  
Using debt financing, coupled with an eight-year amortization period, would reduce the annual revenue requirement by 37%.  
Securitization is a more cost-effective approach than traditional ratemaking for financing storm damage costs.  Securitization would provide GPC with cash upfront, and base rates would be lower (and thus, more affordable) because it would support a more gradual recovery of the costs.  Because storm damage costs are deferred expenses, securitization would not result in lost shareholder profits.   
Rather than recovering the storm damage costs by adjusting base tariff rates, the Commission should require all storm damage costs to be recovered through a separate Storm Cost Recovery (SCR) rider designed as a percentage of base revenues.  This treatment would allow GPC to track the remaining storm damage balance more accurately and adjust the rate, as necessary, to recognize projected sales and base revenue growth.
By deploying more cost-effective alternatives, it becomes unnecessary to use the nuclear PTCs to offset the storm damage costs as proposed by GPC.  Instead, consistent with cost causation, the nuclear PTCs should be used to offset fuel expense in GPC’s calculation of FCR-27 rates in its concurrently pending Docket No. 56765.
Overview
qwHAT is georgia power company proposing in this application?
AGPC is proposing to increase annual base revenues by $270 million (3.2% of total annual revenue) to recover expenses associated with major storms since the Company’s 2022 base rate case over a four-year period.  Pursuant to the 2022 rate case settlement, the increase is to be applied to each customer group on an equal percentage basis, excluding Fuel Cost Recovery (FCR) revenues, to each class’s energy, demand, and basic service charge components.
qis this the only pending application that will impact retail rates?
ANo.  In a concurrent filing GPC is seeking to reduce annual fuel revenues by $388 million (4.6% of total annual revenue).  Together, the two filings would result in a net $118 million (1.4%) reduction in overall rates ($388 million less $270 million).  The FCR and storm cost recovery rates are proposed to become effective June 1, 2026.
qWhy is Georgia Power COmpany proposing to recover storm costs in this proceeding?
AAlthough a three-year rate freeze for the period 2026 through 2028 became effective as a result of the Alternate Rate Plan (ARP) Stipulation approved by the Commission in this docket on July 31, 2025, the ARP Stipulation allowed for recovery of storm costs as the sole exception to this rate freeze.  As a result, GPC is proposing to recover the expenses associated with various weather events that have impacted its system since the 2022 base rate case.
qPlease describe the key components included in Georgia Power COmpany’s proposed storm cost recovery.
AGPC is requesting recovery of the following costs:
$912 million in damages resulting from ten named (and various unnamed) storms not otherwise recovered in base rates, which includes internal labor and fleet, outside services, materials and supplies, and other expenses that are incurred as a result of major storms that exceeded $50,000 per event;
An accrual for annual storm expenses averaging $101.3 million per year; and
Carrying charges on the unamortized balance of the storm regulatory asset ($621 million net of accumulated deferred income taxes) for the period May 2026 through May 2027 at GPC’s current 7.82% WACC, which includes 56% common equity at an ROE of 10.50% and 44% long-term debt at a cost of 4.41%.
qWhat is the total revenue deficiency for which Georgia Power Company is requesting recovery in this proceeding?
AIn total, GPC is requesting recovery of $360 million in storm expenses that are incremental to the $31 million currently recovered in base rates.  GPC is proposing to offset this increase by applying $90 million in nuclear PTCs such that the net revenue requirement requested in this proceeding is $270 million.  
qHow is Georgia Power COmpany proposing to recover past storm damage costs?
AGPC is proposing to use traditional ratemaking practices to recover past storm damage expenses; that is, the $912 million storm recovery regulatory asset balance would be amortized over four years with carrying charges on the unamortized balance at GPC’s current 7.82% WACC.  
qHow is Georgia Power COmpany proposing to recover the $270 million base revenue increase from customers?
AGPC is proposing to spread the increase to each class on an equal percentage of base revenues to each class, pursuant to the ARP Stipulation.  However, it would do so by adjusting all base tariff charges rather than through a separate cost recovery mechanism.  As discussed later, recovering these expenses in a separate mechanism (such as a rider) would facilitate tracking both the revenues collected and the remaining storm damage balance.  
Alternatives to Georgia Power Company’s Proposed Cost Recovery
qHave you identified any issues with Georgia Power COmpany’s proposal to recover these incremental storm costs?
AYes.  There are more cost-effective alternatives for recovering past storm damage expenses.  These alternatives include a longer amortization period and the use of debt financing.  Either alternative would result in less upward rate pressure on customers.
Amortization Period
qOver what period is Georgia Power COmpany proposing to amortize Storm costs in this proceeding?
AGPC is proposing to recover the $912 million under-recovery balance over a four-year period ending May 31, 2030.  
qWHat is the revenue requirement impact of Georgia Power Company’s proposed four-year amortization period?
AA four-year amortization period would result in an initial annual revenue requirement of $390 million.  
qHow does the choice of amortization period impact affordability?
AThe amortization period defines the number of years over which past storm damage expenses would be recovered.  Thus, extending the amortization period would spread cost recovery over more years, thereby reducing the annual revenue requirement and making rates more affordable.  For example, if recovery were extended to eight years, the initial annual revenue requirement would be $280 million, approximately 28% less than the 4-year recovery.
qHow would a longer amortization period impact the total revenues Georgia Power COmpany would recover from customers?
AOver time, an extended amortization period would result in GPC recovering more money, but the rates paid by customers would be more affordable, particularly in the near term.
Financing Costs
qhow is georgia power proposing to finance the $912 million actual storm recovery regulatory asset balance?
AGPC is proposing to use traditional ratemaking to recover the balance.  Under traditional ratemaking, GPC can accrue carrying charges on the unamortized balance.  For example, GPC projects that the average unamortized balance of the $912 million storm damage regulatory asset would be $621 million (net of accumulated deferred income taxes) for the 13-month period from May 2026 through May 2027.  The associated carrying charges at GPC’s 7.82% WACC would be $48.6 million.  The equity (or shareholder benefit) portion alone would be $36.5 million.  
qWould additional costs be incurred to finance the storm damage regulatory asset?
AYes.  Equity is fully taxable.  Further, the incremental revenues would increase GPC’s taxable income.  Thus, in addition to carrying charges, GPC would have to pay state and federal income taxes.  During the first year, income taxes would add $85 million to the annual revenue requirement.

qshould Georgia Power COmpany’s shareholders benefit from the recovery of storm damage costs?
ANo.  GPC’s shareholders should not profit from storm damages that resulted in significant outages and inconveniences to its customers.
qAre there any alternative methods of financing storm damage costs that would result in more affordable rates?
AYes.  If affordability is a concern, then storm damage costs should be financed entirely through long-term debt which, coupled with an eight-year amortization period, would reduce the initial annual revenue requirement by 37% to $245 million.  This treatment would remove the equity component that is included in GPC’s proposal, thereby preventing shareholders from benefiting from the inconveniences caused by prolonged outages and more accurately reflect that the storm damage costs are essentially incremental operation and maintenances costs rather than rate base investments.  
qare there more cost-effective ways to extend cost recovery while also using debt financing?
AYes.  Securitization would allow GPC to extend the recovery of storm damage costs while also using 100% debt financing, thereby improving affordability.  
qwhat is securitization?
ASecuritization is a financing mechanism that provides timely recovery of certain regulatory assets, such as storm damage, by issuing a type of debt that would significantly lower the carrying costs of the assets relative to the costs that would be incurred using traditional ratemaking practices.  Specifically, a special purpose (off-balance sheet) entity would be created whose sole purpose is to issue transition bonds that are used exclusively for purposes of reducing the amount of recoverable regulatory assets along with any other amounts as determined by the Commission through the refinancing or retirement of utility debt or equity.  This special purpose entity would be entitled to the revenues collected by the utility through a separate cost recovery mechanism in an amount sufficient to timely recovery of the debt services associated with securitization bonds.  Under this structure, securitization bonds would be “AAA” rated and carry the lowest possible interest rate.

qhow do the interest rates on securitized bonds compare with georgia power company’s weighted average cost of capital?
AGPC’s WACC is 7.82%.  However, as previously stated, equity is fully taxable.  Adjusting for income taxes, GPC’s pre-tax WACC is 9.78%.  Securitization is entirely debt financing.  Thus, there would be no income taxes associated with securitization.  Even after accounting for issuance costs, securitization financing would be less costly than traditional ratemaking.  Further, securitization bonds can allow cost recovery to be extended beyond four years.  This would result in less upward rate pressure than under traditional ratemaking.  
qIs securitization widely used?
AYes.  Over 30 states have authorized electric utilities to use securitization to finance costs, including storm repairs, wildfire mitigation, and infrastructure damage.  In the Southeast alone, regulatory commissions in Florida, North Carolina, South Carolina, and West Virginia have authorized securitization of storm damage costs.  Thus, securitization is a widely used regulatory tool.  A summary of enacted legislation authorizing securitization is provided in Exhibit JP-1.  As can be seen, securitization is widely used for recovering large expenditures, such as for damages caused by major storms and wildfires.  
qis securitization more cost-effective than traditional ratemaking? 
AYes.  For example, Exhibit JP-2 quantifies the nominal and net present value (NPV) costs of recovering $700 million of past storm damage expenses using traditional ratemaking (the solid green and dashed black lines) and securitization (the orange bars).  In this illustration, I removed the $101.3 million of annual storm damage expense and used GPC’s proposed cost of capital, current state/federal income tax rates, and both accelerated (4-year) and nominal (8-year) amortization.  Finally, I assumed a securitized interest rate of 4.3% and 2% issuance costs.  
qHow does securitization compare with traditional ratemaking?
AThe vertical axis of Exhibit JP-2 shows the annual revenue requirement.  The higher the annual revenue requirement, the higher the rate impact.  As can be seen, the annual revenue requirement for securitizing $700 million of regulatory assets is about $110 million per year over the 8-year average life of the securitization bonds.  By contrast, the annual revenue requirements under traditional ratemaking (for either a four- or eight-year amortization period) would be higher than for securitization. qhave credit rating agencies expressed any opinions on the use of securitized financing?
AYes.  For example, Moody’s Investors Service opined that utility cost recovery through securitization is credit positive provided that it is not overused.  A copy of Moody’s assessment is provided in Exhibit JP-3.  The key takeaways are:
Securitization typically benefits utilities and their current customers because utilities receive an immediate source of cash and are ensured recovery of large costs in a timely manner, and because the cost of securitized debt is lower than the utility’s all-in pre-tax cost of capital, it reduces the impact on customers’ monthly bills. 
Utility cost recovery charge securitization has become more versatile and prevalent.  It is now used to recover costs associated with storm restoration and environmental costs, utility restructuring, deferred fuel costs and renewable energy projects. 
State law and financing orders strongly protect securitization assets which ultimately protect the assets backing the bonds.
Too much securitization can have negative consequences such as impacting customer bills substantially while hurting the utility’s financial flexibility and ability to raise rates for other reasons.  
Regulatory Research Associates, a subsidiary of Standard & Poor’s, recently observed that there has been a rekindled interest in securitization, stating: 
Securitization allows utilities to recover sizable costs associated with impactful, and oftentimes unexpected, events upfront through the receipt of cash upon issuance of the bonds. This cash can be used to repay relatively expensive debt and equity financing traditionally used by utilities to initially absorb costs.
A second reason why utilities choose to issue securitization bonds is that it modestly reduces regulatory risk. Using securitization is usually less impactful on the customer bill than alternate forms of financing that the utility may pursue, such as the issuance of equity and/or corporate debt, which may be more costly. This typically results in less customer complaints, increasing the likelihood that the utility can continue to manage its regulatory risks effectively.
Furthermore, securitization often results in improved credit measures for the utility. Because securitization bonds are typically recovered through a dedicated source of revenue--in the form of an irrevocable, non-bypassable charge on the customer's bill with full recovery mandated by statute--S&P Global Ratings will often deconsolidate (i.e., remove) securitization debt from the utility's balance sheet. Accordingly, this improves a utility's financial measures and credit quality.
Qis securitization authorized under the current regulatory and legal environment in Georgia?
ANo.  It is my understanding that securitization would require both a change in law and regulations.  Given that securitization is widely used and accepted in a majority of states, there is no reason why a securitization bill could not be introduced and enacted in the next legislative session.
qWhat is the primary benefit of securitization?
AThe primary benefit of securitization is affordability for both GPC and GPC’s customers; that is, it would immediately provide GPC a significant cash infusion while allowing base rates to be significantly lower than under traditional ratemaking.  As securitization bonds would not be on GPC’s balance sheet, securitization would not impact GPC’s credit metrics or bond ratings.  
qWhat are the downsides to securitization?
ASecuritization charges are non-bypassable; that is, once securitization bonds are issued, they must be repaid through a dedicated non-bypassable fee on customer bills.  Although the Commission can periodically adjust the fees to better track revenues with the required debt service (interest and principal payments), once the costs have been approved and the bonds have been issued, it cannot subsequently disallow cost recovery.  Regardless of how they are financed, the presumption is that the securitized costs are both prudent and reasonable.  Thus, in this respect, securitization would not be substantively different from traditional ratemaking.
Cost Recovery Mechanism
qhow should storm damage expenses be recovered?
AThe Commission should require that all storm damage expenses be recovered through a separate SCR rider designed as a percentage of base revenues.  This would be similar to the design of the Environmental Compliance Cost Recovery rate.  
qwhy should storm damage expenses be recovered in a separate rider?
AA separate SCR rider would allow GPC to reduce the remaining storm damage balance based on the actual revenues collected while also allowing for periodic adjustments to reflect projected changes in base revenues due to sales growth.  Further, if securitization is authorized, the remaining regulatory asset balance can be easily determined prior to issuing the bonds.  The annual storm damage accrual would continue to be recovered in the SCR rider.  Thus, recovering storm damage costs through a separate SCR rider would provide a better mechanism to periodically adjust rates to ensure that cost recovery remains on track.  
Nuclear PTCs
qWhat are the nuclear production tax credits?
ASection 13105 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 created section 45U, the zero-emission nuclear power production credit, for electricity produced at a qualified nuclear power facility and sold by the taxpayer to an unrelated person in tax years beginning after December 31, 2023, and before January 1, 2033.
qWhat nuclear power facilities qualify for the INTERNAL revenue code Section 45u production tax credits?
ATo qualify, a nuclear power facility must have been placed in service before August 16, 2022.  This means that GPC’s Hatch and Vogtle units 1 and 2 potentially qualify to earn section 45U tax credits.
qHow are production tax credits earned?
APTCs are earned for every kilowatt-hour (kWh) generated by a qualified nuclear power facility.  
qdo production tax credits reduce a utility’s cost to serve?
AYes.  PTCs can be used to reduce the utility’s tax liability.  If the utility does not have a sufficient tax liability to utilize the PTCs, the utility can monetize the PTCs by selling them to third parties and use the proceeds to reduce costs.
qHow is georgia power company proposing to utilize the nuclear production tax credits?
AGPC is proposing to amortize 25% of the total PTCs generated in 2024 and 2025 to partially offset and lower storm damage costs.  GPC originally proposed to defer these PTCs to a regulatory liability and amortize them over a three-year period beginning on January 1, 2026.  However, to address affordability, GPC decided to use the section 45U PTCs to benefit customers.
qShould the nuclear production tax credits be used to offset storm damage expense?
ANo.  Because extending the recovery period and using debt financing will more appropriately address affordability for GPC’s customers, it is unnecessary to utilize the nuclear PTCs to offset storm damage expense.  Instead, as discussed in my testimony in GPC’s concurrent FCR-27 proceeding, these PTCs are more appropriately used to offset fuel expenses.
qPlease explain why Nuclear Production tax credits are more appropriately used to offset fuel expense.
AFirst, as previously stated, the nuclear PTCs are earned for every kWh generated by a qualified nuclear power facility.  Thus, PTCs are clearly more similar to fuel costs — which vary based on the amount of kWh generated — than to storm damage costs. 
Second, if PTCs are used to offset storm damage costs, the benefit would not be allocated properly. This is because, as previously stated, pursuant to the ARP Stipulation, storm damage costs are to be allocated and recovered on equal percentage of base revenues.  However, the percentage of base revenues charged to the various customer classes is quite different from the corresponding percentage of recoverable fuel costs. qplease explain table 4. 
ATable 4 quantifies the percentage of base revenues and the percentage of fuel revenues by customer class as presented in the class cost-of-service study filed by GPC in its 2022 rate case.  Also shown are the percentages of transmission and distribution costs.  The latter are a proxy for the storm damage costs, which primarily impact transmission and distribution facilities.  
qwhat do you conclude from table 4?
ATable 4 demonstrates that Residential customers have a higher percentage of base revenues and transmission/distribution costs than fuel revenues, while the opposite is true for Industrial customers.  This reflects the fact that Industrial customers typically operate at higher load factors than either Residential or Commercial customers, and Industrial customers take service at higher delivery voltages.  
Thus, using the nuclear PTCs to offset the FCR-27 costs is consistent with cost causation because these tax credits are earned on a kWh basis, and fuel costs are also directly related to the amount of kWh generated.  Further, there is no prohibition in the FCR rate that would prevent the return of nuclear PTCs if the Commission determines this treatment to be more appropriate.  
qwhat do you recommend?
ANuclear PTCs are not needed to offset storm damage expenses to address affordability.  Accordingly, the Commission should reject GPC’s proposal to use the nuclear PTCs to offset storm damage expenses in this proceeding and adopt my proposal in the FCR-27 preceding to use these PTCs to instead offset recoverable fuel costs.

 7   Q   WHAT IS SECURITIZATION?

 8   A   Securitization is a financing mechanism that provides timely recovery of certain

 9       regulatory assets, such as storm damage, by issuing a type of debt that would

10       significantly lower the carrying costs of the assets relative to the costs that would be

11       incurred using traditional ratemaking practices. Specifically, a special purpose (off-

12       balance sheet) entity would be created whose sole purpose is to issue transition bonds

13       that are used exclusively for purposes of reducing the amount of recoverable

14       regulatory assets along with any other amounts as determined by the Commission

15       through the refinancing or retirement of utility debt or equity. This special purpose

16       entity would be entitled to the revenues collected by the utility through a separate cost

17       recovery mechanism in an amount sufficient to timely recovery of the debt services

18       associated with securitization bonds. Under this structure, securitization bonds would

19       be “AAA” rated and carry the lowest possible interest rate.

21   A    A separate SCR rider would allow GPC to reduce the remaining storm damage

23        adjustments to reflect projected changes in base revenues due to sales growth.

 7   Q    WHAT ARE THE NUCLEAR PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS?

 8   A    Section 13105 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 created section 45U, the zero-

 9        emission nuclear power production credit, for electricity produced at a qualified nuclear

10        power facility and sold by the taxpayer to an unrelated person in tax years beginning

11        after December 31, 2023, and before January 1, 2033.


12   Q    WHAT NUCLEAR POWER FACILITIES QUALIFY FOR THE INTERNAL REVENUE

13        CODE SECTION 45U PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS?

14   A    To qualify, a nuclear power facility must have been placed in service before August

15        16, 2022. This means that GPC’s Hatch and Vogtle units 1 and 2 potentially qualify to

16        earn section 45U tax credits.


17   Q    HOW ARE PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS EARNED?

18   A    PTCs are earned for every kilowatt-hour (kWh) generated by a qualified nuclear power

19        facility.


20   Q    DO PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS REDUCE A UTILITY’S COST TO SERVE?

21   A    Yes. PTCs can be used to reduce the utility’s tax liability. If the utility does not have

22        a sufficient tax liability to utilize the PTCs, the utility can monetize the PTCs by selling

23        them to third parties and use the proceeds to reduce costs.

 1    Q         HOW IS GEORGIA POWER COMPANY PROPOSING TO UTILIZE THE NUCLEAR

11              appropriately address affordability for GPC’s customers, it is unnecessary to utilize the

13              in GPC’s concurrent FCR-27 proceeding, these PTCs are more appropriately used to

16              APPROPRIATELY USED TO OFFSET FUEL EXPENSE.

18              a qualified nuclear power facility. Thus, PTCs are clearly more similar to fuel costs —

19              which vary based on the amount of kWh generated — than to storm damage costs.

SECURITIZATION: A VALUABLE TOOL FOR COST
RECOVERY OPPORTUNITIES OUTSIDE A NORMAL
RATE CASE
In recent years, a flurry of legislative activity has addressed utility securitization. Seventeen states have
passed legislation offering securitization options to their electric utilities in the last decade, and several
other states have proposed similar legislation. Securitization, while not a new concept, has been a
successful tool for utilities to improve cost recovery while saving customers money on their electric bills.
In this paper, we analyze trends in securitization legislation and detail the options that may work for your
utility.


What Is Securitization?

When a utility incurs a large or unexpected but prudent expenditure, it typically seeks recovery of that
expenditure through a rate case. This creates regulatory lag, which is not ideal for the utility’s financials.
Further, it is not ideal for customers, as delays in cost recovery can increase the cost burden for customers
due to regulatory assets accruing carrying charges. These charges are often calculated at the utility’s cost
of debt.

Securitization can help solve these problems. Under securitization legislation, a utility usually issues a
bond—often backed by state government—for the amount needed to cover the expenditure. This bond
issuance allows the utility to receive the cash inflow all at once, instead of through the typical process in
rate case proceedings. In a traditional rate case, utilities attempt to recover the costs of asset construction
after the fact, with cost recovery spread over the useful life of the asset. With securitized property, the
utility receives the cash from the bond immediately, along with the right to recover the bond’s principal
and interest from customers.

The cost of securitization is typically lower than the utility’s typical cost of debt. Securitization replaces
the utility’s usual rate of return on capital, including cost of equity, and replaces it with the interest rate of

                                                                                                                REGULATORY SERVICES

                                                                                                                        Exhibit JP-1
                                                                                                                        Page 2 of 7

the bond. The difference between these two can be substantial, with rates of return that can be two to five
times higher than the secured bond’s interest rate. This allows the utility to lower customers’ bills, or the
savings can be reinvested in additional or expanded programs.


Is Securitization the Perfect Solution?

Securitization has its drawbacks. It has seen pushback in legislatures for letting utilities “off the hook”
for bad investments, especially when securitizing fossil-fueled plants. Some investors also do not like to
securitize assets that were previously collecting a substantial return. As Brad Viator, former vice president
of external affairs for the Edison Electric Institute, said, “Securitizing the closure of those facilities
eliminates or reduces the return on equity, meaning the utilities are not making the money they were
promised when they built the facility.”1 It is not a tool that works for all asset recovery situations.

However, when properly used, securitization is a win
for all parties involved. Utility shareholders see an
immediate infusion of cash that will be used over time
for the construction of additional assets and capital
projects, customers see lower bills, and advocates
often see greenlighting of projects that might not have
occurred under the traditional process.


Restructuring: The 90s Securitization Boon

Securitization started being widely used by utilities
in the mid-to-late 1990s, when many states began to
undergo utility restructuring. Under restructuring and
the subsequent introduction of customer choice, electricity prices and demand per generation facility
were expected to fall more than production costs. This lowered the earnings of the generation assets
owned (or previously owned) by the utility and subsequently the assets’ value as well, leading to stranded
assets or unrecoverable costs for the utilities. Many states in the Mid-Atlantic and New England that
restructured in this period enacted legislation allowing utilities to securitize these stranded costs and/or
investments. Between 1996 and 2002, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Michigan, New Hampshire, New Jersey,
Pennsylvania, and Rhode Island all passed legislation to securitize either stranded assets or deferred
supply charges and retired debt and equity due to restructuring.

The results of restructuring did not end at the turn of the millennium. Several states phased in or deferred
changes in energy prices under restructuring, allowing time for a supply market to develop in their regions.
In Maryland, for example, residential customers faced a 72% increase in their electric supply charge in

The Fall of Coal: Securitization in the 2010s

The 2010s saw a dramatic change in the fuel diversity of generating plants in the United States.2 Due to
cheap imports of natural gas and the increased supply from fracking, natural gas prices plummeted. Once
considered one of the more expensive fuel sources, used almost completely for peaking plants, natural gas
quickly became the most viable fuel source to handle base load. This drop in the price of natural gas came
while state and federal legislation enacted stricter emission standards for power plants, which impacted
coal plants the most. As a result, many coal plants throughout the United States were selected for early
retirement. Between 2010 and 2019, seven states (Colorado, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi, Montana, New
Mexico, and Wisconsin) enacted legislation that securitized the retirement and abandonment costs of
these plants, or the costs associated with plant improvements to comply with stricter emission standards.
This legislation lowered the cost to customers of switching to cheaper and cleaner fuel sources.


Storm Recovery: The Early 2020s

A series of weather and other related major events in the
early 2020s saw utilities incur unexpectedly large costs
that required recovery, often in the form of increased rates
to customers. Whether the result of unexpected severe
winter storms in Texas and Oklahoma, wildfires in the
west, or the COVID-19 pandemic, some utilities suddenly
faced billions of dollars of expenditures to cover fuel
purchases, fire mitigation, or storm recovery that needed
to be recovered from customers. While such major event
expenses used to be recovered through special riders using
the utility’s typical cost of debt, the sheer dollar amounts
led many states (including Arizona, Arkansas, California,
Kentucky, Missouri, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Texas, and West Virginia) to enact securitization
legislation. Securitizing these costs spread out the recovery time, mitigating “rate shock” to customers
and alleviating working capital pressure on utility balance sheets to preserve credit ratings. In New York,
to proactively save customers money and decrease the impact of severe weather events, legislation was
enacted to securitize the cost of improving system resiliency.

What Is the Future of Securitization?

Securitization appears to once again be gaining momentum. More than 30 states have enacted
securitization legislation. In 2023 alone, four new states have added or expanded securitization legislation,
often for reasons different from historic trends, and Arizona passed securitization legislation in 2025.
Virginia and West Virginia now allow fuel costs to be recovered through securitization, joining Idaho, which
passed legislation to that effect back in 2001. In Oregon, utilities may now securitize the cost of energy
conservation programs and capital investment projects that will affect the affordability of residential bills.
Indeed, New York’s resiliency legislation in 2022 shows that securitization of new types of projects, assets,
and costs will likely be allowed as time moves on.

This is not to say that securitization will be given free rein. Jurisdictions still see the need for prudence
regarding securitization. For example, the wildfires that devastated the island of Maui in Hawaii in
August 2023 seemed a prime candidate for the use of securitization. To rebuild the destroyed electric
infrastructure, Hawaiian Electric would need a large amount of capital for immediate investment.
Additionally, customers recovering from the fire would benefit from the reduced financing costs offered by
securitization. In January 2024, SB2922 was introduced, a bill that would have allowed recovery of costs
and expenses caused by wildfires through securitization. However, additional amendments to the bill that
would have reorganized Hawaiian Electric, separating its generation and grid functions, along with worries
that this was a bailout for a company that had some liability in the wildfires, ultimately killed the bill.

At MCR, we recommend performing an analysis of securitization legislation to determine if it can be
implemented at your company. Our summary of securitization legislation (below) provides a good starting
point. If securitization is available, it is an important tool for success in moving forward with projects
that have high up-front costs. For utilities in states where securitization is not an option, we recommend
working with your state legislature to educate them on the benefits of securitization of large, unexpected
expenditures to create a win for customers and the utility.

    Securitization typically benefits utilities and their current customers
    UCRC securitization was widely used after the deregulation of the utility sector in the late 1990s as a way to finance so-called stranded
    costs—the shortfall between the market value of utilities’ generation assets and their book value when certain states switched to
    competitive electric supply markets and utilities sold their generation assets. In UCRC securitization, utilities issue bonds with lower
    financing costs that are paid back through a discrete customer charge. We typically view use of the technique as credit positive for
    utilities.

    A utility benefits from the securitization because it receives an immediate source of cash. The ability to use securitization generally
    means the utility is allowed to recover all or most of the costs in question in a timely manner. The ability to use securitization as a tool
    to recover costs related to large or unforeseen developments allows utilities to avoid potentially credit negative events. The utility’s
    ratepayers benefit because customer rates are lower than if the securitization was not utilized and in many cases avert the need for a
    substantial rate increase. Under state legislation, the utility must show that the savings to its customers on a net present value basis
    will be higher than they would have been without securitization.

    The savings result from the cost of the securitized debt being lower than the utility’s unsecuritized cost of debt and much lower than its
    all-in cost of capital, which reduces the revenue requirement associated with the cost recovery. The special surcharges involved are also
    spread out over a long period, typically corresponding to the maturity of the securitization bonds. This eases the impact on customer
    bills when compared with requesting cost recovery from customers through a one-time payment.

    Exhibit 1 shows an illustrative example of the potential impact over time on a utility’s ratio of cash flow from operations pre-working
    capital changes (CFO pre-W/C) to debt, all else being equal. Depending on the size of the securitization debt as a proportion of total
    debt, the impact on a utility's financial metrics can vary. If the securitization is a significant component of total debt then a utility's
    ratio of CFO pre-W/C to debt could be severely negatively affected.

    representation, but in most cases we follow the accounting in audited statements under US Generally Accepted Accounting Principles

    have been required to consolidate securitization debt under GAAP, even though it is technically non-recourse.

        CenterPoint Energy Houston Electric has a long history of issuing securitization bonds
        In 1999, the Texas legislature adopted the Texas Electric Choice Plan, under which integrated utilities operating within the Electric Reliability
        Council of Texas, Inc. (ERCOT, Aa3 stable) were required to unbundle their operations into separate retail sales, power generation, and
        transmission and distribution companies. The legislation provided for a transition period and a true-up mechanism for the utilities to recover
        stranded and certain other costs resulting from the transition. Those costs were recoverable, after approval by the Public Utility Commission of
        Texas (PUCT), either through the issuance of securitization bonds or through the implementation of a competition transition charge as a rider
        to the utility's tariff.

        In the early 2000s, CenterPoint Energy Houston Electric, LLC (CEHE, A3 stable) restructured its business in accordance with the new law and
        its generating stations were sold to third parties. Over the years that followed, CEHE has worked with regulators to obtain recovery of most its
        stranded assets and associated costs through the use of securitization bonds and other regulatory mechanisms.

        In October 2011, PUCT approved a final order that allowed CEHE to recover an additional $1.695 billion of stranded costs through the use
        of securitization bonds. In January 2012, CEHE created a new special purpose subsidiary, CenterPoint Energy Transition Bond Company IV,
        LLC, which issued $1.695 billion of securitization bonds in three tranches with interest rates ranging from 0.9012% to 3.0282% and final
        maturity dates ranging from April 15, 2018 to October 15, 2025. The securitization bonds will be repaid over time through a charge imposed
        on customers in CEHE's service territory.

        The overall time-weighted interest rate of approximately 2.5% for the securitization bonds was substantially lower than the average rate on
        CEHE's unsecuritized debt of about 7.66% at that time. The PUCT estimated that the reduced interest charges from the securitization of the
        stranded costs resulted in savings for CEHE's customers of more than $700 million over the life of the bonds.

    UCRC securitization has become more versatile and widespread
    UCRC bonds were created after the deregulation of utilities in the late 1990s as a way to finance stranded costs. To date, more than
    20 states have used this model to recover not only stranded costs but also costs associated with storm recovery and to a lesser degree
    environmental restoration, utility restructuring, deferred fuel costs and renewable energy projects.

    In June 2005, for example, Section 366.8260 of the Florida Statutes was enacted through Senate Bill 1366, allowing the Florida
    Public Service Commission to authorize the state’s utilities to securitize storm recovery costs. Following Hurricanes Katrina, Rita and
    Wilma in 2005, Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas joined Florida by passing special legislation giving utilities operating in
    their jurisdictions the option of utilizing securitization for recovery of storm costs. Recently in California, legislators are considering
    an amended version of Assembly Bill 33 which, as amended, would allow securitization to be used for prudently incurred costs arising
    from wildfires, a credit positive step for utilities dealing with potentially significant wildfire-related liabilities. Exhibit 3 shows a list of
    securitizations completed by utilities in recent years.

    In each case, with the exception of the Entergy New Orleans LLC's (ENO, Ba1 stable) bond issuance (Aa1 (sf)) in 2015, we rated the
    securitization bonds Aaa (sf) owing to the strength of the state legislation, including the state's non-impairment pledge, the irrevocable
    financing order typically from the state public utility commission, credit enhancement consisting of a statutory uncapped true-up
    adjustment mechanism, the manageable size of the cost recovery charge and the remote likelihood of a successful legal, political or
    regulatory challenge, among other factors.

    The Aa1 (sf) rating on ENO's securitization bond issuance, which is one-notch lower than the typical Aaa (sf) rating, reflects the relative
    small size and concentration of the ratepayer base from whom the storm recovery charge will be collected. The bonds are exposed to
    the risk of declines in the rate payer base in the service area of ENO in case of severe events, such as anther severe hurricane.

    State law and financing order strongly protect the securitization assets
    There are three major components of a UCRC securitization: state legislation, a financing order and a true-up mechanism, as shown in
    Exhibit 4. The securitization law and financing order legally protect the assets backing the bonds.

    Exhibit 4
    UCRC securitization has three major components

                          Financing Order                                                                       True-up Mechanism

    The state legislature typically passes a law authorizing the utility to finance the recovery of certain costs through the issuance of
    securitization bonds. The legislation authorizes the creation of a property right allowing the issuer to collect special charges from
    customers which are used to repay the bonds. Bondholders receive protection through a non-impairment pledge, under which the state
    pledges that it will not take any actions that alter the charges or the law until the bonds have been repaid in full.

    The legislation also mandates an irrevocable financing order, typically issued by the state public utility commission, which means the
    state cannot change or revoke the financing order once it is issued. The order authorizes the transaction servicer, typically the utility, on
    behalf of the issuer of the debt, to charge and collect the special surcharges from the utility’s ratepayer base.

    The securitization law and the financing order mandate a true-up adjustment mechanism under which the servicer must adjust the
    charges at least annually to ensure the collection of adequate funds to provide for timely payments on the securitization bonds. The
    securitization law also establishes the issuer of the debt as a bankruptcy-remote special purpose entity (SPE), and the utility sells the
    securitized asset (the property right) to the SPE via a true sale transaction. The assets are thus legally isolated from the utility. The SPE
    issues the bonds and uses the proceeds to acquire the asset. The SPE then uses the charge collected from the utility’s customers to pay
    debt service until the bonds are repaid in full. The utility receives the proceeds from the bond issuance.

    Too much securitization can also have negative consequences
    While the use of securitization does provide more timely recovery of costs for the utility, there can be some downside. In cases
    where utilities use securitization to recover stranded costs, the mechanism requires utilities to give up the opportunity to include the
    corresponding asset in its rate base as well as the ability to earn a return on that asset. This diminishes the utility's future earnings
    power and cash flow generation.

    A significant amount of securitization debt could represent a substantial portion of the utility's customer bills. This would not only raise
    customer rates but could also prevent regulators from approving rate increases in the future, out of concern that rates are rising too
    much. This could in turn affect the utility's capital investments and the ability to add any such investments to rate base and earn on a
    return on them.

    In addition, since the surcharge on customer bills used to pay off the securitization bonds will typically exist for several years, any new
    customers in the utility's service territory will be subject to this surcharge. As a result, future customers will be paying for costs related
    to historical occurrences, which may deter new commercial and industrial businesses from moving into the service territory if rates
    become less competitive.

    Further, customer rates or cash flow used to service securitization debt is senior and has a higher legal priority to the utility's remaining
    cash flow generation. As such, securitization bondholders would have a senior claim in a liability waterfall during times of financial
    distress. So a significant amount of securitization debt within a capital structure could put secured and unsecured debt holders at risk of
    less than full recovery in a bankruptcy filing.


         Pacific Gas & Electric's securitization during bankruptcy in the early 2000's demonstrates the enforceability and resiliency of the
         legal structure
         In 1997, Pacific Gas & Electric Company (PG&E, A3 negative) issued $2.9 billion of securitization bonds after obtaining approval by the
         California Public Utility Commission to recover stranded asset costs associated with the state's utility deregulation. When PG&E filed for
         bankruptcy on 6 April 2001, both the company and bankruptcy court respected the bankruptcy-remote structure of the securitization that
         the parties had established in order to isolate the assets of PG&E’s securitization from PG&E’s bankruptcy estate. PG&E remained the servicer
         of the transaction and continued to collect and remit the securitization payment. The securitization cash flows were not affected by the
         bankruptcy due to a build-up in the reserve fund and the base level of customer consumption used to calculate the 2001 tariff remained
         relatively stable. For these reasons among others, the Aaa (sf) rating on PG&E’s stranded costs recovery securitization bonds was maintained
         throughout the company's bankruptcy.

         The bankruptcy remoteness of securitization transactions is stronger than that of other, purely corporate asset-backed securities for several
         reasons including the explicit recognition, by state legislation, of the right to collect the special surcharge from customers as well as the first
         lien on the asset that is often granted by statute upon its transfer. The consumption-based fee is imposed on ratepayers and is not dependent
         on a particular electrical supplier. The fee is not affected if the servicer becomes bankrupt. The underlying legislation usually requires that any
         successor to the original utility (due to bankruptcy, reorganization, merger, or acquisition) must satisfy all obligations of the original utility,
         including the collection of the special surcharge. The right to collect the special surcharge is irrevocable and cannot be altered by either the
         state utility commission or the state.

         In January 2005, PG&E issued $1.9 billion of securitization known as energy recovery bonds (ERBs). The securitization financing accelerated the
         company's collection of the regulatory asset that was created as part of PG&E's bankruptcy. A second securitization financing was completed
         in late 2005 which enabled PG&E to largely recover the entire regulatory asset. This was another example where securitization was used as a
         tool to significantly reduce the uncertainty and length of time in the recovery of significant costs, a credit positive, while also reducing costs
         for customers by keeping rates lower over the long-term.

                         II.     SUMMARY OF THE STIPULATION

       The Commission should approve Georgia Power’s FCR-27 Application as modified by the

Stipulation, which is a comprehensive resolution of the issues among the Stipulating Parties in this

proceeding. The constructive Stipulation, which reduces the fuel costs requested in the Company’s

initial filing and provides for the timely recovery of fuel costs, addresses each of the Company’s

requests in this case and resolves all other issues raised by PIA Staff and GAM in response. As

such, the Stipulation is a reasonable resolution of the FCR-27 case and the Commission should

adopt the mutual recommendations contained in the Stipulation.

       First, the Stipulating Parties agreed to a $13 million cumulative reduction of the fuel

balance effective May 31, 2026, to be reflected in fuel rates. (Stipulation, Para. 3.) Second, the

Stipulating Parties agreed that the Commission should make no finding of prudence, imprudence

threshold from $200 million to $300 million while maintaining the current 40% maximum rate

rate adjustment for Commission consideration if the IFR threshold is reached in either direction.

Fourth, the Stipulating Parties agree that the Commission shall further review the methodology by

which the Company credits real time pricing (“RTP”) revenue to the FCR revenues and the

allocations that the Company is making under such methodology in this FCR-27 docket, which

proceeding shall be concluded by December 31, 2026. (Stipulation, Para. 6.)

                                  III.    LEGAL STANDARD

       Under O.C.G.A § 46-2-26, Georgia Power “may change its base rates solely because of

increased or decreased fuel costs only after filing with the [C]ommission an estimate of its fuel

costs and retail sales for the next three consecutive calendar months and proposed base rates to

recover those costs.” O.C.G.A § 46-2-26(c). The Commission shall conduct a hearing on the

Company’s FCR-27 Application “for the purpose of determining its accuracy,” and “the burden of

proof to show that an increased rate, based on fluctuations in fuel costs, is just and reasonable” is

on the utility. Id. § 46-2-26(d). Georgia law allows for a public utility to recover its fuel costs by

adjusting rates based on actual expenses “in order that the accumulated retail fuel costs of the

utility shall equal, as nearly as possible, the revenues recovered pursuant to the fuel recovery

allowance contained in its base rates.” O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(g).

       Disallowances or adjustments for fuel costs shall only be made for “illegal or clearly

imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” Id. § 46-2-26(h). In Docket No. 6739-U, the

Commission articulated its prudence standard as follows:

       “A decision must not be judged as correct or incorrect in the light of perfect
       hindsight. Rather, a decision must be judged as to whether it was reasonable given
       the facts and circumstances which were known or which reasonably should have
       been known at the time the decision was made. In applying this standard, it must
       be recognized that in any decision-making process there may exist a range of
       choices, any, or all of which could have been adopted by reasonable management
       in good faith and under the same set of circumstances. If the Company has made a
       decision which falls within that “zone of reasonableness,” that decision must be
       found to have been prudent, irrespective of whether others may have selected
       another alternative, and irrespective of whether in hindsight another decision may
       now appear in hindsight to have been a more correct decision.”

                                       IV.     ARGUMENT


A.     THE STIPULATION REPRESENTS A FAIR AND REASONABLE RESOLUTION
       OF THIS PROCEEDING AND SHOULD BE ADOPTED BY THE COMMISSION.

1.     The fuel rates requested by Georgia Power and adopted by the Stipulation are reasonable
       and supported by the evidence.

       Georgia Power’s FCR-27 Application proposes to adjust current fuel rates to an appropriate

level to recover the Company’s future fuel costs as projected in the two-year FCR-27 test period

from June 1, 2026, through May 31, 2028. Based on the recovery of previously incurred fuel costs

over the three-year period as approved by the Commission in FCR-26, virtually all of the prior

under recovery is projected to be collected by May 31, 2026, and the Company proposes to recover

the under-recovered balance accrued since the implementation of FCR-26 rates over a three-year

period ending May 31, 2029. No party challenged the reasonableness of the Company’s projections

or the recovery period of prior incurred fuel costs. Consequently, based on average annual fuel

rates, the proposed FCR-27 rate changes, as modified by the Stipulation, are projected to decrease

the monthly bill of a typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month by

approximately $5.84. Contemporaneous with this fuel rate filing, the Company filed a Storm

Damage Recovery Application. As a result of the Stipulation, the net result of the two proceedings

will reduce the bill of the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month

by $4.04.

       The Stipulation provides significant benefits to customers and represents a fair and

reasonable resolution of all issues in this case. There is no evidence in the record to support further

capacity to absorb natural fuel price fluctuations and the normal swings of over- or under-recovery

B.     THE  COMMISSION   SHOULD    REJECT   OTHER    INTERVENOR
       RECOMMENDATIONS INCONSISTENT WITH THE STIPULATION.

       Intervenors advance several recommendations in this proceeding that should not be

adopted by the Commission, including but not limited to the creation of a fuel cost sharing

mechanism and disallowances related to coal unit operation. These recommendations are not

relevant to the reasonableness of the Company’s requested fuel rates, are unnecessary given

existing incentives to lower fuel costs for customers, and should be rejected by the Commission.


1.     A fuel cost sharing mechanism as proposed by NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE is
       unnecessary and, further, inconsistent with Georgia law.

       Intervenors NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE recommend that the Commission implement a

fuel cost sharing mechanism that would require Georgia Power and its shareholders to bear a

portion of the risk associated with its fuel costs and procurement. (See Tr. vol. 1, 679-81.) At the

outset, a fuel cost sharing mechanism is inconsistent with Georgia’s statues and regulations

governing the Company’s fuel cost recovery. In addition, this recommendation is based on the

false assumption that the Company does not voluntarily monitor and seek out ways to reduce costs

for customers. As explained in more detail below, the Commission should reject intervenor

recommendations for the adoption of a fuel cost sharing mechanism. Rather, the Commission

should continue to exercise its appropriate oversight of the Company’s fuel cost recovery process

through the FCR proceedings and the Company’s regular monthly and semi-annual reporting.


       i.      A fuel cost sharing mechanism is inconsistent with Georgia law governing fuel cost
               recovery.

       The fuel cost sharing mechanism proposed by intervenors is inconsistent with Georgia law.

Pursuant to O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26, “[r]ate changes based solely on increases or decreases in the cost

of fuel may be determined as set forth in this Code section.” And “[t]he [C]ommission shall

disallow and make appropriate adjustment for any reported fuel cost that is the result of illegal or

clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” While NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE Witness

Kalin recommends the Commission require the Company in future FCR proceedings to be

responsible for some portion of fuel purchase risks and costs, (Tr. vol. 1, 791-92), such fuel cost

sharing mechanism ignores the plain language of O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h), which contemplates

disallowances of fuel costs by the Commission in only two express situations: illegal or clearly

imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.


       Contrary to the assertions of NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE Witness Kalin, Georgia’s fuel

cost recovery statute cannot and should not be read to include “flexibility” and Commission

discretion not otherwise authorized. Witness Kalin, who is not an attorney licensed in Georgia,

adopts an interpretation of O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(c) that would undermine and contradict the

standard for disallowance of fuel costs provided for in O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h). The fuel cost

recovery statute provides that “each utility shall file with the commission an estimate of fuel costs

and an estimate of retail sales … and propose base rate tariffs to recover those costs.” (O.C.G.A.
                        ________________________________________________
                                   Georgia Power Company’s Brief
                                      GPSC Docket No. 56765
                                            Page 12 of 19

§ 46-2-26 (c) (emphasis added).) The reference to “those” costs are the full costs that the utility

expects to incur – not a subset, fraction, or portion of its fuel costs. The utility estimates its costs

and is permitted to propose rates to adequately recover those costs, full stop. To read into the

statute that the Commission may require Georgia Power to propose rates to recover something less

than all of its projected fuel costs is an overreach of legal interpretation. To require Georgia Power

to propose to recover something less than 100% of its projected fuel costs would make the standard

for disallowance and fuel rate reduction superfluous and unnecessary. (See e.g., McIver v. State,

314 Ga. 109, 119-20 (2022) (noting that it is a fundamental rule of statutory construction “to

construe the statute according to its terms, to give words their plain and ordinary meaning, and to

avoid a construction that makes some language mere surplusage.”) (emphasis added).) The

Georgia Legislature meant what it said when it adopted O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26 (h), which provides

that “[t]he Commission shall disallow and make appropriate adjustment for any reported fuel cost

that is the result of illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” To simply ignore

the express requirement to prove illegal or clearly imprudent conduct of the utility prior to

summarily disallowing a portion of fuel costs necessary to reliably serve customers is an absurd

outcome that ignores the plain text of the statute. For this reason alone, the Commission should

disregard NRDC/Sierra Club/SACE’s proposed fuel cost sharing mechanism as inconsistent with

Georgia Law.

out opportunities to lower and optimize fuel costs for customers and to contract for fuel in the most

efficient manner possible. This is incumbent upon the Company, and a responsibility that Georgia

The constructive Stipulation, which reduces the fuel costs requested in the Company’s initial filing and provides for the timely recovery of fuel costs, addresses each of the Company’s requests in this case and resolves all other issues raised by PIA Staff and GAM in response. As such, the Stipulation is a reasonable resolution of the FCR-27 case and the Commission should adopt the mutual recommendations contained in the Stipulation.

LEGAL STANDARD
Under O.C.G.A § 46-2-26, Georgia Power “may change its base rates solely because of increased or decreased fuel costs only after filing with the [C]ommission an estimate of its fuel costs and retail sales for the next three consecutive calendar months and proposed base rates to recover those costs.” O.C.G.A § 46-2-26(c). The Commission shall conduct a hearing on the Company’s FCR-27 Application “for the purpose of determining its accuracy,” and “the burden of proof to show that an increased rate, based on fluctuations in fuel costs, is just and reasonable” is on the utility. Id. § 46-2-26(d). Georgia law allows for a public utility to recover its fuel costs by adjusting rates based on actual expenses “in order that the accumulated retail fuel costs of the utility shall equal, as nearly as possible, the revenues recovered pursuant to the fuel recovery allowance contained in its base rates.” O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(g).
Disallowances or adjustments for fuel costs shall only be made for “illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” Id. § 46-2-26(h). In Docket No. 6739-U, the Commission articulated its prudence standard as follows:
“A decision must not be judged as correct or incorrect in the light of perfect hindsight. Rather, a decision must be judged as to whether it was reasonable given the facts and circumstances which were known or which reasonably should have been known at the time the decision was made. In applying this standard, it must be recognized that in any decision-making process there may exist a range of choices, any, or all of which could have been adopted by reasonable management in good faith and under the same set of circumstances. If the Company has made a decision which falls within that “zone of reasonableness,” that decision must be found to have been prudent, irrespective of whether others may have selected another alternative, and irrespective of whether in hindsight another decision may now appear in hindsight to have been a more correct decision.”
See Order, GPSC Docket No. 6739-U: Georgia Public Service Commission Staff’s Review of Georgia Power Company’s Rocky Mountain Pumped Storage Facility (Jan. 14, 1998).

No party challenged the reasonableness of the Company’s projections or the recovery period of prior incurred fuel costs. Contemporaneous with this fuel rate filing, the Company filed a Storm Damage Recovery Application. As a result of the Stipulation, the net result of the two proceedings will reduce the bill of the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month by $4.04. 
The Stipulation provides significant benefits to customers and represents a fair and reasonable resolution of all issues in this case. There is no evidence in the record to support further reduction in the Company’s request beyond the terms of the Stipulation. As noted above, Georgia Power may recover its fuel costs by adjusting rates based on actual expenses so “that the accumulated retail fuel costs of the utility shall equal, as nearly as possible, the revenues recovered pursuant to the fuel recovery allowance contained in its base rates.” O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(g). The fuel recovery statute sets a high bar for the disallowance of reported fuel costs, requiring evidence of “illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility” for any such adjustments to be made. The +/- $300 million threshold provides a more appropriate balance between the frequency of the IFR trigger of potential rate adjustments and mitigating significant over- or under-recovery balances in the future. Witness Lin’s recommendation is unduly restrictive and would impose unnecessary limitations on the Company and the Commission’s authority. Accordingly, the changes to the IFR mechanism included in the Stipulation will provide the Company with increased flexibility to manage over- and under-recovered balances while also balancing the timely recovery of such balances. B. THE COMMISSION SHOULD REJECT OTHER INTERVENOR RECOMMENDATIONS INCONSISTENT WITH THE STIPULATION.
Intervenors advance several recommendations in this proceeding that should not be adopted by the Commission, including but not limited to the creation of a fuel cost sharing mechanism and disallowances related to coal unit operation. These recommendations are not relevant to the reasonableness of the Company’s requested fuel rates, are unnecessary given existing incentives to lower fuel costs for customers, and should be rejected by the Commission.
A fuel cost sharing mechanism as proposed by NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE is unnecessary and, further, inconsistent with Georgia law.
Intervenors NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE recommend that the Commission implement a fuel cost sharing mechanism that would require Georgia Power and its shareholders to bear a portion of the risk associated with its fuel costs and procurement. (See Tr. vol. 1, 679-81.) At the outset, a fuel cost sharing mechanism is inconsistent with Georgia’s statues and regulations governing the Company’s fuel cost recovery. In addition, this recommendation is based on the false assumption that the Company does not voluntarily monitor and seek out ways to reduce costs for customers. As explained in more detail below, the Commission should reject intervenor recommendations for the adoption of a fuel cost sharing mechanism. Rather, the Commission should continue to exercise its appropriate oversight of the Company’s fuel cost recovery process through the FCR proceedings and the Company’s regular monthly and semi-annual reporting. 
A fuel cost sharing mechanism is inconsistent with Georgia law governing fuel cost recovery. 
The fuel cost sharing mechanism proposed by intervenors is inconsistent with Georgia law. Pursuant to O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26, “[r]ate changes based solely on increases or decreases in the cost of fuel may be determined as set forth in this Code section.” And “[t]he [C]ommission shall disallow and make appropriate adjustment for any reported fuel cost that is the result of illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” While NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE Witness Kalin recommends the Commission require the Company in future FCR proceedings to be responsible for some portion of fuel purchase risks and costs, (Tr. vol. 1, 791-92), such fuel cost sharing mechanism ignores the plain language of O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h), which contemplates disallowances of fuel costs by the Commission in only two express situations: illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility. 
Contrary to the assertions of NRDC, Sierra Club, and SACE Witness Kalin, Georgia’s fuel cost recovery statute cannot and should not be read to include “flexibility” and Commission discretion not otherwise authorized. Witness Kalin, who is not an attorney licensed in Georgia, adopts an interpretation of O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(c) that would undermine and contradict the standard for disallowance of fuel costs provided for in O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h). The fuel cost recovery statute provides that “each utility shall file with the commission an estimate of fuel costs and an estimate of retail sales … and propose base rate tariffs to recover those costs.” (O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26 (c) (emphasis added).) The reference to “those” costs are the full costs that the utility expects to incur – not a subset, fraction, or portion of its fuel costs. The utility estimates its costs and is permitted to propose rates to adequately recover those costs, full stop. To read into the statute that the Commission may require Georgia Power to propose rates to recover something less than all of its projected fuel costs is an overreach of legal interpretation. To require Georgia Power to propose to recover something less than 100% of its projected fuel costs would make the standard for disallowance and fuel rate reduction superfluous and unnecessary. (See e.g., McIver v. State, 314 Ga. 109, 119-20 (2022) (noting that it is a fundamental rule of statutory construction “to construe the statute according to its terms, to give words their plain and ordinary meaning, and to avoid a construction that makes some language mere surplusage.”) (emphasis added).) The Georgia Legislature meant what it said when it adopted O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26 (h), which provides that “[t]he Commission shall disallow and make appropriate adjustment for any reported fuel cost that is the result of illegal or clearly imprudent conduct on the part of the utility.” To simply ignore the express requirement to prove illegal or clearly imprudent conduct of the utility prior to summarily disallowing a portion of fuel costs necessary to reliably serve customers is an absurd outcome that ignores the plain text of the statute. For this reason alone, the Commission should disregard NRDC/Sierra Club/SACE’s proposed fuel cost sharing mechanism as inconsistent with Georgia Law.
Georgia Power continuously evaluates ways to reduce and control fuel costs for the benefit of customers. 
As a secondary matter legally, but of primary importance, Georgia Power regularly seeks out opportunities to lower and optimize fuel costs for customers and to contract for fuel in the most efficient manner possible. This is incumbent upon the Company, and a responsibility that Georgia Power takes seriously as part of the regulatory compact. For example, the Company has a demonstrated record of proactively negotiating fuel transportation contracts to reduce fuel and production costs for customers. At rail captive generating facilities, like Plants Bowen and Scherer, where no direct rail delivery competition exists, the Company was able to negotiate multi-year transportation contracts with the railroads to lower prices for scheduled deliveries to the Company’s plants. (Tr. vol. 2, 896.) Recently, for one such contract, the Company negotiated transportation terms that resulted in material and demonstrable production cost savings over the 2025–2029 period, with the latest Company projections indicating even higher level of customer savings from the lower negotiated transportation rates over time compared to the initial estimate based on the system needs and operating conditions. (Id.) The Company negotiated comparable transportation arrangements during the 2024–2026 period that similarly delivered material transportation cost reductions, reinforcing the Company’s demonstrated ability to secure customer benefits through disciplined commercial negotiations. (Id.) Even without a fuel cost sharing incentive, 100% of these cost savings are passed directly onto customers.
The Company also seeks to control and reduce fuel costs through improved operation of its generating units. Energy Information Administration (“EIA”) data shows that Georgia’s coal and gas plants are among the most efficient in the country, and the Company has implemented a standardized heat rate program that directly links plant operational discipline and maintenance practices to measurable efficiency outcomes. This operational discipline is reinforced through centralized economic dispatch that commits and dispatches generating resources to minimize total system production cost, subject to reliability, environmental, and operational constraints. Thus, all else equal, units with better heat rate performance tend to be dispatched more frequently under this framework, while less efficient units tend to be utilized less. (Tr. vol. 2, 897.) This results in fuel cost savings for customers. (Id.) Because this existing framework already drives operator behavior, prioritizes efficiency improvements, and oversees heat rate performance through objective measurement and review rather than cost redistribution, the implementation of a separate fuel cost sharing mechanism would be duplicative and unnecessary.
These examples, among others, demonstrate some of the ways in which Georgia Power takes its responsibility for prudent fuel management seriously and is constantly looking to optimize and lower fuel costs for customers whenever possible. Finally, intervenors that advocate for fuel cost sharing have not provided sufficient evidence in their testimonies that the Company took specific imprudent actions that resulted in increased fuel costs. The current FCR mechanism ensures that the customers only pay for actual fuel costs incurred and there is no return earned on fuel costs. Therefore, any additional incentive to encourage the Company to lower fuel costs for customers is unnecessary.
Georgia Power prudently manages the dispatch of its coal generating fleet.
NRDC/Sierra Club/SACE Witness Goggin argues that the Commission should disallow $152 million in fuel costs as a result of alleged uneconomic coal unit operation, an assertion that he bases—by his own admission—on an incomplete and inaccurate analysis of the economics of Georgia Power’s coal unit dispatch. (Tr. vol. 1, 834-37.) Witness Goggin takes an unnecessarily strict and narrow view of what counts as “economic dispatch,” creating a bespoke methodology that ignores numerous facts and factors, to generate a proposed disallowance drastically out of proportion with the reasonableness with which Georgia Power operates its generation fleet. Accordingly, the Commission should disregard Witness Goggin’s recommendation that the Commission disallow $152 million in fuel costs due to alleged uneconomic dispatch. 
To start, Mr. Goggin’s counterfactual analysis is based on incomplete data, which he admitted during cross examination. (Id. at 834.) Instead, Mr. Goggin’s analysis compares a unit’s committed and dispatched costs to a historical day-ahead lambda price, which gives limited consideration to a single unit operating constraint—minimum up times—while ignoring several constraints that impact the operation of the units and the system as a whole. For example, this analysis failed to account for the minimum downtime requirements for each unit as reflected in Aurora inputs for Plants Bowen and Scherer. (Id. at 833; Tr. vol. 2, 919.) Minimum downtimes are important to consider because they reflect unit commitment risks, such as natural gas price, load volatility, and other ever-changing system conditions, over a week-long time period, compared to the day-ahead approach used in Mr. Goggin’s analysis. That longer planning horizon requires managing operational and market risk over multiple days—a key consideration absent in Mr. Goggin’s analysis. Moreover, although Mr. Goggin claims to have considered minimum downtime in his analysis, cross examination revealed that he either failed to consider that factor altogether or used incorrect assumptions in reaching his faulty conclusions. (Tr. vol. 2, 833-36.)
Mr. Goggin’s analysis also fails to account for how removing large generators from the system, like during an outage or as a result of transmission constraints, would change overall system production costs and marginal costs, and does not account for the resulting impacts to system reserves. The Company performs a production cost optimization subject to all applicable system and unit constraints to determine which units should be online and when. Using historical day-ahead lambda, as Witness Goggin does, to determine the impact on fuel costs is inappropriate without considering all the possible reasons the referenced plants may or may not have operated. This potential outcome could create additional constraints in the system and, in turn, drive up system lambda. In addition, there are operating reserve considerations that must be considered in the economic dispatch of the units. A committed Plant Bowen or Plant Scherer unit provides regulating, contingency, and operating reserves to the system as a benefit, which is captured in the production cost model, but not the dispatch price that Witness Goggin relied upon. 
Throughout the hearing, the Company reiterated its commitment to operating its system to ensure reliable and cost-effective generation when customers need it most. (Tr. vol. 2, 901, 932, 1017.) As Company Witness Ohlson testified, economics are clearly part of the Company’s goal, but reliability, system, and unit constraints can present real challenges that are not reflected when looking at strict energy merit order or the dispatch cost of a plant. (Id. at 929-30.) What the Company made clear, however, is that the electrical system has stability and reliability needs that are continuously considered by the Company, along with all other operating constraints, to provide reliable and safe electric service. (Id. at 920.) As established, Mr. Goggin’s analysis does not show true, uneconomic dispatch, (id. at 918), and fails to account for meaningful operational constraints among other flaws. It further fails to support a finding of clear imprudence—as required by O.C.G.A. § 46-2-26(h)—which Witness Goggin conceded he did not address during cross examination by counsel for the Company. (Tr. vol. 1, 821-22.) The Commission should reject this recommendation.

However, the fact that the Company may not respond to a particular position of Staff or an Intervenor should not be construed as acceptance of such position.” (Tr. vol. 2, 866-67.) Like our testimony, Georgia Power focused its discussion in this brief on the core issues in dispute in this proceeding, rather than devoting unnecessary time and resources to refute positions that are clearly wrong or simply irrelevant to the issues presented in this docket. (Id.) Moreover, even if Georgia Power had not expressly reserved this position, the Commission, PIA Staff, and Intervenors should not conclude that something was agreed to by the Company merely because it was not specifically addressed.
CONCLUSION
The Stipulation, reached as a compromise between the Company, PIA Staff, and GAM, provides significant benefits to the Company’s customers and is a fair and reasonable resolution of the issues in this case. As explained above, additional recommendations provided by other intervenors are unnecessary or duplicative in light of the Commission’s existing, robust oversight of the Company’s fuel costs. For the reasons set forth herein, the Stipulation is in the best interest of customers and should be approved without modification.

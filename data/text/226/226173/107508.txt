III.PRUDENCE STANDARD

Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE STANDARD OF PRUDENCE THAT YOU APPLIED IN YOUR REVIEW OF THE PLANT HATCH AND PLANT VOGTLE OUTAGES.
A.I have applied the standard of prudence adopted by the Georgia Public Service Commission in Docket No. 6739-U, Review of Georgia Power Co.’s Rocky Mountain Pumped Storage Facility.  The standard articulated by the Commission in the Rocky Mountain proceeding is as follows:
“A decision must not be judged as correct or incorrect in the light of perfect hindsight.  Rather, a decision must be judged as to whether it was reasonable given the facts and circumstances which were known or which reasonably should have been known at the time the decision was made.  In applying this standard, it must be recognized that in any decision-making process there may exist a range of choices, any, or all of which could have been adopted by reasonable management in good faith and under the same set of circumstances.  If the Company has made a decision which falls within that “zone of reasonableness,” that decision must be found to have been prudent, irrespective of whether others may have selected another alternative, and irrespective of whether in hindsight another decision may now appear in hindsight to have been a more correct decision.”

This standard is essentially a “reasonable man standard” in that the utility’s actions or decisions are compared to the reasonable actions or decisions of a qualified and experienced utility manager or operator given what was known, or reasonably should have been known, at the time without benefit of hindsight.  Thus, the actions and decisions of Company personnel must be judged on what they knew, or reasonably should have known, at the time the action was taken, or the decision was made without benefit of hindsight.  The “reasonable man standard” does not hold a Company responsible for actions or decisions that may not have been appropriate had additional information been available at the time certain actions or decisions were taken. I have applied this standard in my evaluation of the Plant Hatch and Plant Vogtle outages discussed below.


Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE IN MORE DETAIL THE APPLICATION OF THIS STANDARD TO THE PRUDENT MAINTENANCE AND OPERATION OF PLANT HATCH AND PLANT VOGTLE.  
A.Prudent maintenance and operation of Plants Hatch and Vogtle is the responsibility of Company management. This requires that Company management establish the proper environment and culture as well as provide effective programs and resources for plant personnel.  Management must establish high expectations for plant personnel and equipment and is responsible for plant personnel and equipment performing at a high standard.  The appropriate environment and culture would include:
Ensuring a safety-first priority;
Providing adequate plant procedures;
Maintaining a questioning attitude;
Avoiding complacency;
Identifying the risk associated with plant activities;
Ensuring that plant equipment is maintained in good operating condition;
Correcting known problems in a timely manner; and
Requiring proper operating practices as described below.

In addition, management must ensure that the programs and procedures in use are effective.  An effective corrective action program is essential in safe and prudent plant operation.  This program defines the methods used in determining the cause of plant events and ensuring that they are not repeated.  The corrective action program must ensure that:
Root cause analyses are effective in determining the actual cause of the event or problem.
Root cause analyses are sufficiently broad to identify any generic implications of the event.
Corrective actions are effective in preventing a reoccurrence of the problem.
Plant personnel share the responsibility for operating the plant in a reasonable and prudent manner in their decisions and actions.  They must utilize the good operating practices and techniques required by plant procedures and plant management.  These practices include:
Proper use of plant procedures;
Adequate briefing of plant personnel;
Proper communications techniques;
Proper use of error detection practices such as self-verification, independent verification, and peer checking. 
Safely stopping nuclear plant operation if anything unusual occurs.

Q.WHAT GENERAL CRITERIA CAN BE USED TO IDENTIFY IMPRUDENT OPERATION OF A NUCLEAR POWER PLANT?
A.One must apply the definition of prudent operation, which compares operation of the plant to operation by a reasonable and experienced plant management and staff given what was known or should have been known at the time.  Applying this definition requires specialized expertise and experience in nuclear plant operation.  However, the response to the questions below can be useful in making a prudence determination.

Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE FACTORS YOU CONSIDER IN THE EVALUATION OF PRUDENCE IN NUCLEAR PLANT OUTAGES.
A.A nuclear plant forced outage is rarely the result of one simple mistake.  There are often precursor events and other opportunities for an outage to be avoided by prudent management and plant operation.  The following are factors that should be evaluated in determining if an outage is prudently incurred or the result of clear imprudence:
Have there been similar or related events in the past?
Were prior events entered into the corrective action program as required?
Are there indications of problems with the corrective action program or in problem identification and resolution?
Was the review of similar or related events broad enough to identify the potential for the event that caused the outage or was the review too narrowly focused?
Were the corrective actions by Company management and operators thorough and effective in preventing recurrence?
Was this a longstanding problem that had not been corrected?
Was the review of prior events by Company management of sufficient depth and rigor to identify and resolve the problem?
Could the use of Operating Experience have identified the problem and avoided or mitigated the outage?
If the event involves an operator error:
Are there indications of human performance problems?
Was the operator properly trained?
Did the operator have the necessary education, experience, and training to successfully perform the work?
Were the procedures correct and adequate?
Were the procedures followed?
Would the procedures result in proper and prudent operation?
Were operating techniques to minimize human performance errors employed effectively?
Were there other human factors involved that should have been identified?
If the event involved equipment failure:
Have there been similar equipment failures in the past?
Were the corrective actions adequate and effective?
Did management take steps to prevent or mitigate subsequent equipment failures?
Was prior maintenance performed in a timely manner?
Was preventive maintenance adequate?
Were there prior indications of equipment problems before the event?
Was there Operating Experience available that should have alerted the operator to the potential problem?
If the event involved an engineering calculation or analysis:
Have problems been identified with the adequacy or rigor of the engineering calculations or analyses?
Did the personnel performing the engineering analysis have the necessary education, training, and experience to perform the analysis in a prudent manner? 
Was the root cause determination appropriate?
Was the engineering analysis given sufficient time for completion without undue schedule pressure?
Were proper engineering procedures followed?
Was the engineering calculation or analysis adequately reviewed and checked to confirm it was complete, correct, and accurate? 
Were there human performance issues?
Was Operating Experience used effectively?

Q.IN THE CASE OF FUEL RECOVERY COSTS, THE REQUIRED STANDARD IS THAT THE OUTAGES MUST BE “CLEARLY IMPRUDENT” FOR THE ADDITIONAL FUEL COSTS TO BE DISALLOWED.  PLEASE DESCRIBE YOUR UNDERSTANDING OF WHAT IS MEANT BY “CLEARLY IMPRUDENT.”
A.I have been advised by Staff counsel that any determination of whether conduct by the Company was “clearly imprudent” is ultimately made by the Commission. A witness is responsible for supporting his conclusion that conduct was imprudent. In my testimony, I provide such support for my conclusion that certain conduct by the Company was imprudent, applying the standard described above. After a witness testifies that certain conduct was imprudent, it is up to the Commission to decide whether such imprudent conduct was “clearly imprudent”, using whatever standard the Commission deems appropriate. For example, the Commission could apply such commonly accepted standards as “clear and convincing”, “preponderance of the evidence”, or “beyond a reasonable doubt” in determining whether imprudent conduct was also clearly imprudent. While I understand that the role of a witness is to support a conclusion that conduct was imprudent, allowing the Commission to apply whatever standard it deems appropriate, the Commission may desire that a witness recommend a standard to be applied by the Commission when reviewing acts of imprudence. In my opinion, in determining whether particular imprudent conduct is also clearly imprudent, the Commission should apply a “clear and convincing evidence” level of scrutiny. The “clear and convincing evidence” test is a medium level of scrutiny which is a more rigorous standard to meet than the preponderance of the evidence standard, but a less rigorous standard to meet than proving evidence beyond a reasonable doubt. In order to meet the standard and prove something by clear and convincing evidence, a party must prove that it is substantially more likely than not that it is true. 
Applying this standard, it is my opinion that each of the outages described in this testimony were the result of clear imprudence. That is, it is substantially more likely than not that the outages described in this testimony were clearly imprudent. 

Q.PLEASE PROVIDE AN EXAMPLE OF HOW YOU WOULD APPLY THE “CLEARLY IMPRUDENT” STANDARD.
A.Assume an outage is caused by a plant operator placing a valve in the wrong position.  As discussed above, I examine the situation to include the following questions.  Was the operator properly trained to conduct this task?  Was the operator following a procedure properly?  Was the procedure correct?  Was the pre-job brief adequate?  Was the operator using error prevention techniques to prevent mistakes? Did the operator have a history of mistakes?  If the answers to these questions indicate that this was a one-time mistake by the plant operator, I would likely conclude that the operator was imprudent in that he did not take adequate care in conducting this activity. When I discover a pattern of similar events, and actions to prevent this type of event were inadequate or not timely, I would likely conclude this outage to be clearly imprudent.  Similarly, if a plant has experienced repeated failures of a plant component that led to an outage and has not identified and implemented effective corrective actions to prevent recurrence of the failure in a timely manner, I likely would conclude that this situation is clearly imprudent.  Thus, a single, one-time event may not be clearly imprudent depending on the circumstances, but an outage that indicates a negative pattern or trend and a failure of corrective actions to prevent recurrence of the event is clearly imprudent.

Summary

Joseph R. Reynolds, PE. is internationally recognized as a forensic engineer and an authority regarding the investigation and evaluation of utility system incidents, including power generation and distribution equipment failures as well as electrical contact accidents, fires and explosions. He has served as the team leader and technical director, as well as principal testifier, in large complex investigations and research. Services to the industry have included: conducting forensic root cause analyses of major power generation, transmission, distribution, telecommunications, system failures, accidents, fires, and equipment condition and performance audit issues; providing recommendations for improved equipment reliability; assessing the expected life and condition of aging equipment for repair/replace options; and addressing prudence issues related to rate base and regulatory hearings.

Mr. Reynolds is the founder and president of RTI Group, LLC based in the US, as well as the founder and chairman of the London based UK firm Reynolds Technological Inquiries Limited. The RTI companies are an international consultancy of experienced expert witnesses supported by a accredited and certified ISO/IEC 17025:2017 independent testing laboratory located in its Maryland Research Centre to determine the cause of electrical, material, and mechanical failures. An advance multimedia studio provides the visuals to effectively present key expert findings. A highly diversified firm, RTI specializes in accident and failure analysis of various size matters involving aviation, marine, rail, telecommunications, power utilities, and high risk industries.

RTI also provides proactive consulting services from Safety Management Systems (SMS) to  Security Risk Assessments.


Mr. Reynolds has represented public and government utilities, state regulatory commissions, and OEM insurers in numerous electric power matters, including the following examples:

Appointed by a utility regulator to serve as lead investigator and team manager for the state’s official investigation and analysis of a high-profile utility service outage resulting in recommendations to improve system reliability, which were adopted through the legislative rule-making process.

Investigated regional blackouts as well as numerous power plant issues.

Provided testimony at rate base hearings before state public service commissions involving the utility’s handling of unplanned plant outages, and evaluation of the resulting repair costs.

Authored several publications on behalf of the Electric Power Research Institute, EPRI, including a guideline to assist utilities in improving the reliability of power plant electrical equipment as an alternative to the cost of constructing new plants.

Author of EPRI guide to the Forensic Investigation of Distribution Systems Accidents. Provided member utilities training over 2 years based on this report. 

Evaluated the options to restore the performance of a damaged hydroelectric generator station when compared with the availability of replacement power.

Mr. Reynolds has given expert testimony in arbitration hearings and courts of law, and has appeared before state and federal boards, commissions, counsels, press conferences, and international professional forums.

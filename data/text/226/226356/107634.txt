II.INTERIM FUEL RIDER
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION TO MAINTAIN THE IFR AT ITS CURRENT THRESHOLD AND VARIANCE AMOUNT?
A.No. For context, the $200 million threshold was established and approved by the Commission in 2012 in the FCR-23 proceeding. The increase in the IFR threshold from the current amount of $200 million to $300 million is more appropriate given the relative size of the total fuel expenses incurred and to be incurred by the Company on an aggregate basis. The higher threshold provides greater rate stability for customers and allows increased capacity to absorb natural fuel price fluctuations and the normal swings of over- or under-recovery balances on a monthly basis. Further, the increase to a $300 million threshold provides a more appropriate balance between the frequency of the IFR trigger of potential rate adjustments and mitigating significant over- or under-recovery balances in the future.
Q.HOW WOULD THIS CHANGE TO THE IFR MECHANISM BE APPLIED?
A.Consistent with the currently approved IFR mechanism, the $300 million threshold will be applied in either direction of over-recovery or under-recovery with the current maximum percentage of +/- 40% of cumulative rate change unaltered from the current approved level. The Company proposes that the timing and the filing requirements remain the same. The increase in threshold will provide greater rate stability for customers and will allow increased flexibility to absorb natural fuel price fluctuations and normal swings of over- or under-recovery balances on a monthly basis. 
Q.SHOULD THE COMPANY BE REQUIRED TO FILE FOR A RATE ADJUSTMENT IN AN IFR FILING?
A.No. There are numerous factors when considering whether a rate adjustment is needed in an IFR filing, which include but are not limited to, the projections of fuel over- or under-recovery balance, the latest fuel price forecast specifically for natural gas, incremental time required to implement the rate change, and the proximity of the next fuel rate case filing. Given those factors, the flexibility of whether to propose a rate adjustment is an important consideration with respect to rate stability for customers. As such, the Company and the Commission should maintain the current optionality afforded within the IFR mechanism to implement or forego a rate adjustment.
Q.SHOULD THE CURRENT IFR THRESHOLD BE RETAINED TO MAINTAIN FREQUENCY OF REPORTING FCR OVER/UNDER STATUS AS RECOMMENDED BY GIPL AND SOUTHFACE?
A.No. The Company files an update of its over/under status of the FCR balance with the Commission in Docket No. 3382-U as part of its monthly reporting requirement. Therefore, even without the trigger of the IFR filing, the FCR over/under status and the associated details are publicly available to be tracked and reviewed on a monthly basis. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH GIPL AND SOUTHFACE’S RECOMMENDATION TO LOWER THE MAXIMUM IFR RATE ADJUSTMENT WITHIN A FCR PERIOD FROM 40% TO 15%?
A.No. As evidenced in the prior periods when the IFR was set at the maximum percentage of 15%, the potential rate adjustment at that level may not adequately result in the desired impact to bring the cumulative FCR balance to a zero position, which is the primary goal of the IFR.
III.COAL PROCUREMENT, INVENTORY MANAGEMENT, AND ECONOMIC DISPATCH OF COAL UNITS 
Q.PLEASE PROVIDE AN OVERVIEW OF GEORGIA POWER’S COAL PROCUREMENT STRATEGY.
A.Georgia Power’s coal procurement strategy is designed to ensure reliable supply for generation while meeting system fuel requirements at the lowest reasonable total cost to customers. Procurement decisions are made using a disciplined portfolio approach that balances: (1) reliability of supply and delivery; (2) total delivered cost; (3) operational flexibility to respond to changing dispatch and burn requirements; and (4) risk management to address market volatility, supplier performance, and evolving environmental requirements.
Reliability first, verified through performance history and due diligence: Suppliers are evaluated economically and through technical and financial reviews; contract terms may include replacement-cost remedies and performance incentives, where appropriate.
Portfolio contracting to manage volume uncertainty: A portion of projected requirements is secured under long-term agreements to support dependable supply and mitigate price volatility, with remaining needs met through spot purchases and negotiated volume flexibility to adjust to changing dispatch and burn requirements.
Active price management: Procurement maintains competitive pricing and limits exposure to market volatility through regular, formal, staggered solicitations. Awards are structured with competitive initial pricing, controlled escalation, and staggered expirations to avoid concentrated purchasing or renewal activity that could increase total delivered costs.
Diversity of supply sources and transportation options: Maintaining optionality across producing regions and transportation routes supports competition, mitigates supply disruptions, and allows the system to adapt as market and regulatory conditions change.
Environmental and regulatory risk mitigation: Procurement terms for longer-dated commitments may include environmental provisions intended to preserve flexibility to respond to current and future compliance requirements and to manage the risk of changing rules affecting unit operations.
Overall, the Company’s integrated approach to coal-related procurement activities is planned and executed to support dependable fuel availability and prudent cost outcomes for customers under a range of market and operating conditions.
Q.WHAT DOES THE COMPANY CONSIDER WHEN NEGOTIATING AND ENTERING INTO COAL SUPPLY AND TRANSPORTATION CONTRACTS?
A.When negotiating coal supply and transportation contracts, the Company considers multiple factors, with the objective of meeting system fuel requirements at the lowest reasonable total cost while supporting safe and reliable generation. Some of these key considerations include:
Fuel reliability and diversity to ensure dependable supply under varying market, operational, and regulatory conditions;
Total delivered cost, including commodity price, transportation, handling, and environmental compliance costs, rather than focusing solely on Free on Board (“FOB”) pricing;
Volume and contract term flexibility, recognizing uncertainty in future dispatch, load, plant operations, and regulatory outcomes;
Alignment between supply and transportation agreements to avoid mismatched commitments and unnecessary cost exposure; and
Risk allocation structured to balance performance incentives, cost predictability, and protection against unbounded exposure from fuel unavailability or market volatility.
The Company evaluates these factors holistically and through competitive processes with appropriate cross‑functional input to ensure procurement decisions are prudent, balanced, and consistent with Commission‑approved strategies and system reliability obligations.
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO NRDC, SIERRA CLUB, AND SACE WITNESS GOGGIN’S RECOMMENDATION THAT THE COMPANY SHOULD NOT ENTER INTO COAL SUPPLY OR TRANSPORTATION CONTRACTS THAT INCLUDE PENALTIES FOR FAILING TO MEET MINIMUM FUEL DELIVERY REQUIREMENTS.
A. Mr. Goggin’s recommendation ignores the reality that coal suppliers and transportation providers typically require minimum delivery provisions in their contracts to secure commitments. The inclusion of minimum delivery requirement provisions is industry standard. Minimum delivery requirements and related performance provisions are a risk‑management mechanism designed to reduce exposure to the much larger and less controllable costs associated with fuel unavailability, including emergency procurement and higher‑cost replacement fuels. These provisions are not intended to compensate non‑performance; they are structured to allocate performance risk to the carrier that controls execution. 
Where applied, such provisions are bounded, transparent, and paired with other customer protections, including force majeure protections, performance standards, and remedies for repeated failure. Eliminating minimum delivery requirements, if even desirable, does not remove the underlying cost of securing transportation capacity. Rather, it shifts those costs into higher base rates or fixed charges with less direct correlation to performance. Accordingly, the Company’s focus in contracting should remain on whether the overall contract structure reasonably minimizes total cost while meeting reliability obligations—not on the presence or absence of minimum delivery requirements in isolation.
Q.PIA STAFF TESTIFIES THAT THE COMPANY ENTERED INTO SUPPLY CONTRACTS FOR COAL THAT WERE HIGHER THAN COAL AVAILABLE FROM OTHER SUPPLIERS. CAN YOU ELABORATE? 
A.The Company continues to pursue a diversified coal procurement strategy for Plant Bowen. While traditional Illinois Basin and Northern Appalachian (“NAPP”) coals—generally the most economic sources—are expected to comprise a substantial majority of Plant Bowen’s coal portfolio going forward, the Company incorporates other sources in its portfolio to enhance supply diversity and reliability. 
The Company runs competitive solicitations for coal supply and uses price and non-price factors, including supplier performance history, production risk, transportation or rail risk, and the operational impacts of short falls, in making its supplier selections. Although lower-priced tons were available from certain Illinois Basin and NAPP suppliers during the relevant solicitations, increasing volumes within the same region or among a small number of large suppliers would have further concentrated Bowen’s supply and transportation exposure. Accordingly, the Company limited the higher-priced volumes to a modest share of Plant Bowen’s projected needs (less than 10% in the relevant years) and used those tons to mitigate supplier risk within the Bowen portfolio rather than as a replacement for the core Illinois Basin and NAPP supply. Including a historically reliable supplier from the Central Appalachian region enhances supplier/mine diversity, adds geographic diversity, avoids congestion along certain transportation corridors, and provides scalable capacity that can help mitigate the operational and market impacts of unplanned production issues at other mines as seen in recent years.  
Q.PLEASE PROVIDE AN OVERVIEW OF GEORGIA POWER’S INVENTORY MANAGEMENT STRATEGY.
The Company’s coal inventory management strategy is governed by SCS’ Fossil Fuel Policy and is designed to ensure system reliability while optimizing supply chain performance by balancing on‑site inventories and planned deliveries. Coal stockpiles are maintained on-site to protect against unanticipated demand changes and supply disruptions caused by factors such as weather, transportation issues, or production variability. Each plant operates under defined target, critical, and physical inventory limits that are reviewed annually by Georgia Power and SCS. SCS emphasizes steady, ratable deliveries while allowing inventories to draw down during high‑burn periods and rebuild during low‑burn periods to better accommodate changing burn requirements. To manage risk, SCS continuously evaluates coal burn scenarios and projected inventory levels. When critical situations are anticipated, SCS implements mitigation measures such as planned burn, load curtailment, accelerated or deferred deliveries, use of off‑site inventory, or—subject to Georgia Power approval—coal buyouts or resales.
To ensure that coal supply continues to be available in the future, Southern Company has entered several coal supply contracts that provide a fixed and consistent delivery of coal on an on-going basis. As rail companies’ ability to provide service flexibility has decreased, Southern Company has transitioned to a more proactive coal inventory management process to mitigate a more restrictive coal supply chain and ensure reliability of fuel supply to our plants. The intent of the coal inventory management program is to maintain greater flexibility with the stockpile at each unit so that the unit remains responsive to both higher demand periods and lower demand periods while still receiving regular shipments of coal. 
A recent example that highlights the importance of this reliability of supply was Winter Storm Fern, during which gas prices increased significantly, and Southern’s coal fleet was able to respond when called during this peak event due to ample supply of fuel, which helped reduce overall costs to customers during a winter event with extremely high gas prices. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO ALLEGATIONS THAT THE COMPANY DISPATCHES GENERATION UNECONOMICALLY.
A.Southern Company does not dispatch generation on an uneconomic basis. Generation resources are committed and dispatched based on system‑wide production cost considerations rather than on unit‑specific incremental energy cost alone. The system-side production costs are optimized considering many factors.  As described more fully below, certain generating units as part of the overall optimization may be dispatched out of strict energy merit order when necessary to comply with binding transmission facility ratings, contingency constraints, voltage support requirements, reserve obligations, minimum operating levels, environmental or fuel-related operating limitations, avoidance of uneconomic start-up or cycling costs, or other power delivery limitations affecting the ability to reliably serve load across the interconnected system. Such commitments reflect the least‑cost, feasible operating solution for the system as a whole and are undertaken to ensure reliable service to native load customers consistent with prudent utility practice.
Q.WHAT FACTORS INFLUENCE UNIT COMMITMENT DECISIONS?
A.Typically, generation units are dispatched in the pool to reliably meet customer demand at the lowest total production cost without regard to ownership. Meaning, units are typically committed to serve customer load on the system based on which resources are least costly to run and most economic for customers. However, other factors independently, or concurrently, may impact the decision to commit a unit for reasons other than strict economics to meet system needs. For example, the types of operational limitations and constraints may include:  
System Reliability Requirements – Units are placed on “reliability must run” at the discretion of the Reliability Coordinator, not Fleet Operations, based on the Reliability Coordinator’s assessment of operational requirements to maintain system reliability (e.g., voltage support, avoidance of transmission line overloads, etc.) 
Environmental Limitations – Certain units may be limited based on the number of hours of operation allowed in compliance with air permits or other environmental restrictions. 
Minimum Uptime / Downtime Constraints – Some units have a minimum run-time or down-time requirement. For example, a unit might be placed on transmission must-run for one day, but not the next. If the unit has not met the minimum up time or is expected to be needed again within the seven days, then it may continue to operate in the interim to be available when needed next. 
Testing Requirements – Various testing requirements often require a unit to run to demonstrate environmental compliance, capability, availability post outage, or to confirm operational health of a unit.
Real-Time Constraints – Fleet Operations may start or shutdown a unit quickly to preserve system integrity and reliability in response to real time unit performance or changes in load.
Fuel Management – Operational limitations on fuel supply may restrict typical usage or unit availability. For example, natural gas units may be held back during a portion of the day to ensure that enough natural gas remains available on the pipeline during the afternoon peak in the winter. 
Q.CAN IT STILL BE PRUDENT AND IN THE BEST INTERESTS OF CUSTOMERS TO OPERATE A UNIT EVEN IF OUT OF STRICT ECONOMIC ORDER?
A.Yes. As described above, operational complexity, system needs, and unit characteristics all must be considered in how system operators determine which units are needed to run at a given time. In evaluating the various constraints that may impact unit commitment, it is important to frame the decision within the following context: (1) the fleet is operated as one portfolio to achieve the lowest cost of operation in lieu of any constraints; (2) an operating limitation or constraint is not necessarily indicative of uneconomic dispatch—a unit may be running for reliability reasons, while concurrently remaining economical; and (3) at any given time, a unit may be limited by a single constraint while at other times multiple constraints may be occurring simultaneously. 
Q.PLEASE SPECIFICALLY ADDRESS NRDC, SIERRA CLUB, AND SACE WITNESS GOGGIN’S ANALYSIS REGARDING ECONOMIC DISPATCH. 
A.Mr. Goggin’s analysis compares a unit’s committed and dispatched costs to a historical day-ahead lambda price and gives limited consideration to a single unit operating constraint—minimum up times—within a number of constraints that impact the operation of the units and the system as a whole. However, Mr. Goggin does not include the minimum downtime requirements for each unit as reflected in Aurora inputs for Plants Bowen and Scherer. Minimum downtimes are important to consider because they reflect unit commitment risks, such as natural gas price, load volatility, and other ever-changing system conditions, over a week-long time period, compared to the day-ahead approach used in Mr. Goggin’s analysis. That longer planning horizon requires managing operational and market risk over multiple days—a key consideration not reflected in Mr. Goggin’s analysis. Furthermore, he does not consider constraints critical to the operation of the Company’s generation system such as transmission reliability, and environmental or fuel-related operating limitations. 
In addition, Mr. Goggin’s analysis does not account for how removing large generators from the system would change overall system production costs and marginal costs, nor does it account for the resulting impacts to system reserves. The Company performs a production-cost optimization subject to all applicable system, and unit constraints to determine which units should be online and when. The marginal cost is an output of that constrained optimization—it is not an input that can be held constant while selectively removing generation. Therefore, using a historical day-ahead lambda, which does not assume the operation of a generating resource such as Plant Bowen or Plant Scherer, to determine the impact to fuel costs is inappropriate without considering the full impact of a scenario where the referenced plants would not have operated. This potential outcome could create additional constraints in the system and, in turn, drive up the system lambda. In addition, there are operating reserve considerations that must be considered in the economic dispatch of the units. A committed Plant Bowen or Plant Scherer unit provides regulating, contingency and additional operating reserves to the system as a benefit, which is not necessarily captured in the dispatch price.

V.NATURAL GAS HEDGING PROGRAM
Q.PLEASE PROVIDE A BRIEF BACKGROUND OF THE COMPANY’S NATURAL GAS HEDGING PROGRAM. 
A.The Company has maintained a Commission‑approved natural gas hedging program for over two decades as part of its fuel procurement practices. The program is intended to mitigate customer exposure to volatility in natural gas prices, which represent a significant component of the Company’s fuel costs, by fixing or capping the price for a portion of the Company’s projected natural gas burn.
The Company’s hedging activities are financial in nature and utilize instruments such as swaps and options, subject to parameters established through Commission orders in prior fuel cost recovery proceedings. The objective of the hedging program is to reduce exposure to adverse natural gas price movements and improve cost stability based on information available at the time hedging decisions are made, rather than to minimize realized fuel costs in hindsight. The structure of the hedging program has evolved over time as the Commission has adjusted allowable hedge volumes, tenors, and instruments in response to changing market conditions and policy considerations. The program is administered by SCS on behalf of Georgia Power, with hedging strategies and approvals established by Company management. All hedging activity is conducted within the parameters approved by this Commission. Consistent with Commission policy, all realized hedging gains and losses flow through the fuel cost recovery mechanism.
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO PIA STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION THAT THE EXISTING NATURAL GAS HEDGING PROGRAM BE TERMINATED.
A.Georgia Power disagrees with this recommendation. The purpose of the hedging program is not to take speculative positions for gains or losses—it is to guard against volatility of natural gas prices in the market. The Company believes that rate stability for customers is an important consideration in setting regulatory policy. However, it’s important to clarify the difference between rate stability and price stability as important customer protections. Rate stability refers to how often rates are changed, whereas price stability refers to whether the underlying costs upon which the rates are based are subject to volatility. The FCR over/under true-up mechanism and the adequate period between fuel cases provide rate stability for customers. The hedging program is the mechanism that provides price stability for natural gas purchases by locking in the future purchase prices today, as described further below. In combination, the Company’s fixed fuel rates and the natural gas hedging program protect against volatility and provide customers with the benefits of rate stability and price stability, respectively.

A.No. An increase in the hedging volume is advisable at this time given the additional natural gas burn projected in the test period. Likewise, an increase in the hedging window to 48 months incorporates a longer-term natural gas price forecast into the mix of hedges, which provides additional diversity and stability of fuel price. During periods of sharply rising prices, the program reduces peak costs, while during periods of rapidly declining prices, it results in higher prices than spot market outcomes. This reflects the intended function of a risk mitigation strategy, not an attempt to outperform the market on a realized cost basis. 
Q.WITH RELATIVELY LOW NATURAL GAS PRICES, WHY IS HEDGING NATURAL GAS PRICES STILL REASONABLE?
A.Even at lower prices, natural gas prices are still volatile. Natural gas prices have an inherent level of volatility due to factors such as supply-demand imbalances, weather, and market dynamics. Given the Company’s increased reliance on natural gas generation and corresponding increase in the volume of natural gas purchased, smaller changes in price can have a material impact on the Company’s total natural gas fuel costs. For example, due to the Company’s increased consumption, a $1 per MMBtu change in the price of gas today could cause a $260 million change in cost, whereas as recently as 2010, prices would have had to change over $2 per MMBtu to cause the same $260 million change. Continuing the Company’s natural gas hedging program will mitigate the impact of this volatility. 
Q.DO THE HISTORICAL HEDGE LOSSES REFLECT INEFFECTIVENESS OF THE HEDGES?
A.No, the hedge losses and gains are a function of the natural gas prices in the market compared to the hedged positions and should not be used to determine effectiveness of the hedging program. As mentioned earlier, the Company does not take speculative positions, and the intention of the program is to mitigate price volatility. Moreover, the cumulative hedge gains or losses depend on the time period for which they are measured. As seen in the table provided in PIA Staff’s testimony, the hedge positions over the last ten years have resulted in a cumulative gain over that time period. On a long-term basis, the Company expects the hedge gains and losses to largely offset as natural gas prices typically fluctuate up and down.  
Q.IS THERE A FUNDAMENTAL MARKET DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE TIME PERIODS COVERED BY THE HEDGING PROGRAM IN THE EARLY 2000’S COMPARED TO THE LAST FIFTEEN YEARS? 
A.Yes. The natural gas market has fundamentally changed as result of shale gas driven by hydraulic fracturing (“fracking”) and horizontal drilling, which had a dramatic impact on the supply of natural gas within the industry. With the abundance of supply, the price pressure brought on by potential structural scarcity of natural gas was no longer a prominent force driving the market. In 2008, the average NYMEX prices for the following year were trading over $12/MMBtu during certain times, driven by a narrative that the country was running out of natural gas and would soon be dependent on liquified natural gas (“LNG”) imports to meet its needs. The introduction of shale gas and fracking soon changed the natural gas outlook and prices dropped precipitously. As a result, the market is operating in a much different environment now given that there is no structural scarcity driving up prices. It is widely believed that the United States has decades of natural gas supply available at prices generally in the range of prices observed in the market today. However, while long‑term supply abundance has reduced structural scarcity risk, natural gas prices remain subject to material volatility driven by weather‑related impacts on both demand and supply, infrastructure constraints, and broader market dynamics.

VII.REAL TIME PRICING 
Q.HAS THE COMPANY INTRODUCED A NEW RTP FUEL ACCOUNTING METHODOLOGY IN THIS CASE?
A.No. The Company continues to use the methodology approved by the Commission in the Company’s 2010 base rate case (Docket No. 31958) and in practice for the last 15 years. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE CURRENT RTP FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY.
A.RTP incremental load is priced based on marginal cost. That revenue received from RTP customers is allocated partially to base and partially to fuel. The amount allocated to fuel is the average marginal replacement fuel cost of all Georgia Power resources operating in that hour plus Georgia Power’s share of pool sales or purchases and off-system sales or purchases. 
Q.WHY WAS THIS METHODOLOGY UPDATED IN 2010?
A.The RTP fuel credit methodology was updated in 2010 to better mirror the overall RTP rate and more quickly reflect changes in fuel costs incurred by the Company. The revised methodology better aligns fuel revenues and expenses attributable to RTP customers and limits wide swings in amounts credited to the fuel and base accounts during periods of fuel price volatility. 
Q.ARE FIRM TRANSPORTATION OR NON-DISPATCHABLE PPA COSTS INCLUDED IN THE RTP FUEL CREDIT CALCULATION? 
A.No. Firm Transportation (“FT”) and non-dispatchable PPA costs have not been included under the current methodology. These fixed costs are, by definition, not marginal replacement fuel costs. 
Q.WERE FT AND NON-DISPATCHABLE PPA COSTS RECOVERED THROUGH THE FUEL DOCKET PRIOR TO 2010?
A.Yes, FT and non-dispatchable PPA costs were recorded as FCR expenses prior to 2010, when the current methodology was approved by the Commission, and have continued to be since the implementation of the current methodology.
Q.DO RTP CUSTOMERS CONTRIBUTE TO FIXED COSTS INCLUDING FT AND NON-DISPATCHABLE PPAS? 
A.Yes. The RTP incremental load is assessed a marginal cost-based rate. The price for RTP incremental load includes the marginal running cost of the system (lambda) even though most of the kilowatt hours sold are generated by plants with a lower running cost. As a result, most hours priced at RTP provide a contribution to the fixed costs of the system by lambda being greater than the underlying energy costs to serve them. Further, the Customer Baseline Load (“CBL”) portion of an RTP customer’s load is assessed the fully embedded base and FCR rate.  
Q.WOULD CHANGING THE FUEL CREDIT METHODOLOGY CHANGE THE TOTAL REVENUES RECEIVED FROM RTP CUSTOMERS OR IMPACT TOTAL COSTS FOR OTHER CUSTOMERS?
A.No. As discussed in the 2000 Rule Nisi proceeding (Docket No. 11708-U) focused on RTP and Georgia Power’s 2010 base rate case proceeding, where the RTP methodology was changed, a change in accounting practice would have no effect on the RTP price or the resulting revenues. It would merely shift base revenue to fuel to lower fuel costs, but the lost base revenue would need to be made up in increased base rates of those same customers who would now have lower FCR charges. This would have no lasting effect on the cost to customers.

A.Various intervenors suggested the Company adopt a fuel cost sharing mechanism, where the Company shares in the risk of the actual fuel costs in comparison to the forecasted budget on which the fuel rates are set. Any amounts over the fixed percentage amount are presumed to be captured through the over/under mechanism to be addressed in the next fuel rate case.

Fuel costs have always been designed as a direct pass-through on a dollar-for-dollar basis. Based on their proposal, the fuel costs will now potentially flow through as income for the Company. Fuel costs are often volatile based on market dynamics on both macro and micro-economic level, most of which are outside of the Company’s control. However, in citing these reports, Witness Reynolds does not address the fundamental differences between the purpose of the analysis conducted for such reports and the determination of prudence that occurs in the context of this proceeding.  
The reports relied upon were not written with the goal of analyzing whether the decisions in question were reasonable given what was known at the time. Instead, the reports utilize hindsight to identify the potential causes of the incident in question, whether such causes could have been avoided through the application of reasonable management decisions at the time of the event. The goal of identifying possible causes of an event is quite different from evaluating whether the actions or decisions in question were reasonable given the circumstances that existed at that time. Often, these reports are based on information that was not available at the time the underlying decisions were made or they omit key background information or other general issues that were considered by the decisionmakers.  
Further, Witness Reynolds only presents selected portions of the material included in the reports, which provide insufficient context for the Commission’s review. The terminology utilized in such reports often has meanings that are specific to the goals and ends of such reports. For example, “inadequate” connotes that the relevant actions or decisions did not result in the desired outcome but does not mean that the actions or decisions were unreasonable or reflective of a lack of diligence and care by Company employees. Thus, to use these reports as Witness Reynolds has, without proper context or independent analyses, risks misinterpreting or misconstruing such reports and the actions taken in response to certain events. In particular, the Root Cause and Apparent Cause reports typically do not delve into the underlying basis or rationale for a decision, such as why an engineer, technician, or operator took a particular action, but rather, summarily conclude that the decision did not lead to the desired result.
Q.DO YOU STAND BEHIND THE ACCURACY AND COMPLETENESS OF THE REPORTS?  
A.Yes. I am extremely proud of the strength of our evaluative process as reflected in these reports. I, along with all of Southern Nuclear’s leadership, encourage thorough and exhaustive analysis of these outages in order to identify every possible corrective action that could have been taken to better inform practice and process improvements to prevent such events from reoccurring. We apply a very high standard to our causal analysis findings and corrective actions consistent with nuclear industry measures of excellence. It is important to note, however, that the purpose of post-event causal analysis is to find opportunities for improvement using information that is available only after the event occurs. Accordingly, these analyses intentionally apply a high standard and benefit from hindsight and are not necessarily designed to assess whether decisions made prior to the event are reasonable based on the information available at that time. We acknowledge that not all processes are perfect and there are always opportunities for improvement and the causal analyses bear that fact. However, the Company’s efforts to constantly improve our processes and performance should not be construed as evidence of imprudence.

This goal is exactly why Southern Nuclear conducts Root Cause and Apparent Cause analyses without regard for cost or what was actually or reasonably known at the time. Southern Nuclear intends to utilize every resource and every avenue for improvement that is available. My testimony is not intended to trivialize the outages that occurred or minimize the importance of the additional fuel costs incurred. A.While it is the Company’s position that none of these outages were the result of imprudent actions or decisions, the fact that Witness Reynolds only recommended disallowance of replacement fuel cost for five outages is a testament to the high level at which the Company operates these plants. Over the three-year period covered by this proceeding, Plants Hatch and Vogtle have contributed 64,802,106 megawatt hours (“MWh”) of low-cost, emission free generation to Georgia Power’s customers. Q.DOES THE EFFECTIVENESS OF SOUTHERN NUCLEAR’S FLEET FIGURE INTO THE CONTEXT OF THE INCIDENTS DISCUSSED IN MR. REYNOLDS’ TESTIMONY?
A.Yes. Southern Nuclear’s excellent and cost-effective performance provides the context necessary to truly understand why the decisions that Southern Nuclear made—based upon known facts and competing priorities at the time, as the prudence standard requires—were reasonable. In particular, Southern Nuclear has a demonstrated history of success in reducing the impact of unplanned outages. Although there are no criteria that measure “prudence,” there are metrics for the reliability of nuclear plants. One of these metrics is called the Capacity Factor, a measure of how often a power plant runs over a specific period of time. The Capacity Factor is expressed as a percentage and calculated by dividing the actual unit electricity output by the maximum possible output. This ratio indicates how fully a unit’s capacity is used. The Capacity Factor of Southern Nuclear’s Georgia fleet (not including Vogtle Units 3 and 4) over the previous three years is 93.63%. This is in line with the average of 93.2% for nuclear power generation in the United States. Vogtle Units 3 and 4 do not have three full years of service at this point and, as previously discussed with the Commission, Unit 4 was operated at a lower power level to extend fuel life.

A.The success of Southern Nuclear’s fleet on metrics of cost, production, efficiency, and safety could not be achieved without a culture of safety, reliability, and sensible decision making that benefits Georgia Power’s customers. The Root Cause and Apparent Cause reports, which Witness Reynolds relies upon, are not written within this framework.

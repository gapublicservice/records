 8   Natural Gas Hedging

 9   Q    WHAT IS A HEDGE?

10   A    In general, a hedge refers to a financial transaction that aims to mitigate exposure to

11        market price volatility. Within the context of natural gas markets, a hedge represents

12        a financial position in gas futures contracts that can offset potential losses (or gains)

13        incurred by fluctuations in the natural gas commodity price. That stated, natural gas

14        hedging can protect the utility from upside price risk if market prices at the time of

15        delivery are higher than the hedged price, but it can also expose the utility to losses if

16        the market price settles below the hedged price.


17   Q    WILL A HEDGING STRATEGY RESULT IN BELOW-MARKET FUEL COSTS?

18   A    No, not necessarily. A proper hedging strategy means managing price risk and not

19        entering into speculative transactions to beat the market. Over time, hedging should

20        result in a utility purchasing fuel at the market price (plus reasonable transaction and

21        administrative costs) but with much less volatility. As discussed later, this expected

22        result has not materialized for GPC.

 1   Q   IS NATURAL GAS HEDGING COST-FREE?

 2   A   No. First, GPC will incur its own costs to develop hedging strategies and to procure

 3       and manage its fuel hedges. Second, GPC will incur fees to execute various financial

 4       instruments (i.e., swaps and collars). Third, because financial swaps lock-in the price

 5       of natural gas for specific quantities and time periods, GPC may incur either gains or

 6       losses if a specific swap settles above or below the current market price, respectively.


 7   Q   WHY IS GEORGIA POWER COMPANY ENGAGED IN NATURAL GAS HEDGING?

 8   A   GPC’s gas hedging program was implemented in response to the increasing volatility

 9       of natural gas prices, as well as its growing reliance on natural gas generation.


10   Q   ARE NATURAL GAS PRICES ALWAYS VOLATILE?

11   A   No. However, natural gas prices do react to major storms and geopolitical events.

12       Exhibit JP-1 provides a history of spot natural gas prices traded at the Henry Hub

13       since 1997. Also shown are the major events that triggered spikes in gas prices. As

14       can be seen, the most extreme price spikes occurred during Winter Storm Uri in

15       February 2021 and, more recently, Winter Storm Fern in January 2026. Both storms

16       featured severe snow and ice and sustained below-normal temperatures.

17              Given the history, while an active hedging program could help to manage

18       volatility, it is reasonable to question whether customers directly benefit when the utility

19       executes hedges to stabilize natural gas prices.

 7   Nuclear PTCs

 8   Q    WHAT ARE THE NUCLEAR PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS?

 9   A    Section 13105 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 created section 45U, the zero-

10        emission nuclear power production credit, for electricity produced at a qualified nuclear

11        power facility and sold by the taxpayer to an unrelated person in tax years beginning

12        after December 31, 2023, and before January 1, 2033.


13   Q    WHAT NUCLEAR POWER FACILITIES QUALIFY FOR THE INTERNAL REVENUE

14        CODE SECTION 45U PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS?

15   A    To qualify, a nuclear power facility must have been placed in service before August

16        16, 2022. This means that GPC’s Hatch and Vogtle units 1 and 2 potentially qualify to

17        earn section 45U tax credits.


18   Q    HOW ARE PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS EARNED?

19   A    PTCs are earned for every kWh generated by a qualified nuclear power facility.


20   Q    DO PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS REDUCE A UTILITY’S COST TO SERVE?

21   A    Yes. PTCs can be used to reduce the utility’s tax liability. If the utility does not have

22        a sufficient tax liability to utilize the PTCs, the utility can monetize the PTCs by selling

23        them to third parties and use the proceeds to reduce costs.

 1       Q    HOW IS GEORGIA POWER COMPANY PROPOSING TO UTILIZE THE NUCLEAR

 8       Q    SHOULD THE NUCLEAR PRODUCTION TAX CREDITS BE USED TO OFFSET

 9            STORM DAMAGE EXPENSE?

10       A    No. First, as previously stated, the nuclear PTCs are earned for every kWh generated

11            by a qualified nuclear power facility. Thus, PTCs are clearly more similar to fuel costs,

13                    Second, if PTCs are used to offset storm damage costs, the benefit would not

14            be allocated properly. This is because, under the Settlement Agreement approved in

15            ARP Extension, storm damage costs are to be allocated and recovered on equal

16            percentage of base revenues.10 However, the percentage of base revenues charged

17            to the various customer classes is quite different from the corresponding percentage

18            of recoverable fuel costs. The differences are illustrated in Table 2.

QON WHOSE BEHALF ARE YOU TESTIFYING IN THIS PROCEEDING? 
AI am testifying on behalf of the Georgia Association of Manufacturers (GAM).  The members of GAM are customers of Georgia Power Company (GPC).  They consume large quantities of electricity, primarily for manufacturing, under a variety of rate schedules.  
QWhat issues are you addressing in your direct testimony?
AFirst, I present an overview of GPC’s proposed Fuel Cost Recovery- (FCR-27) rate proposal.  I will then discuss GPC’s proposal to expand its natural gas hedging program.  Finally, I  address why nuclear production tax credits (PTCs) authorized under section 45U of the Internal Revenue Code should be used to offset FCR-27 costs rather than storm damage costs.
qDoes the fact that you are not addressing all issues in this proceeding constitute an endorsement of the positions of georgia power company or other parties on these issues?

Summary
qPlease summarize your findings and recommendations.
AMy findings and recommendations are as follows:
GPC is proposing to concurrently reduce fuel cost recoveries while increasing base revenues, effective June 1, 2026.  The proposed FCR-27 would reduce fuel revenues by $388 million (12.5%).  This reduction would be partially offset by GPC’s storm damage recovery proposal, which would increase annual revenues by $270 million.  
GPC is also proposing:
To continue and expand its natural gas hedging program;
To increase the threshold under the Interim Fuel Recovery (IFR) mechanism from $200 million to $300 million; and  
To continue recovering through the FCR all renewable requests for proposal (RFPs) and renewable subscription program-related costs that are not otherwise recovered through bid fees and winner’s fees.
GPC has not demonstrated that its natural gas hedging program is providing actual benefits to customers despite the significant costs incurred.  Accordingly, the Commission should reject GPC’s proposal to expand gas hedging until tangible benefits can be demonstrated.  
GPC is proposing to use $90 million of section 45U nuclear PTCs to offset $360 million of storm damage expense.  These tax credits were generated in 2024 and 2025. 
PTCs are earned for every kilowatt-hour (kWh) generated from eligible nuclear production facilities, which include GPC’s Hatch and Vogtle units 1 and 2.  Thus, these PTCs more closely resemble fuel costs than storm damage expenses.  
Using PTCs as an offset to storm cost recovery would not be consistent with cost causation because (pursuant to the Settlement Agreement approved in the Annual Rate Plan (ARP) extension) they would be allocated to customer classes on base revenues.  
A more reasonable and cost-based approach is to use the nuclear PTCs to offset FCR-27 recoverable fuel costs. Therefore, the Commission should use these tax credits to offset recoverable FCR-27 costs.

qIs georgia power company proposing other requests in this proceeding?
AYes.  First, GPC is proposing to expand its hedging program to include up to 60% of budgeted natural gas burn for a period of up to 48 months.  Currently, GPC is allowed to hedge 40% of budgeted natural gas burn for a period of up to 36 months. 
Second, GPC is proposing to increase the threshold under the IFR mechanism from $200 million to $300 million.
Finally, GPC requests continued authorization to recover through the FCR all renewable RFPs and renewable subscription program-related costs that are not otherwise recovered through bid fees and winner’s fees.
qis this the only pending application that will impact retail rates?
ANo.  Concurrently, GPC made a second filing to increase annual revenues by $270 million (5.0%) to recover expenses associated with major storms that occurred since the Company’s 2022 base rate case (Docket No. 44280).  Recovery would occur over a four-year period, and GPC would earn its authorized weighted average cost of capital, including a 10.5% return on common equity, on the unamortized balance.  The $270 million includes $360 million of incremental storm costs, which GPC is proposing to partially offset by using $90 million of nuclear PTCs earned in the years 2024 and 2025.
Together, the two filings would result in a net $118 million (1.0%) reduction in overall rates ($388 million less $270 million).  The FCR and storm cost recovery rates are proposed to become effective June 1, 2026.
qDo Georgia power company’s applications raise important policy issues?
AYes.  As previously stated, the Company is proposing to continue, and expand, its natural gas hedging program.  This raises the question of whether it is both necessary and beneficial for GPC to engage in natural gas hedging to help “stabilize” fuel costs.
Second, GPC is proposing to use nuclear PTCs to make the proposed storm recovery charges more affordable for consumers.  However, as discussed later, nuclear PTCs are no different in nature from recoverable fuel expenses.  Both are related to kWh generation.  Whether the nuclear PTCs are used to offset storm damage costs or to lower the FCR rate, GPC’s customers would still benefit and rates would be more affordable.  However, using the nuclear PTCs to offset the FCR balance would be consistent with cost causation.  
Natural Gas Hedging
qWhat is a hedge?  
AIn general, a hedge refers to a financial transaction that aims to mitigate exposure to market price volatility.  Within the context of natural gas markets, a hedge represents a financial position in gas futures contracts that can offset potential losses (or gains) incurred by fluctuations in the natural gas commodity price.  That stated, natural gas hedging can protect the utility from upside price risk if market prices at the time of delivery are higher than the hedged price, but it can also expose the utility to losses if the market price settles below the hedged price.  
qwill a hedging strategy result in below-market fuel costs?
ANo, not necessarily.  A proper hedging strategy means managing price risk and not entering into speculative transactions to beat the market.  Over time, hedging should result in a utility purchasing fuel at the market price (plus reasonable transaction and administrative costs) but with much less volatility.  As discussed later, this expected result has not materialized for GPC.  
qis natural gas hedging cost-free?
ANo.  First, GPC will incur its own costs to develop hedging strategies and to procure and manage its fuel hedges.  Second, GPC will incur fees to execute various financial instruments (i.e., swaps and collars).  Third, because financial swaps lock-in the price of natural gas for specific quantities and time periods, GPC may incur either gains or losses if a specific swap settles above or below the current market price, respectively.  
qWhy is Georgia power company engaged in natural gas hedging?
AGPC’s gas hedging program was implemented in response to the increasing volatility of natural gas prices, as well as its growing reliance on natural gas generation. 
qare natural gas prices always VOLATILE?
ANo.  However, natural gas prices do react to major storms and geopolitical events.  Exhibit JP-1 provides a history of spot natural gas prices traded at the Henry Hub since 1997.  Also shown are the major events that triggered spikes in gas prices.  As can be seen, the most extreme price spikes occurred during Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 and, more recently, Winter Storm Fern in January 2026.  Both storms featured severe snow and ice and sustained below-normal temperatures. 
Given the history, while an active hedging program could help to manage volatility, it is reasonable to question whether customers directly benefit when the utility executes hedges to stabilize natural gas prices.
qDid the commission previously adjust georgia power Company’s gas hedging program?  
AYes.  In FCR-25, GPC agreed to reduce the hedge window from 48 months to 36
months and reduce the limit from 50% to 40% of budgeted natural gas burn recognizing, in part, lower relative price volatility. 
qHave retail customers clearly benefitted from Georgia Power Company’s fuel hedging strategies?
ANo.  Although the primary objective of hedging is to mitigate fuel price volatility, it is unclear whether this has translated into less volatile retail electricity prices.  This, in large part, recognizes the long-standing ratemaking practices used by the Commission to set and periodically review GPC’s FCR rates, which facilitate rate stability.  
qdoes fuel hedging guarantee that retail electricity prices will be less volatile?
ANo.  Reducing the volatility of fuels acquired by GPC to generate electricity does not necessarily translate into less price volatility for GPC’s customers.  
qhas Georgia power Company provided evidence that its past fuel hedging programs have resulted in less volatile retail electricity prices?
ANo.  
qis fuel hedging the only tool available to mitigate electricity price volatility?
ANo.  The Commission’s rate setting practices can also mitigate volatility in both natural gas and other fuel commodity markets.  For example, the Commission sets GPC’s FCR rates using a 24-month test period.  This helps to smooth any monthly or quarterly variation in natural gas prices that may occur within a calendar year.  Should there be a material change in gas prices (either up or down) resulting in an accumulated balance that exceeds $200 million, GPC can implement an IFR that would absorb unanticipated short-term changes in natural gas and other commodity costs, while not creating substantial price volatility.  Finally, any remaining actual over- or under-collected costs can be recovered when setting the next FCR rate.  
Together, setting a two-year FCR rate, coupled with the IFR and carrying forward past-period balances smooths the impact of changes in natural gas and other commodity prices on retail electricity prices.  These effective mechanisms reduce fuel volatility and are sufficient to address fuel price swings.  Hedging to further address volatility may be unwarranted given that GPC’s hedging programs increased recoverable fuel costs by $274 million from 2023 through 2025.  Further, during this period, hedging losses occurred in all but one month.  An effective hedging program should track market prices over the long term.  Thus, it is not unreasonable to expect that hedging losses would be offset by hedging gains.  
qhave any utilities discontinued the gas hedging programs because they resulted in significant hedging losses?
AYes.  The investor-owned utilities in Florida previously engaged in gas hedging programs since 2002.  However, these hedging programs increased recoverable fuel costs by nearly $7 billion through 2016.  As a result of these huge costs, these utilities no longer engage in hedging.  
qWhat do you recommend?
AGPC is ultimately responsible for managing all of its costs, fuel included, in a prudent manner.  At times, this may require hedging to better manage commodity risk.  However, before allowing GPC to expand its current gas hedging program, the Commission should require GPC to document and quantify how its past hedging strategies have benefitted retail customers.
Nuclear PTCs
qWhat are the nuclear production tax credits?
ASection 13105 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 created section 45U, the zero-emission nuclear power production credit, for electricity produced at a qualified nuclear power facility and sold by the taxpayer to an unrelated person in tax years beginning after December 31, 2023, and before January 1, 2033.
qWhat nuclear power facilities qualify for the INTERNAL revenue code Section 45u production tax credits?
ATo qualify, a nuclear power facility must have been placed in service before August 16, 2022.  This means that GPC’s Hatch and Vogtle units 1 and 2 potentially qualify to earn section 45U tax credits.
qHow are production tax credits earned?
APTCs are earned for every kWh generated by a qualified nuclear power facility.  
qdo production tax credits reduce a utility’s cost to serve?
AYes.  PTCs can be used to reduce the utility’s tax liability.  If the utility does not have a sufficient tax liability to utilize the PTCs, the utility can monetize the PTCs by selling them to third parties and use the proceeds to reduce costs.
qHow is georgia power company proposing to utilize the nuclear production tax credits?
AIn its storm damage filing (Docket No. 44280), GPC is proposing to amortize 25% of the total PTCs generated in 2024 and 2025 to partially offset and lower storm damage costs.  GPC had originally proposed to defer these PTCs to a regulatory liability and amortize them over a three-year period beginning on January 1, 2026.  However, to address affordability, GPC decided to use the section 45U PTCs to benefit customers.
qShould the nuclear production tax credits be used to offset storm damage expense?
ANo.  First, as previously stated, the nuclear PTCs are earned for every kWh generated by a qualified nuclear power facility.  Thus, PTCs are clearly more similar to fuel costs, which vary based on the amount of kWh generated, than to storm damage costs. 
Second, if PTCs are used to offset storm damage costs, the benefit would not be allocated properly. This is because, under the Settlement Agreement approved in ARP Extension, storm damage costs are to be allocated and recovered on equal percentage of base revenues.  However, the percentage of base revenues charged to the various customer classes is quite different from the corresponding percentage of recoverable fuel costs.  The differences are illustrated in Table 2.

qplease explain table 2. 
ATable 2 quantifies the percentage of base revenues and the percentage of fuel revenues by customer class as presented in the class cost-of-service study filed by GPC in its 2022 rate case.  
qwhat do you conclude from table 2?
ATable 2 demonstrates that Residential customers have a higher percentage of base revenues than fuel revenues, while the opposite is true for Industrial customers.  This reflects the fact that Industrial customers typically operate at higher load factors than either Residential or Commercial customers.  
Thus, using the nuclear PTCs to offset the FCR-27 costs is consistent with cost causation because these tax credits are earned on a kWh basis, and fuel costs are also directly related to the amount of kWh generated.  
qWould Georgia Power Company’s net revenues be the same if the nuclear production tax credits are used to reduce the fuel cost recovery balance rather than to offset storm damage expense?
AYes.  
qis there any precedent in Georgia for using nuclear production tax credits as an offset to fuel cost recovery?
ANo.  Section 45U became effective after the 2022 rate case.  Thus, there has been no prior determination of how nuclear tax credits should be treated for ratemaking purposes.
qDoes the fcr rate preclude crediting the nuclear production tax credits through fuel rates?
ANo.  The definition of fuel costs in the FCR-26 tariff includes:
(9) other costs or credits as determined by the Commission for inclusion in, or reduction of, recoverable fuel costs.
Clearly, nuclear PTCs will reduce recoverable fuel costs.
qAre there examples of other non-fuel related costs that the commission has authorized for recovery in the fuel cost recovery rate?
AYes.  GPC is requesting continued authorization to recover all renewable RFPs and renewable subscription program-related costs that are not otherwise recovered through bid fees and winner’s fees.  These are neither fuel nor fuel-related costs.  They are not caused by the kWh generated by GPC’s power plants or the kWh purchased under GPC’s various power purchase agreements.  However, GPC rationalizes treating them as fuel costs because the benefits of renewable energy procured through power purchase agreements accrue to customers in the form of lower fuel costs.  
Similarly, although nuclear PTCs are not fuel costs per se, there is a more direct relationship between tax credits and recoverable fuel costs than the renewable RFP and subscription program-related costs.  Unlike renewable RFP costs, nuclear PTCs are caused by the amount of energy generated by eligible nuclear power plants.  Like the former, they will directly lower fuel costs.  Thus, there is no policy reason not to treat nuclear tax credits as an offset to recoverable fuel expenses.  
qwhat do you recommend?
AConsistent with cost causation, the nuclear PTCs should be used to offset the projected FCR-27 costs.  These PTCs are not needed to offset storm damage expense to address affordability because applying them to reduce recoverable fuel costs will have the same overall impact.  Further, as will be discussed in our testimony in the concurrent storm proceeding, there are more affordable ways to recover storm damage costs than GPC is proposing.  
qDoes this conclude your testimony?
AYes.

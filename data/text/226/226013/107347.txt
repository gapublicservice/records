STF-PIA-3-15

Question: 

What governs the amount of wholesale market opportunity sales that Georgia Power can make in any given hour? Can Georgia Power engage in sales in any given hour that reduces the unit capacity in that hour to a point below the reliability threshold established in the most recent IRP (forecasted load plus Target Reserve Margin)?

Are the terms “wholesale market opportunity sales” and “economy sales synonymous” for Georgia Power?

Response:

The Intercompany Interchange Contract (“IIC”) is the governing document underlying all Pool transactions including Pool transactions with others, such as wholesale market opportunity sales.  As described in Section 9.4.2 of the IIC, “the Pool has the exclusive right to use generations resources committed to the Pool (“Pool Resource”) to engage in opportunity transaction with OTHERS that would begin and end during the period from the current hour through Friday (midnight) of the following week.” Any amount of sales governed by this process would take into account items such as system conditions, market conditions, fleet economics,  reserves available on the system, system loads, etc.
The Target Reserve Margin serves as a planning metric, which determines the size and nature of the fleet available to serve loads in real time, but otherwise does not factor into the day-to-day operation of the System or any determination of amount of sales.
Wholesale market opportunity sales represent Pool Sales of Capacity and Energy and are described more fully in Section 9.4.2 of the IIC. These wholesale market opportunity sales are sometimes referred to as economy sales, but that term is not utilized in the IIC.

STF-PIA-3-11

Question: 

Per the RTP rate schedule, RTP “Hourly prices are determined each day based on projections of the hourly running cost of incremental generation (including approved environmental costs), provisions for losses, projections of hourly transmission costs and reliability capacity costs for each day (when applicable), and a three (3) mill/kWh recovery factor.”

a.Please describe the function of the provision for losses, transmission costs, reliability capacity costs, and recovery factor – what costs are they intended to recover and describe how each component is calculated.
b.Are any of the revenues for “provisions of losses” attributed to the fuel balance (used determine the FCR rate)?
c.Are any of the revenues for “projections of hourly transmission costs” attributed to the fuel balance (used determine the FCR rate)?
d.Are any of the revenues for “reliability capacity costs” attributed to the fuel balance (used to determine the FCR rate)?
e.Are any of the “three mill/kwh recovery factor” attributed to the fuel balance (used to determine the FCR rate)?

Response:
 
The hourly running cost of incremental generation (including approved environmental costs), known as lambda, collects fuel plus variable operations and maintenance (“O&M”) cost and approved environmental emissions costs on the next kilowatt hour (“kWh”) being generated or purchased. Lambda is calculated using hourly replacement fuel costs, system load, and unit availability.  

Provisions for losses are to account for marginal load losses between the generator and the customer.  Losses are calculated by applying the respective loss factors to lambda, reliability, and transmission component values.   

Transmission costs are to send a price signal reflecting the marginal capital costs associated with maintaining the reliability of the transmission system. The transmission component is calculated using the carrying cost of marginal transmission capital costs and is designed to trigger in the 300 highest-load hours in a normal weather year. The triggering mechanism is based on hourly system load relative to the annual forecasted system peak load and includes a temperature element that reflects the load carrying capability of the transmission system.

Reliability capacity costs are to send a price signal reflecting peaking capacity costs during peak hours. The reliability component is calculated using the marginal value of generation capacity and is designed to trigger in the 50 highest-load hours in a normal weather year. The triggering mechanism is based on hourly system load relative to annual forecasted system peak load.

The recovery factor is to account for forecast risk and to offset other fixed costs. The recovery factor is calculated by applying 3/10 cent (3 mills) per kWh for RTP-DA and 2/10 cent (2 mills) for RTP-HA.
 
Losses are included in the lambda portion of the hourly RTP price. The appropriate amount that is applied to the recovery of fuel cost is based on the hourly average marginal fuel cost for the applicable hour as calculated and described in the Company’s response to STF-JKA-1-4 subpart (b).

===== DKT 56765 STF-PIA-3 PUBLIC DISCLOSURE/STF-PIA-3-19.docx =====
STF-PIA-3-19

Question: 

Please explain if and how coal combustion residual (CCR) costs are included in the RTP pricing. Why or why not?

Response:

Coal combustion residual costs are not included in the RTP price. Coal combustion residual costs are recovered through the Environmental Compliance Cost Recovery (“ECCR”) tariff. For RTP customers, ECCR is applied to the CBL portion of the rate. The exclusion of the incremental portion of RTP load from the application of ECCR tariffs was approved as part of the stipulated agreement resolving Georgia Power’s 2007 Rate Case in Docket 25060, which introduced ECCR. To include fully embedded costs of environmental compliance in the marginal portion of the RTP rate would prevent these tariffs from functioning as marginal rates with hourly prices reflecting real-time marginal costs.

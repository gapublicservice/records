II.STORM COST RECOVERY OVERVIEW
Q.WHAT ARE STORM DAMAGE COSTS?
A.Storm damage costs are the costs the Company incurs to restore electric service when a major storm event damages the Company’s distribution, transmission, and generation systems used to provide electric service to our retail customers. We will discuss storm cost recovery in more detail later in our testimony. 
Q.HOW HAS THE COMMISSION PREVIOUSLY ADDRESSED THE RECOVERY OF COSTS INCURRED TO RESTORE ELECTRIC SERVICE TO CUSTOMERS FOLLOWING MAJOR STORM EVENTS?
A.Storm costs are a recognized cost of electric service and have been included as a component of base rates through the same accounting methodology approved by the Commission over the past few decades. In prior base rate cases, the Commission has established an annual storm accrual in base rates for projected storm costs based upon historical storm expense. When the Company incurs expenses to restore power to customers due to major storm events and those expenses exceed or fall below the amount of the annual storm accrual established by the Commission, any excess or shortfall is deferred to a regulatory asset or liability, as appropriate, for future adjustment. Both the recovery or return of previously deferred over- or under-recovered storm expenses and the accrual of future storm costs have been historically determined in the Company’s base rate case proceedings.
Q.WHAT IS THE BASIS OF THE COMPANY’S FILING IN THIS CASE? 
A.In its July 31, 2025, Order in Docket No. 44280, the Commission approved a stipulation reached between Georgia Power and the Public Interest Advocacy Staff to extend the ARP (“ARP Stipulation”). The ARP Stipulation keeps base rates stable and provided the support the Company needed to forgo its planned rate case filing. Notwithstanding the three-year rate freeze, the ARP Stipulation provided for the Commission to separately review and consider Georgia Power’s storm damage costs. As such, the ARP Stipulation recognized the significant under-recovery of storm costs due to major storm events since the Commission’s last rate case decision and acknowledged the negative impacts of carrying such costs for an extended period of time, thereby allowing for consideration of those costs in this case.
Q.PLEASE DISCUSS THE PROVISIONS OF THE ARP STIPULATION AS IT PERTAINS TO STORM COST.
A.The ARP Stipulation provides that the terms and conditions of the ARP approved by the Commission on December 30, 2022, shall remain in effect and that the base rates under the ARP Stipulation will not be adjusted for the next three years (2026, 2027, and 2028), except for storm damage cost. For those costs, the ARP Stipulation provides that:
[t]he Company will be allowed to recover actual reasonable and prudent storm damage cost incurred through December 31, 2025, that exceeds the amount of the annual storm damage accrual approved in the Order Adopting Settlement Agreement as Modified. The Company will file for the recovery of under-recovered storm damage cost no sooner than February 1, 2026, and no later than July 1, 2026, with new rates effective the 1st of the month following 90 days after the request for recovery. The Company's filing will include at a minimum pre-filed direct testimony and the documentation supporting the request for changes in the storm damage accrual as well as the proposed period over which to allow recovery of the under-recovered storm damage balance. The Commission shall determine the rates, the period over which under-recovered storm costs will be recovered, and any other issues the Commission deem necessary to address the limited issue of storm damage cost recovery. The rate increase shall be applied to each traditional base rate on an equal percentage basis. The energy, demand, and basic service charge components shall all be adjusted equally, provided that the Company shall not apply any increase to the basic service charge for the domestic and small business rate groups. The Commission shall issue a final decision within 90 days of the Company’s filing.
The limited scope proceeding required by the ARP Stipulation balances the significant impact of storms like Hurricane Helene, the Company’s response to storms since 2022, and the need to recover the associated costs in a timely manner.

III.OVERVIEW OF RECENT MAJOR STORMS AND GEORGIA POWER’S RESPONSE 
Q.CAN YOU PROVIDE AN OVERVIEW OF THE COMPANY’S PROCESSES FOR IDENTIFYING AND RESPONDING TO MAJOR STORM EVENTS?
A.As a state-regulated, vertically integrated utility, the Company’s storm preparation and response is coordinated across the entire electric system from the generator to the customer meter. The Company identifies potential storm events through continuous weather monitoring and initiates cross-functional planning that aligns generation, transmission, and distribution resources under a single, integrated operating model. During major storm events, these organizations operate in concert using a structured incident management approach to ensure safe operations, efficient resource deployment, and prioritized restoration. This end-to-end coordination allows the Company to support a reliable grid and restore service to customers as safely and efficiently as possible.
The Company follows a structured and comprehensive process for planning for, identifying, and responding to major storm events. This process was developed using successful past practice and industry best practices and has evolved over time. Prior to any storm, the Company assesses forecasts from internal and external meteorologists to evaluate the storm’s expected severity and potential impact areas. The Company compares these forecasts with historical system performance to estimate both transmission and distribution system impacts and anticipate the number and type of resources required. Based on this analysis, the Company pre-stages specialized resources such as transmission and distribution line crews, specialized off-road equipment capable of accessing remote rights-of-way, and materials needed to repair both transmission and distribution structures quickly and safely. Personnel resources are acquired in a prioritized manner beginning with Georgia Power crews and contractors, proceeding to affiliate company personnel, and finally mutual assistance crews from other utilities and contractors. 
When a storm strikes, damage assessment evaluators are pre-positioned to patrol affected areas as soon as conditions are safe. The initial damage assessments reveal the location and extent of damage and inform the first estimated restoration times for each affected area. Damage summaries are communicated to the Company’s Storm Center and area leadership, guiding decisions on resource allocation. As restoration progresses, the evaluators continue reassessing conditions to ensure resources align with evolving needs until all outages are resolved. Simultaneously, the logistics operation team oversees accommodation planning, vendor mobilization, and basecamp construction, ensuring that crews are safely housed, fed, fueled, and supported for the duration of restoration efforts.
Throughout the storm, our teams are focused on communicating progress and estimated times of restoration to customers, employees, community leaders, and government representatives. This coordinated process allows the Company to prepare for, respond to, and recover from major storm events efficiently and safely.
Q.WHAT TOOLS DOES THE COMPANY UTILIZE TO AID IN DETERMINING WHAT RESOURCES WILL BE NEEDED TO RESPOND TO A MAJOR STORM EVENT? 
A.The Company uses a variety of resources to ensure the safe and efficient restoration of power to customers following a major storm event. Once a resource need has been determined, a distribution resource management (“DRM”) tracking application is used to assign resources and ensure logistics are maintained for working teams. For major events such as Hurricane Helene, Georgia Power assesses resource requirements by referencing similar historical weather events from the DRM database. Based on the actual resources that were used in those prior storms and the projected impact of the impending storm, storm leadership estimates the resources required such as number of personnel, work location, and resource type. Other resources, such as vegetation trimming crews and damage assessment teams, are secured based on guidance from storm and local leadership. 
The Company continually works to improve its toolset in responding to storms. Two tools that were under development during Hurricane Helene that are now in use are Storm Prediction, Estimated Restoration Times (“ERTs”) and Reporting (“SPEAR”) and GridCritical. SPEAR is an advanced analytics platform that uses weather forecast models and historical data to predict distribution system impacts and estimate the impact of a storm event. GridCritical is a resource management tool that builds on the Company’s longstanding resource tracking practices and provides enhanced capabilities for coordinating crew movement and supporting logistical needs. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY WORK WITH OTHER UTILITIES TO FACILITATE STORM RESTORATION?
A.Yes. If a storm’s impact is predicted to require resources beyond what Georgia Power employs, utility assistance is acquired in a prioritized manner beginning with Georgia Power contractors, affiliate company personnel, and finally mutual assistance crews from other utilities and contractors. As part of the Southeastern Electric Exchange (“SEE”), Georgia Power collaborates with other utilities to address restoration of power following major storm events. Such assistance is essential and allows the Company to timely restore power when major storm events occur. The SEE is one of seven Regional Mutual Assistance Groups (“RMAGs”) in the United States. If an event impacts multiple utilities and requires a national response, other RMAGs are engaged through coordination with the SEE.
Hurricane Helene
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE HURRICANE HELENE.
A.Hurricane Helene made landfall in Florida as a Category 4 hurricane on September 26, 2024, at around 11 p.m., with maximum sustained winds of 140 mph. Helene then traveled to Georgia as a Category 2 storm with wide wind fields, barreling through the state and leaving behind significant devastation. Nearly two-thirds of the state bore the heaviest destruction from Helene, amounting to roughly 46,000 square miles in a line from Valdosta through Macon to Augusta. These areas saw tropical storm and hurricane force wind speeds and nearly 15 inches of rain in local areas. The National Weather Service also confirmed the presence of three tornadoes. The center of the storm passed through the state by the afternoon of September 27, 2024, leaving historic damage in its wake.
Q.CAN YOU ELABORATE ON THE DEVASTATION CAUSED BY HURRICANE HELENE?
A.With 41 counties declared major disaster sites by FEMA, Helene became the most destructive hurricane in Georgia Power’s 140-year history—causing more damage than Hurricanes Matthew (2016), Irma (2017) and Michael (2018) combined. Helene caused 1,544,132 customer outages, second only to Irma (1,633,800) in the Company’s recorded history. Over 62% of the Company’s customers were impacted by Hurricane Helene. The extent of the infrastructure damage and the storm’s breadth across numerous regions far exceeded the impacts of other major storm events in Company history. Additionally, leading up to the storm, a round of thunderstorms had impacted the state, causing scattered outages and saturated soils. Helene was also notable for the breadth of its wind field and for passing through the state with tropical storm and hurricane force winds, while dumping several inches of rain. While previous storms such as Matthew, Michael, Zeta (2020), and Idalia (2023) produced significant system damage, Helene stands apart due to the unique characteristics of the storm itself, infrastructure destruction, and customer impact. 
Q.WHAT LEVEL OF PHYSICAL DAMAGE DID HURRICANE HELENE CAUSE TO THE ELECTRICAL SYSTEM? 
A.Helene produced far greater physical damage than any storm in the Company’s historical record. The storm resulted in 12,239 broken poles and more than 20,000 downed spans of wire, more than Hurricanes Matthew, Irma, Michael, Zeta, and Idalia combined. Helene also generated 26,500 individual outage events, the most of any storm evaluated, underscoring both the geographic breadth and intensity of the system impacts. In sum, Helene caused dramatically higher infrastructure damage than any prior storms on the Company’s system, required the largest mobilization of resources in Company history, and produced a more protracted and complex restoration effort due in part to the geographic reach of its destruction.
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE GEORGIA POWER’S STORM RESTORATION EFFORTS IN CONNECTION WITH HURRICANE HELENE.
A.The restoration effort following Hurricane Helene was extreme in scale and coordination. The Company repaired or replaced 12,239 power poles, approximately 1,524 miles of power lines, and 4,968 transformers. In some areas, the damage was so severe that crews had to rebuild portions of the power grid from the ground up. The Company mobilized the largest restoration operation in its history. More than 20,000 personnel worked tirelessly to rebuild critical infrastructure and restore power to customers. This workforce represented a nationwide and international collaboration, with crews arriving from 35 states across the country and even from Canada, in addition to resources from our affiliate companies. 
The logistics operation supporting these personnel also required incredible planning and coordination. Across the state, our logistics teams established 34 cot sites and basecamps, providing accommodations for more than 20,000 restoration workers. These facilities were supported by hundreds of local vendors, ensuring that crews had access to meals, fuel, laundry, and other essential services necessary for sustained, safe, and efficient restoration work. The record scale of this logistical response was critical to maintaining continuous operations in the field.
Q.HOW LONG DID IT TAKE FOR THE COMPANY TO RESTORE POWER AFTER HURRICANE HELENE?
A.Because of the extensive destruction, the restoration timeline for Helene was materially longer than for other recent storms. But despite the massive levels of destruction, 50% of customers had power restored within two days, 75% had power restored within six days, and 95% of impacted customers had power restored within 10 days. Within the first 48 hours alone, the Company restored power to more than 800,000 customers.
Q.DID THE COMPANY MOBILIZE CONTRACT VENDORS IN RESPONDING TO HURRICANE HELENE?
A.Yes. Contract vendors were used to provide distribution and transmission craft labor, base camp equipment and manpower, vegetation management, damage assessment services, and other services.
Q.WHAT EQUIPMENT WAS NECESSARY TO COMPLETE THE RESTORATION EFFORTS AND HOW WAS IT DEPLOYED?
A.Equipment consisted of two categories: (1) vehicles assisting with the restoration of power and (2) equipment used for support purposes. The first includes operational restoration vehicles, such as bucket trucks, derricks, cranes, light- to medium-duty pickups, off-road utility task vehicles, heavy machinery, and all-terrain equipment capable of entering backyards or swamps. The second category comprises logistical assets, which encompass fleet and rental vehicles; generators for staging sites; fuel trucks and transports; service trucks; trailers for sleeping, showering, and laundry; as well as heavy support equipment like transport trailers used for moving machinery. Asset deployment was determined by crew requirements for operational repairs. Staging site support was allocated according to personnel numbers and available equipment in the region.
Q.HOW DID THE COMPANY ENSURE THAT CUSTOMERS RECEIVED UP-TO-DATE INFORMATION REGARDING HURRICANE HELENE?
A.In the aftermath of Hurricane Helene, the Company undertook a series of coordinated actions to ensure that customers received timely and accurate information regarding storm conditions, restoration progress, and estimated restoration times. These efforts spanned operational updates, customer-facing communications, and enhanced transparency through multiple channels. The Company improved the precision of ERTs by issuing area ERTs as soon as field conditions allowed. Instead of broad restoration times at the region level, the Company provided substation level and feeder level ERTs, enabling customers to receive highly localized information about when their service was expected to be restored. These ERTs were communicated through approximately 3.9 million proactive outage alerts, ensuring broad and rapid dissemination. 
The Company’s Corporate Affairs and Stakeholder Engagement teams also played a central role in maintaining public awareness before, during, and after the event. These teams engaged proactively with multiple media outlets to provide safety messaging, storm impact updates, and ongoing restoration information. Additionally, Georgia Power employees traveled in person to areas lacking phone or internet service to hand-deliver flyers reassuring customers that power restoration was underway and that they had not been forgotten. Communication teams delivered real-time content across social media channels, including preparation guidance, field level updates from restoration crews, and post-event messages expressing appreciation for customer patience. These coordinated efforts ensured that customers received clear, consistent, and frequent communication through the platforms they rely upon most. 
The Company worked to improve the customer experience on the Georgia Power Outage Map by refining the messaging displayed to customers during the event. Teams across Corporate Affairs and Power Delivery collaborated to enhance clarity, accuracy, and usefulness of outage information to further build customer confidence during the restoration period.
Finally, during the storm response, the Company prioritized customer safety and access to critical services. The Company utilized direct outreach to medically vulnerable customers. Specifically, Customer Care leaders contacted 71 customers who were dependent on life support equipment and coordinated the delivery and installation of generators at 13 locations for medically vulnerable customers experiencing extended outages. This ensured that these customers received personalized and immediate support. 
Q. WHAT STEPS DID GEORGIA POWER TAKE TO PROVIDE SUPPORT TO CUSTOMERS FOLLOWING THE DAMAGE CAUSED BY HURRICANE HELENE?
A.The Company understood that the significant impact of Hurricane Helene in the communities we serve would last even after power was restored. In that regard, the Company made a comprehensive effort to support our customers as they worked to get their lives back to normal. These efforts included suspending disconnections, late payment charges, and collection activities for impacted customers through December 31, 2024. The Company also offered enhanced installment payment plans and deferred balances for Prepay and Pay by Day customers. In total, the Company deferred over $107 million in payments and waived over $5.5 million in late payment charges and disconnection fees. More than 200,000 customers took advantage of payment plans. 
The Company also took additional steps to ensure that eligible customers were aware of energy assistance opportunities and resources. For example, Georgia Power customer service teams guided many customers through the process of participating in the federally funded Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (“LIHEAP”) as well as Georgia Power’s Income Qualified Discount to help with their utility bills.
Q.WHAT ADDITIONAL STEPS DID THE COMPANY TAKE TO SUPPORT THE COMMUNITIES MOST IMPACTED BY HELENE? 
A.Georgia Power, Southern Company, and Southern Company Gas charitable foundations collectively donated over $3 million to aid hurricane relief efforts, including $1.3 million donated by the Georgia Power Foundation to local organizations serving Georgia’s impacted communities. Georgia Power provided extensive support for immediate basic needs and essential resources throughout the affected communities and worked collaboratively with numerous community partners during and after Hurricane Helene to support recovery efforts. For example, the Company partnered with United Way of South Georgia to address immediate housing, food, and essential needs, and supported meal distribution through Second Harvest of South Georgia for residents affected by widespread power outages. The Company’s funding efforts also supported the Home Builders Association Hurricane Emergency Relief Project, which, in partnership with Performance Food Group, assembled and distributed food packages to families in need.
In the communities most heavily impacted by Helene, the Company also provided targeted, on-the-ground support that addressed immediate needs and helped stabilize local services. In Augusta, this included short-term relief funding for 11 community agencies; medium-term grants to organizations such as Paine College, Golden Harvest Food Bank, and the Community Foundation of the CSRA; and the launch of a long-term Community Resilient Hub to support customers during future disasters. In the Coastal region, Georgia Power supported eight relief agencies, deployed solar powered charging stations at shelters in Savannah, and directly reached customers in rural areas without communications service. Across South Georgia, the Company partnered with schools, food banks, local nonprofits, and universities to provide relief funding, support displaced students, sustain meal distribution, and assist critical local industries recovering from the storm.
Q.HAS THE COMPANY RECEIVED ANY RECOGNITION OR AWARDS FOR ITS RESPONSE TO HURRICANE HELENE? 
A.Yes. The Company was awarded the EEI Emergency Recovery Award for 2024. This award recognizes EEI member companies that make outstanding efforts to restore service promptly to the public following a storm or other disaster. The Company won this award for its efforts during and after Hurricane Helene.

IV. COST RECOVERY
Q.WHAT IS THE TOTAL AMOUNT OF THE STORM COST REGULATORY ASSET THAT GEORGIA POWER IS REQUESTING TO RECOVER IN THIS CASE?
A.Based on the ARP Stipulation, the Company will be allowed to recover actual reasonable and prudent storm damage cost incurred through December 31, 2025. The Company has been accruing approximately $31.4 million per year, as authorized by the Commission, to address costs related to storms during the ARP period, which included returning to customers the benefit of the projected over-recovered storm balance of approximately $100 million as of December 2022. Since the approval of the 2022 ARP, Georgia Power has quickly responded to safely restore service to the communities we serve following significant storms, most notably Hurricane Helene, resulting in an under-recovered storm damage balance of $912 million as of December 31, 2025. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE GENERAL PROCESS OF ACCOUNTING FOR STORM COSTS.
A.Georgia Power’s Storm Accounting Guidelines establish a consistent framework for recording costs associated with storm restoration to the Company’s Distribution, Transmission, and Generation systems in the event of a major storm and other emergency situations. Major storms are defined as a storm where the cost to repair or restore the system to normal operating conditions exceeds a defined level of incremental costs, including labor, materials, and other directly related incremental costs.
Once a storm declaration is issued, unique projects and tasks are established that provide the ability to correctly record restoration costs to either operations and maintenance (“O&M”) or the storm damage regulatory asset. This information is communicated to all relevant leadership and personnel engaged in storm restoration as soon as it is practical and reasonable, detailing the relevant accounting codes and established guidelines.
These guidelines outline the required accounting controls, including detailed account coding by cost category. In addition, they provide a straightforward narrative outline of expenses that qualify for storm-related treatment. Further, the guidelines provide direction for accounting for capital work, as well as both field and management responsibility, among other things.
Q.WHAT CATEGORIES OF STORM DAMAGE COST ARE EXCLUDED FROM THE STORM DAMAGE REGULATORY ASSET?
A.The Company does not include in the regulatory asset base distribution and transmission costs or other major storm restoration work charges that are not incremental in nature. These costs include transmission and distribution straight time labor and associated fleet costs. All Company straight time labor and related payroll expenses during storm restoration, including any related to the repair of transmission and distribution lines, facilities, and equipment, are expensed to O&M accounts and are not eligible to be deferred to the storm reserve.
In addition, all capital asset costs are excluded from the storm regulatory asset. Capital plant assets associated with meters and transformers during storm restoration are instead recorded directly to the plant accounts when purchased. The remaining capital costs initially recorded to the storm regulatory asset are later identified and transferred from the storm regulatory asset to the appropriate capital plant asset accounts following the storm based on the requirements per the Company’s capitalization policy, which is consistent with U.S. GAAP, ASC 360.
Q.WHAT ARE THE PRIMARY CATEGORIES OF STORM DAMAGE EXPENSES INCLUDED IN THE REGULATORY ASSET?
A.The primary categories of storm damage expenses included in the regulatory asset are internal labor and fleet, outside services, materials and supplies, and other expenses, as shown on MFR 3. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE WHAT MAKES UP THE CATEGORY OF INTERNAL LABOR AND FLEET.
A.Internal labor and fleet costs included in the storm regulatory asset are made up of Company overtime and excess straight time and associated payroll taxes, incentive pay related to covered employee overtime, as well as allocated fleet charges related to Company equipment that is used in support of storm restoration activities.
Q. PLEASE DESCRIBE WHAT MAKES UP THE CATEGORY OF OUTSIDE SERVICES.
A. This category includes all contract labor and outside vendors that are used during storm restoration. Contract labor is made up of external resources, such as distribution line maintenance and construction, transmission construction, vegetation management, hydro vac services, and damage assessment. Other outside vendors include base camp vendors, catering, security, traffic control, and medical support.
Q. PLEASE DESCRIBE WHAT MAKES UP THE CATEGORY OF MATERIALS AND SUPPLIES.
A. Materials and supplies, as shown in MFR 3 and MFR 4, are Company materials that include, but are not limited to, non-capital items such as brackets, nuts, bolts, fuses, connectors and other miscellaneous hardware. 
Q. PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COSTS THAT ARE INCLUDED IN “OTHER EXPENSES.”
A.Other expenses include meals and travel expenses such as lodging, rental cars, mileage, and meals for Company crews. Also included in this category are supplies such as drinks, ice, and other consumables needed during storm response, as well as certain technological services to help support communication internally as well as within the impacted communities.
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY ROUTINELY VERIFY THAT THE EXPENSES IN THESE CATEGORIES ARE APPROPRIATELY INCLUDED IN THE REGULATORY ASSET FOR STORM DAMAGE? 
A.When a storm declaration is issued, unique projects and tasks are created and communicated to all relevant leadership and personnel engaged in storm restoration. Only incremental costs are allowed to be charged to the storm regulatory asset. As part of the Company’s monthly financial review, costs are reviewed by resource type to ensure that non-incremental costs are properly expensed as O&M and not charged to the regulatory asset. Costs that are incorrectly charged are reclassified from the storm reserve to O&M as needed. 
Q.DID THE COMPANY PERFORM A REVIEW TO ENSURE THE COSTS RELATED TO HURRICANE HELENE WERE APPROPRIATELY ACCOUNTED FOR IN THE STORM REGULATORY ASSET?
A.Yes. Due to the magnitude of storm costs related to Hurricane Helene, the Southern Company Services Internal Audit organization performed an extensive review in 2025 to ensure proper governance was in place and appropriate processes were followed to determine whether the charges in the storm regulatory asset related to Hurricane Helene were accurate. This included reviewing the Georgia Power storm reserve governance processes and procedures (including potential regulatory requirements), performing walkthroughs with responsible personnel, accounting of and categorizing storm reserve expense data, understanding processes for identifying and re-classifying capital expenditures, reviewing internal and third-party expenses, and reviewing accruals and associated processes. In addition, as part of the management response, the Company completed a review of thousands of additional invoices and expense statements to further validate the storm costs.
Q.HOW DID THE REVIEW OF STORM COSTS IMPACT THE STORM REGULATORY ASSET BALANCE?
A.Based on the findings from the review and the additional work performed by the Company, $3.9 million in the storm regulatory asset balance was identified to be base labor or non-storm related charges. This amount was reclassified to O&M expense in 2025.
Revenue Requirement
Q.WHAT IS THE TEST PERIOD USED FOR THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT IN THIS FILING?
A.The test period in the revenue requirement for this filing is June 1, 2026, through May 31, 2027, with new rates effective June 1, 2026.
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE HOW THE COMPANY FORECASTED THE STORM COSTS FOR THE TEST PERIOD.
A.Consistent with prior base rate cases, a 10-year historical average of storm costs ending December 2025 was used to project storm costs for the test period for the filing. However, given the historical magnitude of the $781 million in costs associated with Hurricane Helene, the Company made an adjustment within its 10-year average to reduce the impact of Hurricane Helene to match the costs of the previous largest major storm events, which was approximately $125 million for a single major storm. As a result, the Company projects average annual storm costs of $101 million, as shown in MFR 2. 
Q.DOES THIS APPROACH RISK FUTURE UNDER-RECOVERIES RESULTING FROM MAJOR STORM EVENTS?
A. There is always risk that major storms exceed the historical averages—as has occurred with Helene—but the Company’s risk-adjusted approach to setting the accrual in this case reasonably increases the forecasted costs for major storm events without being overly reliant on a single storm to set the accrual.
Q.WHAT AMORTIZATION PERIOD IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING FOR THE STORM COSTS?
A.The Company is proposing to recover the $912 million under-recovered balance as of December 31, 2025, over a four-year period ending May 31, 2030. This amortization results in an annual recovery of $228 million, as shown in MFR 7.1. Given the magnitude of the storm regulatory asset, the four-year recovery period balances the timely recovery of significant storm costs already incurred with affordability for customers. 
Q.WHAT IS THE CHANGE IN PROPOSED ANNUAL ACCRUAL OF STORM COSTS AND THE RESULTING REVENUE REQUIREMENT RELATED TO THE STORM RECOVERY?
A.As explained in previous responses, the Company is proposing to increase the amount accrued annually from $31.4 million to $329.2 million, as shown in MFR 7.1, based upon the updated 10-year historical average of storm damage costs and recovery of previously under-recovered storm costs. The change in annual accrual, combined with the return on the storm regulatory asset balance, produces a total revenue requirement deficiency of $360 million for the test period, as shown in Exhibit___(CLF/JJS/MSB) Page 1. 
Q.IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING ANY ADJUSTMENTS TO THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT PRESENTED IN THIS FILING?
A.Yes. The Company is proposing to apply a portion of the amortization of PTCs generated in 2024 and 2025 associated with its nuclear generation facilities under Internal Revenue Code (“IRC”) 45U during the test period to lower the revenue requirement deficiency associated with storms for the benefit of customers. 
Q.WERE THESE PTCS CONTEMPLATED IN THE ARP STIPULATION?
A.Yes. Under the ARP Stipulation, any PTCs generated in 2024 and 2025 were to be deferred to a regulatory liability and amortized over a three-year period beginning January 1, 2026. Thus, the PTCs are currently being utilized as a benefit to customers by allowing the Company to keep rates stable during the period that the ARP is extended. The ARP Stipulation did not provide for these PTCs to be applied to the revenue requirement for the recovery of storms in this filing. However, to address affordability for our customers, and to provide a net overall decrease in rates coupled with the fuel rate decrease in the concurrent fuel filing, the Company is proposing to provide the PTC benefit to customers by using this benefit to directly reduce the revenue requirement necessary to recover the under-recovered storm cost balance.
Q.WHAT IS THE IMPACT TO THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT AS A RESULT OF THE APPLICATION OF PTCS?
A.As of December 2025, the Company recorded $193 million and $72 million for IRC 45U PTCs generated in 2024 and 2025, respectively, for a total of $265 million. To monetize the PTCs, the Company entered into a sale of a portion of the PTCs, which will result in net sale proceeds of $261 million, as shown in MFR 7.2. The amount generated for 2025 is still subject to Internal Revenue Service approval and will be finalized when the Company files its 2025 tax return in the fall of 2026. Instead of recognizing the PTC benefit over the three-year period through 2028 as approved in the ARP Stipulation, the Company is proposing to amortize the tax credits within the test period, with 25% of its total generated in 2024 and 2025, or approximately $65 million, applied to partially offset and lower the revenue requirement deficiency associated with storm recovery in this filing for the benefit of customers. When combined, the total revenue requirement deficiency requested in this filing is $270 million as presented in Exhibit___(CLF/JJS/MSB) Page 1. Based on the ARP Stipulation, the rate adjustment will be applied to each traditional base rate on an equal percentage basis. This will result in an increase of $4.42 monthly to the typical residential customer using 1,000 kWh and a 2.2% increase in total retail rates. 
Q.DID THE COMPANY CONSIDER INSURANCE OR OTHER RESOURCES, SUCH AS FEDERAL FUNDING, TO POTENTIALLY OFFSET COST INCURRED FOR HURRICANE HELENE?
A.Yes. The Company considered filing a claim under its property insurance policy, which provides coverage for damage to equipment, including storm-related damage. This includes assets such as plants, substations, headquarters, and similar facilities. However, transmission and distribution (“T&D”) lines and poles are excluded from insurance coverage other than limited de minimis T&D coverage within 1,000 feet of insured properties, such as substations. For damage to insured assets under the storm events, the amount does not exceed the policy deductible. Therefore, no claim was filed. 
The Company also considered funds from the Federal Emergency Management Agency (“FEMA”) Disaster Assistance program. However, FEMA funds are only available for not-for-profit entities, and accordingly, the Company did not qualify for assistance for FEMA funds. 
V. STORM COST REQUEST SUMMARY
Q. PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COMPANY’S REQUEST IN THIS CASE. 
A.The Company has an obligation to serve our customers, which we take seriously and responsibly. The costs requested by the Company for recovery in this case are prudently incurred costs to provide and restore service to our customers as safely and efficiently as possible. Without the extensive efforts of the Company to restore service to customers following Hurricane Helene, many communities across Georgia would have endured sustained periods without electric service. The steps the Company took and the corresponding costs incurred were necessary costs of service and, as such, the Company is appropriately seeking recovery.
Q.HOW DOES APPROVAL OF THE COMPANY’S REQUEST IN THIS CASE ADDRESS AFFORDABILITY AND RELIABILITY FOR CUSTOMERS?
A.The Company is continually balancing the need to keep customer rates affordable with the non-negotiable need for reliable service, which also includes being prepared to respond to any major weather events. Starting with the ARP Stipulation, the Company, with the support of the Commission, has actively taken steps to ensure affordability for our customers during a period of increasing costs for all goods and services. Although the storm damage costs sought in this case must be recovered, as acknowledged by the ARP Stipulation approved by the Commission, the Company is proposing to apply to the revenue requirement of the storm damage under-recovered balance a portion of the PTC benefits which are being utilized to keep base rates stable under the ARP Extension. Thus, allowing the Company to lower the revenue requirements needed to recover the storm damage cost incurred by the Company as discussed in this case. Moreover, the Company has timed this request with its fuel cost adjustment request such that the net result of both requests will reduce what customers pay for their electricity. Combined, the rate adjustments proposed in the storm filing and the fuel rate filing will result in a net bill reduction of $1.32 a month for the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh. Overall rates for total retail customers will decrease 1.0%. This collective result is consistent with the steps the Commission and the Company have already taken by extending the ARP to allow customers to continue to benefit from stable electricity rates while providing safe and reliable service to Georgia Power customers.

A.Yes.

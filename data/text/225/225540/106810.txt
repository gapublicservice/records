Response:

RTP-DA prices are based on projections of the hourly running cost of incremental generation (including approved environmental costs) known as lambda, provisions for losses, projections of hourly transmission costs and reliability capacity costs for each day (when applicable), and a three (3) mill/kWh recovery factor. 

The hourly running cost is determined by the unit commitment production cost model by optimizing the commitment and dispatch of generating resources to meet our load obligations and other requirements. 

For the reliability capacity costs, the respective amount is included when system loads are greater than the 50th highest forecasted load for the year. These costs are designed to be recovered over these 50 hours in a weather normal year. For the transmission capacity costs, the respective amount is included when system loads are greater than the 300th highest forecasted load for the year adjusted for ambient temperature. These costs are designed to be recovered over these 300 hours in a weather normal year.   

Losses are accounted for in lambda, along with the reliability and transmission components (when applicable). 

The three (3) mill/kWh recovery factor is the same in all hours. 
 
Please see STF-JKA-1-4 Attachment A in the Company’s response to STF-JKA-1-4 for how the hourly RTP prices were derived for the two peak price hours of the summer and winter periods of the year 2025. 

The three (3) mill/kWh recovery factor is the same in all hours to account for forecast risk and to offset other fixed costs. The value was originally approved in Docket 11708-U.

STF-JKA-1-7

Question: 

For RTP customers who have a Customer Base Line (CBL) and an RTP billing component:
a.Since 2015, please provide a timeline describing changes to the CBL and RTP billing process, derivation of rates, and/or eligibility.
b.How does the Company verify that RTP customers pay a fair amount for the fuel costs the Company incurs in order to serve their load?
c.Provide any memos, reports, calculations, and any other documentation that exists that supports the reasonableness of the approach that the Company uses.
d.Explain how RTP customers are assessed for costs such as firm transportation costs that are fixed and not necessarily reflected in marginal cost rates. How does the Company validate that the revenues collected cover the costs incurred to serve their load.

Response:

Since 2015, the only changes in the applicability of the Real Time Pricing (“RTP”) tariff are those approved by the Georgia Public Service Commission in Docket No. 42516 (Georgia Power’s 2019 Base Rate Case). Below is a summary of those changes:
Clarified what is considered normal operation, existing load, and new load.
Clarified conditions under which CBL reductions are allowed. 
Increased the time an existing RTP customer must remain off RTP before returning to RTP to 24 months.
Eliminated the RTP-DAA (Day Ahead with Adjustable CBL) and RTP-HAA (Hour Ahead with Adjustable CBL) tariffs. 
Converted Multiple Load Management (MLM) customer CBLs to TOU-RN due to the elimination of the MLM tariff.

The Company accounts for fuel costs from RTP customers based on the methodology approved by the Commission in Docket No. 31958 (Georgia Power’s 2010 Base Rate Case). For the load priced at RTP, the average hourly marginal fuel cost is credited to the fuel account. This average hourly marginal fuel cost is based on all Georgia Power generating units running in each hour plus power pool purchases or deliveries in each hour. This method allocates the appropriate RTP revenue to fuel when fuel prices are increasing and less when they are decreasing, which mirrors the concepts behind the RTP rates and recognizes the fuel revenue with fuel costs attributable to RTP customers. For the CBL portion of RTP customer load, FCR is billed and collected as applicable to the underlying CBL rate. 

Please see the response to STF-JKA-1-7 (b) above. The calculation of the RTP fuel revenue allocation is described in the Company’s response to STF-JKA-1-4.

RTP customers’ CBL load is served on a rate that covers the embedded cost to serve, including fixed costs. The CBL load is also assessed the FCR rate for fuel costs. The incremental load is assessed a dynamic price that is designed to cover both fixed and variable marginal costs. The RTP price includes lambda (the system’s marginal cost of energy), the reliability and transmission adders, which are triggered during peak load hours and designed to recover the marginal costs associated with peaking generation capacity and transmission capacity, respectively, as well as losses and an approved recovery factor.

The marginal cost of energy (lambda) covers multiple costs. A portion of that component of RTP price, the average marginal fuel cost of all Georgia Power units running in each hour, covers fuel costs and is accounted for as fuel revenue. The difference between lambda and the allocated fuel revenue covers two pieces: generation variable O&M and capital substitution. The capital substitution contribution covers additional fixed costs associated with resources that support lower fuel costs, including capital costs for baseload generation and firm transportation (FT).

In developing and applying the marginal cost models used in economic analysis, the Company verifies the underlying principle behind the capital substitution component. Peaker theorem states that the total marginal cost of serving incremental load in an optimally planned system can be reliably calculated as the marginal running cost in every hour (lambda) and the capital costs of peaking generation. This will be true regardless of the resources that are deployed to serve that load in an economically optimal matter. The economic justification for building a more fixed cost intensive form of generation and associated FT would be that the resulting energy benefit outweighs the additional capital costs.

When conducting forward looking economic analysis, the Company validates that projected revenues cover fixed and variable marginal costs to serve a customer’s load by ensuring projects pass the Rate Impact Measure (RIM). Additionally, a Cost of Service Study filed in a base rate case includes the actual revenues,  loads, and costs to determine rate of return and associated parity.

Each unit is grouped based on unit type and/or capacity, and the average for each group is used for the forecast. The outage rate modeled is EUOR, or Equivalent Unplanned Outage Rate, which accounts for both forced outages and maintenance outages.  Note, AURORAxmp requires a separate input for planned outages, which should not be confused with maintenance outages (see NERC definitions for forced, maintenance, and planned outages if further clarification is needed).

For planned outages, the current schedule is used with specific dates and durations by unit. Each year, Maintenance Planning and Resource Planning work together to optimize the planned outage schedule for the generating units of the Southern electric system. The duration and timing of the planned outages used in AURORAxmp for the Georgia Power units result from this system level optimization effort, which is described below. 
 
The plants provide the proposed outage duration, the year of occurrence, and the season of occurrence for each outage. The plant may specify a specific outage start date if a major construction or retrofit project schedule requires a prior outage to complete specific activities. The information provided by the plants is reviewed by Maintenance Planning for business case and conformance to system philosophy.   
 
An “optimized” start date for each unit’s outage is determined considering maintenance requirements, unit operating parameters, marginal fuel cost, reserve requirements, loads (retail and wholesale), craft labor availability, and area stability and reliability, with the objective of minimizing system operational cost.   
 
The resulting preliminary schedule is reviewed by Maintenance Planning, plant personnel, and Fleet Optimization to ensure that none of the constraints have been violated, and budgets align with outages. Engineering judgment is applied, as needed, and the result is an official planned outage schedule for use in subsequent AURORAxmp model studies.   
 
The planned outage schedule is a living document, in that changes are made to it, as needed, following the optimization process. Planned outages are added, removed, and changed throughout the year as the need arises. The latest version of the planned outage schedule is utilized whenever a new AURORAxmp model study is performed.

===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-PIA-3 PD/STF-PIA-3-10.docx =====
STF-PIA-3-10
Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document p. 104 regarding Georgia Power’s proposed Residential and Commercial Solar Plus Storage Pilot Program, Company-Directed Model:
a. Provide all workpapers and analysis in a working Excel spreadsheet that were used to determine the minimum number of hours called in a year, max event duration, both options of upfront incentives, 20% state of charge minimum, capacity value, and term length. 
b. Provide additional information on the restrictions on eligible technologies or configurations that may apply as referenced on page 104.
c. Please explain why the contract is specific to a location and not a customer.
d. Provide all feedback that was received by the Company from interested parties that were considered in the development of this pilot.
Response:
Please see STF-PIA-3-8 Attachment B TRADE SECRET.

The eligible technologies or configurations must meet the Company’s Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) Interconnection policy. Additional technical requirements and restrictions will be drafted in coordination with the third-party facilitator selected to implement the program. Specifically, for the Company-Directed model, more granular or continuous communication capabilities will be required to ensure effective management and integration of the assets into the system. 

The contract is specific to a location rather than a customer to mitigate financial risk associated with customer relocation. As incentives are calculated based on the system value over a 10-year period and paid upfront, the agreement should stay in place for the entire term in order to mitigate the risk of additional costs to non-participating customers if the participant moves before the end of the term. By tying the contract to a specific location, the Company ensures that the capacity and associated benefits remain consistent and reliable, regardless of changes in customer residency.

Please refer to the response to STF-PIA-3-9 (d). For additional information, please see page 41, lines 1-23 of the Direct Testimony of Dr. Ross Beppler, Carley Goff, A. Wilson Mallard, and Andy Phillips in Docket Nos. 56002 and 56003.


===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-PIA-3 PD/STF-PIA-3-11.docx =====
STF-PIA-3-11
Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 105 regarding Georgia Powers’ Customer Connected Solar Program Enhancements:
a. Please describe in detail why the Company is proposing to change the minimum and maximum system size for this program.
b. Has the Company updated the customer agreements and/or program documentation for the proposed CCSP? If so, please provide.
c. Describe any limitations to the BESS technology type or usage as referenced on page 105.
d. Provide all feedback received by the Company from interested parties that were considered in the proposed changes for the Customer Connected Solar Program.
e. Please provide all Georgia Power communications/customer logs with interested customers. Please specify the status of where each customer is at in the screening process, customer name, and potential size.
Response:
The Company seeks to update the eligible project size range in the Customer Connected Solar Program (“CCSP”) Program Guidelines for consistency with the DG RFP, the program after which CCSP was modeled. This range of system size provides customers with more options for facility design. Additionally, expanding the project size range to allow projects as small as 250 kW, provides seamless access to solar plus storage program options for customer projects of all sizes. 

The Company has not yet updated the power purchase agreement (“PPA”) or Program Guidelines for CCSP. The Company will work with Staff to file and seek approval for the modified CCSP PPA and Program Guidelines from the Commission after the conclusion of the 2025 IRP.

The Company is still evaluating options for incorporating battery energy storage system (“BESS”) technology into CCSP and has not established any limitations for BESS technology that would be incorporated into CCSP. The solar and storage assets must comply with the Company’s Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) Interconnection Policy. Further technical requirements may be proposed. Regarding BESS usage, the storage component of an eligible facility would be subject to control by the Company through integration with DERMS; such control provides the capacity value that can then be translated into the customer’s compensation for the facility’s output.

The Company’s proposed program enhancements to CCSP are based on feedback realized throughout administration of CCSP as well as through dedicated market feedback sessions held in 2024. See below for the feedback received in the 2024 market feedback sessions.
On 9/11/2024, GPC met with multiple customers and received feedback summarized below:
The limitation on facility size due to peak demand doesn’t account for customer’s other accounts.
Customer would like ability to build larger sites.
Higher CCSP pricing will likely increase the feasibility of customer sited solar projects.
On 9/25/2024, GPC met with a solar developer and received the following recommendation:
Allow the pricing to be updated on a regular basis
Additional feedback received from interested customers, new customers constructing facilities in Georgia, and installers includes comments on the following topics:
Current program limits the facility location to parcels directly adjacent and contiguous to their premises; interested customers are not able install facilities on other land they own. 
The current CCSP precludes new customers from participating in the program because a participant must have “one or more existing Georgia Power meter(s) on its Premises at least six months prior to applying for the program.” 
Current program is designed such that the Georgia Power account holder is required to be the solar contract counterparty. Therefore, landlords are unable to participate in CCSP and lack flexibility to transfer RECs, hindering customers participation.

The Company does not maintain an exhaustive list of customers interested in CCSP. STF-PIA-3-11 Attachment TRADE SECRET provides examples of previously and currently interested customers, including customer name, potential facility size, and additional notes as available.

STF-PIA-3-12
Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 107 regarding the proposed Customer-Owned Resiliency Program:
a. Describe which commercial and industrial customers would qualify for the program.
b. Describe in detail how the Company will select customers for the program. 
c. Provide proposed tariffs and any other applicable program documentation.
d. Describe if there has been any recent outreach to potential customers. If there is interest in the program, please provide the number of customers with interest in the program, the size of each customer’s load, and any feedback that the Company has received from these customers. 
Response:
Transmission connected customers adding dispatchable, non-emergency resources with a firm fuel supply are eligible for the program as proposed. 

Customers with resiliency needs or otherwise considering on-site generation may contact the Company for information on participating in the Large Customer-Owned Resiliency Program. The Company will also proactively target larger sites to provide economies of scale in managing interconnection and operations. Qualifying customers that enter a program agreement with the Company will be enrolled subject to the system peaking capacity needs and Commission oversight of program size. 

The Company has not yet drafted the proposed tariffs or  program documentation. If the program is approved, Georgia anticipates submitting the tariffs and related program documents  to the Commission for approval following the conclusion of the 2025 IRP. 

The Company has spoken to several large load customers, including data centers, who have expressed interest in a program where customers can own generators and accelerate the timeframe in which resources can be interconnected and associated capacity contributions reflected. The Company is developing an outreach strategy to engage additional potential customers.

STF-PIA-3-13
Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 109. Please provide all presentations regarding the Company’s plans to evaluate the V2X technology through a pilot, starting with public school systems. Please provide the Company’s proposed budget and spending plan for the pilot. How many public schools does the Company plan to include in the pilot?
Response:
The Company intends to pilot the V2X technology, utilizing up to ten bidirectional chargers located at up to four public school system locations. The spending plan for 2026-2028 is roughly $2M to cover the cost of labor, electrical equipment and chargers, materials, installation and interconnection software. To date, no presentations have been made regarding the pilot; however, Georgia Power will provide the Commission with more details on the evaluation framework upon approval of the pilot.

STF-PIA-3-14
Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 109. In the 2025 IRP, what specific approval is the Company requesting from the Commission regarding the Company’s implementation of its proposed DERMS? What are the costs related to the DERMS implementation that have not yet been approved by the Commission?
Response:
In the 2025 IRP, the Company is requesting approval from the Commission to continue the implementation and integration of its proposed Distributed Energy Resource Management System (“DERMS”). Specifically, Georgia Power is seeking approval for the enhanced control of DER devices in addition to visibility and forecasting capabilities as previously approved in the 2022 Rate Case Order. 

As approved by the Commission in the 2022 Rate Case Order, the Company began work towards planning and implementation of DERMS through the execution of a request for information (“RFI”) and request for proposals (“RFP”). Upon conclusion of the RFP in Q2 2024, the Company worked jointly with its subject matter experts and with the DERMS vendor to develop a detailed design and implementation plan. DERMS implementation began in Q4 2024, and the Company currently expects to spend approximately REDACTED through the end of 2025. Further work is required to fully implement and integrate DERMS with the Company’s real-time operations platforms and enable the enhanced control operational capabilities as defined in the 2025 IRP. The Company expects to spend approximately REDACTED from 2026 through 2028 to complete this work. 

Please refer to STF-PIA-3-15 Attachment D TRADE SECRET and STF-PIA-3-15 Attachment E for detailed timeline and breakdown of DERMS costs.

===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-PIA-3 PD/STF-PIA-3-18.docx =====
STF-PIA-3-18
Question:
Please provide any customer feedback to support the requested modification to DCO-1.
Response:
Initial customer feedback on the DER Customer Owned (“DCO”) Program suggested that upfront costs for program participation, which included incremental generator, switchgear, and interconnection costs were limiting factors to the overall value proposition. When considering whether to participate in the program, the uncertainty of value beyond 2031 inhibited customer willingness to make those additional upfront investments. Therefore, the Company is requesting to modify the program’s maximum term length from “through 2031” to “up to 15 years,” which will help to enhance value to participating customers while maintaining benefits for all Georgia Power customers.

            Capacity Credit Valuation
      CEBA members are interested in investing in carbon free capacity resources in addition to renewables to help meet
      decarbonization goals. Large customers can invest in capacity resources in return for OpEx savings, which can be
      applied as a credit to their monthly invoice to offset demand charges.

      Credits should have an annual escalation value applied to the credit.

      The credit can be calculated in several ways:
        1. Capacity cost based on the least-cost alternative (gas-fired generation). This is an all-inclusive calculation to
            cover the investment, required returns, OpEx, ongoing CapEx, and fuel costs.
        2. Capacity cost based on system value. This is a calculation based on the specific characteristics of the asset. The
            calculation could be an annual value. For example, a battery could be based on the cost of capacity at peak
            conditions (summer afternoons and winter mornings). The price would be the cost of capacity in the market
            (neighboring systems) at peak conditions.
        3. Capacity could be an average annual capacity value. This could be an annualized capacity cost for the GPC
            system.

      In addition, different credits can be provided to two types of resources:
         1. Additional Resource Capacity Credit: Resource with grid capacity that is over and above what GPC had planned.
            This would be a full capacity credit, calculated by one of the capacity options outlined above.
         2. Grid Resource Capacity: GPC planned resource that is replaced with a clean alternative because customers
            cover the premium. The credit could be based on the contribution percentage relative to the total resource cost.

      The customer investments enable the resource deployment, so the capacity credit should be available for the life of
      the asset, provided the customer remains a GPC customer.

            Example: Louisiana PSC Sleeved PPA Rule

          In August 2024, The Louisiana Public Service Commission issued a rule allowing for sleeved PPAs in an
          order in Docket R-35462. Commercial and industrial customers are now able to request a “wholesale
          pass-through sleeved PPA” from utilities in Louisiana. Customers can negotiate directly with a
          renewable provider and bring a renewable energy project to the utility. Unlike other sleeved PPA green
          tariff programs offered by a specific utility, customers in Louisiana can request a sleeved PPA from their
          utility, and the utility is then required to develop a tariff and submit it for PSC approval.
          • Additional details on pages 17-23 of this document.

          Definitions
          • Sleeved PPA: A Power Purchase Agreement for the capacity, energy, and renewable attributes of a
             Designated Renewable Generator that is negotiated between an Eligible Customer and a Designated
             Renewable Provider and brought to the Applicable Utility for execution by the Eligible Customer, the
             Designated Renewable Provider, and the Applicable Utility
          • Wholesale Pass-Through Sleeved PPA: A Sleeved PPA that provides financial costs and benefits to
             an Eligible Customer while not affecting any aspects of the Eligible Customer’s Electric Service
             Agreement with the Applicable Utility or the Applicable Utility’s rate schedule under which the
             Eligible Customer takes service.

Question:
Refer to Technical Appendix Vol. 2, the 2024 Renewable Integration Study, p. 7, stating: “An additional 6th scenario was run at 5 GW, but a mitigation cost could not be directly determined due to several factors.”

Response:
Page 25 of the 2024 Renewable Integration Study provides further information regarding the 5 GW solar tranche. The base case model required 500 MW of quick start reserves be maintained as well as a load following target equal to 1% of hourly load in order to manage normal System volatility and contingencies. The combined flexibility of these existing resources was able to absorb the solar volatility present in the 5 GW penetration. However, the model does not report how much of the inherent flexibility of the System was eroded by the solar volatility. As outlined on page 13 of the Study, intra-hour solar volatility profiles were incorporated into the model to properly simulate this intra-hour volatility. The fact that the model did not identify additional flex violations surpassing the base case implies that the existing resources in the model resolved the added volatility. However, the flexibility or ramping capability provided by the existing generation in the System is needed to address intra-hour load volatility and other operational uncertainties. This existing System flexibility was not intended to manage solar volatility. Permitting solar volatility to diminish current system flexibility jeopardizes the System’s ability to effectively manage load and operational variability.

No documentation or analysis was provided from the consultant that performed the Study since the unmitigated 5 GW solar scenario had a flex violation metric equal to the base case.

STF-PIA-3-32
Question:
Refer to Technical Appendix Vol. 2, the 2024 Renewable Integration Study, p. 14, describing the “divergence profile” as “the frequency at which a given level of divergence from the smooth profile would occur.” Please provide a narrative description of what a “smooth profile” is and how the Company calculates divergence from this smooth profile.
Response:
The smooth profile in the model consists of the hourly solar output provided for each weather year, weather station, and technology (fixed or tracking). A single-year, five-minute aggregate profile was developed as described on p.14 of the 2024 Renewable Integration Study. This single-year volatility profile is then stochastically applied to the smooth profiles. This is done by SERVM randomly selecting a volatility value from an hour in the single-year divergence data that is like the hour being simulated. For example, the volatility for modeled hours where solar generation is ~90% of the nameplate will draw from hours where the solar generation was ~90% of the nameplate in the divergence data.
The divergence profiles are modeled on an aggregate portfolio basis, and a pro-rata share of the divergence is applied to every individual solar resource to determine its final output in each five-minute span. While some resources would have positive divergence and other resources negative divergence at a given point in time, the modeling is only concerned with the aggregate divergence.

Question:
Refer to Technical Appendix Vol. 2, the 2024 Renewable Integration Study, p. 30, stating: “Below 1,250 MW, BESS effectiveness is significantly hindered because of regulation and spin requirements.”
a. Please elaborate on this conclusion and explain how the regulation and spin requirements hinder BESS effectiveness.
b. Provide all analysis supporting this conclusion.
Response:
The SERVM model is set up to maintain at least 1,250 MW of regulation plus spinning reserves in all hours pursuant to the operating reserve requirements discussed in the responses to STF-PIA-3-28 and STF-PIA-3-29. If operating reserves drop below 1,250 MW during intra-hour, a flex violation is recorded. If there is not sufficient hourly capacity to maintain 1,250 MW of reserves, the SERVM model will shed load in order to recover reserves back to 1,250 MW. To maintain this 1,250 MW threshold, the SERVM model will allocate resource capacity to fulfill this obligation during each hour. Due to the flexibility of BESS resources, the SERVM model prioritizes these resources to meet these operating reserves. If BESS capacity is assigned to provide these capacity reserves, that same capacity is then unavailable to provide intra-hour volatility support.

This model configuration is intentional. Accordingly, additional analysis is not available.


===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-PIA-3 PD/STF-PIA-3-43.docx =====
STF-PIA-3-43
Question:
Refer to Technical Appendix Vol. 2, the 2024 Renewable Integration Study, p. 22, stating: “The costs associated with increases in curtailment are implicitly included in these calculations since the production costs associated with the additional energy that was generated but did not serve load are included in TPCpost.” Please reconcile this statement with the discussion of solar curtailment on p. 31, stating that the cost of curtailment “is not captured in the solar integration cost analysis.”
Response:
The Renewable Integration Study differentiates between two forms of solar curtailment, that which is present in the the pre-mitigated case and the increase in curtailment present in the mitigated case. Solar curtailment that is present in the pre-mitigated case is a function of commitment constraints that result in total generation exceeding total demand. Under this condition, the model will curtail solar in order to balance generation and demand. The cost impact of this curtailment is not included in the solar integration cost. Solar curtailment may increase between the pre-mitigated case and the mitigated case. This increase in curtailment is a function of the mitigation creating additional commitment constraints in order to provide the needed load following reserves. The cost impacts of this curtailment are included in the solar integration cost analysis.

Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 103 regarding Georgia Power’s Residential and Commercial Solar Plus Storage Pilot Program options:
Please describe how the proposed 50 MW Pilot cap was determined. Did the Company examine other Pilot amounts? With the proposed DERMS, why should there be a limit for participation in this pilot?
Describe how the maximum system sizes of 20 kW and 250 kW for residential and commercial applications respectively were determined.
Describe what the “appropriate application and interconnection requirements” are referring to.
Why is the Company proposing to require Pilot participants to take service on a rate other than R or GS?
Provide internal Georgia Power Company, memos, or other related documents addressing this Pilot.

Describe why the Company-Directed option, as proposed, does not allow standalone storage. 
Has the Company developed a proposed customer agreement or any other applicable program documentation? If so, please provide.
Did the Company model the Proposed Pilot after any other current Pilot or program? If so, please describe the pilots or programs considered in the development of the Company’s Pilot.

Response:
The Company based the proposed target amount for the Residential and Commercial Solar Plus Storage Pilot Program on its prior experience implementing customer-sited renewable programs. The 50 megawatt (“MW”) target was established in consideration of the expected volume of customer applications. To clarify, Georgia Power does not consider the 50 MW target to be a limit on program capacity and did not use the term cap or limit in its filing or testimony. If the 50 MW target is reached, the Company will assess the impacts of the pilot program and propose options to the Commission for moving forward with the program. Finally, the existence of a DERMS system doesn’t alleviate the need for a reasonable target for the pilot program to manage participation during the next three-year regulatory cycle. See page 40, lines 19-26 of the Direct Testimony of Dr. Ross Beppler, Carley Goff, A. Wilson Mallard, and Andy Phillips in Docket Nos. 56002 and 56003 for additional information. 

For residential applications, the 20 kW limit was set with a goal to mitigate costs associated with distribution and service upgrades thereby simplifying the implementation process and minimizing additional costs to customers. This limit will also encourage right-sizing the ratio of solar and storage consistent with the Company’s existing behind-the-meter solar programs. 

For commercial applications, the 250 kW limit is designed to align with the RNR-11 tariff, which caps commercial and industrial (“C&I”) customers at 125% of peak load or 250 kW. This size limitation also aligns with existing interconnection processes, as projects greater than 250 kW are subject to more in depth study, interconnection, testing, and equipment requirements. 

All systems will be interconnected, tested, and operated consistent with the prevailing Southern Company Distributed Energy Resources (“DER”) Policy in compliance with Georgia Power’s specific Rules and Regulations for interconnection of a behind-the-meter generator.

Energy-only rates including R and GS do not provide an appropriate time-of-use (“TOU”) or demand signal to incentivize operation of the solar and battery resources to realize System benefit. Additionally, energy-only rates are not appropriate for the recovery of grid costs for customer generators installed behind-the-meter. See page 40, lines 9-18 of the Direct Testimony of Dr. Ross Beppler, Carley Goff, A. Wilson Mallard, and Andy Phillips in Docket Nos. 56002 and 56003. 

As Georgia Power worked to design the pilot program, the Company developed a series of slide decks, which were subsequently shared as part of the required collaboration sessions. The program development presentations are attached as STF-PIA-3-8 Attachment A.
 
The Customer-Directed option is available for both new and existing assets because it operates on a pay-for-performance basis. Under this model, incentives are provided based on the system’s actual ability to deliver the expected performance. This approach allows for flexibility in including existing assets, as the incentives are directly tied to the measurable outcomes of those systems. 

The Company-Directed option is limited to new assets because it involves providing a larger upfront customer incentive. For this option, it is crucial to have certainty and control over the capabilities of the assets being integrated into the program. By focusing on new assets, the Company can ensure that the systems meet specific standards and requirements, thereby justifying the substantial initial investment and ensuring predictable and reliable capacity contributions to the system.

The Company-Directed option, as proposed, does not allow standalone storage because the option requires that the storage systems be charged exclusively by solar resources. Allowing standalone storage systems to charge from the grid would necessitate the Company dictating when participating customers purchase additional electricity from the grid. By ensuring that storage is charged only from solar resources, the Company avoids causing customers to purchase additional energy and the associated program design impacts.

No, the Company has not yet developed a proposed customer agreement or other applicable program documentation. The customer agreement will be drafted in coordination with a third-party implementation facilitator to ensure that it meets all necessary requirements and effectively supports the pilot program’s objectives.

Yes, the Company gained insights from several other current pilots and programs from various peer utilities. In developing the Company’s pilot program, Georgia Power reviewed and considered programs from the following utilities: Duke Energy, Rocky Mountain Power, Green Mountain Power, Arizona Public Service, National Grid, and Xcel Energy.

          Although rooftop solar can be beneficial for some households, it may only be financially feasible
          for low-income homeowners in states with deep installation subsidies and enforceable
          consumer protections. We recommend that these low-income consumers pursue no-cost or
          deeply subsidized rooftop solar programs that exist in some states or community solar programs
          that are designed to support the energy needs of low-income households.
          Below, we list some of the reported problems and consumer risks of rooftop solar agreements
          and provide policy recommendations to address these concerns for all consumers.

          ROOFTOP SOLAR ABUSES
                 Misrepresenting the availability of tax credits to low-income homeowners who do not
                  owe any taxes, leading to situations where the actual purchase price of a rooftop solar
                  installation is two to three times the promised price, often resulting in unaffordable
                  payments.

                 Promised energy savings not materializing or guaranteed, potentially leaving
                  homeowners with significantly higher energy costs and total monthly expenses often
                  higher than before the solar transaction.

  What’s the Potential Impact of VPPs at Scale?

  VPPs that deliver benefits for utilities and customers have the potential to become a cornerstone resource to the
  US power system. According to DOE, tripling VPP scale by 2030 could address 10%–20% of peak load while saving
  approximately $10 billion per year.

  In this flipbook, we feature programs that provide over 1,500 MW of capacity from more than 3.9 million enrolled customers. Beyond the VPPs
  featured, at least 70 VPPs are located in 25 states and operate today (est. 30–60 GW nationally) with commercially available technology.10

  Yet, VPP benefits will be even greater if more utilities follow suit.

  DER adoption is accelerating and is expected to add hundreds of gigawatts and gigawatt-hours of potential capacity by 2030.

  Each year from 2025 to 2030, customers are expected to add:11
  • EVs: 20-90 GW of nameplate demand capacity from EV infrastructure, 300–540 GWh of nameplate storage capacity

  • Batteries: 7-24 GWh of nameplate storage capacity

  • Flexible demand: 5-6 GW from smart thermostats, water heaters, and non-residential DERs

  •   Distributed solar and fuel-based generators: 20-35 GW of nameplate generation capacity

  This presents a critical opportunity to integrate these DERs into VPPs — ensuring they can provide benefits to customers and the grid.

  VPPs have the potential to scale to 80–160 GW by 2030.12 At this scale, VPPs can play a key role in helping to address national resource adequacy
  and reliability needs and save on the order of $10 billion in annual grid costs, while directing grid spending back to communities and electricity
  customers.
  For additional details on definitions, see DOE’s Bulk Power, Distribution & Edge Services Definitions.

Question:
Please refer to the IRP Main Document, p. 104 regarding Georgia Power’s proposed Residential and Commercial Solar Plus Storage Pilot Program Customer-Directed Model:
a. Provide all workpapers and analysis in a working Excel spreadsheet that were used to determine the minimum number of hours called in a year, max event duration, both options of upfront incentives, ongoing performance-based incentive, capacity value, and term length.
b. Does the Company propose having a maximum number of hours called during an annual period?
c. Provide any technical requirements and restrictions for the solar and storage assets.
d. Provide all feedback that was received by the Company from interested parties that were considered in the development of the proposed Solar Plus Storage Pilot.
Response:
a. Please see STF-PIA-3-8 Attachment B TRADE SECRET.
b. No, the Company does not propose having a maximum number of hours called during an annual period. Since customers have the option to opt out of events, there is no need to set caps on the number of hours called. This approach provides flexibility for customers and allows them to maximize potential value while also providing the Company the flexibility to effectively manage and respond to System needs as they arise.
c. The solar and storage assets must comply with the Southern Company Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) Interconnection Policy. Further technical requirements will be drafted in coordination with the third-party program implementation partner once selected.  
d. The Company received written and verbal feedback from interested stakeholders through the Commission-hosted Solar Plus Storage collaboration and additional feedback directly from market participants. Please see STF-PIA-3-9 Attachment containing information provided through the Commission-hosted feedback process.

Question: 

Aurora Load Forecast: Please provide all workpapers and components used to develop the load forecasts utilized in Aurora for both GPC and Southern Company accounting for any coincident peak adjustments. Provide an accompanying narrative explanation to describe the process.

Response:

The data and models used to produce the Georgia Power Load forecast are attached in STF-PIA-1-16 Attachments A – R. As previously described in JKA-1-1(a) in Docket No. 56002, forecasts are developed for each of the operating companies and then aggregated. As such, there is no documentation specific to the development of the aggregated Southern Company retail load forecast, which is a sum of the individual retail operating company load forecasts. 

Georgia Power used MetrixND and MetrixLT in Budget 2025 to develop peak demand forecasts. These are Itron products used to create regression models and calibrate the hourly models to monthly energy forecasts. These models were originally developed for Budget 2021 forecasts and are re-evaluated each year to track with recent data. 
 
MetrixND uses historical hourly load research data for each customer class to derive regression models that utilize the day’s weather, day of week, holiday flags, month of year, and other pertinent independent variables. To predict class demands, the following components are used: the relationships of load and weather developed in MetrixND from the historical load research data; the description of “typical” weather; and the class monthly energy forecast. Both the coincident and non-coincident demands by class are produced with the model. 
 
The Georgia Power hourly load forecasting models use load shapes, primarily based on class, to define its territory. Currently, there are the five primary classes: residential; commercial; industrial; governmental lighting; and MARTA; plus two additional categories: electric vehicles and behind-the-meter solar. Most of the class input historical load shapes use load research historical data from 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023. The electric vehicle and behind-the-meter solar categories require several specific load shapes to properly capture their expected behavior. In Budget 2025, five load shapes were used for electric vehicles, including medium- and heavy-duty vehicles, and three were used for behind-the-meter solar.

The methodology determining “typical” weather uses 44 years of average daily temperatures (1980-2023), which were rank ordered and then averaged to form a “temperature duration curve.” The most representative pattern of actual temperatures was chosen using a least sum of squares criteria. The temperature duration curve was then re-sorted to the chronological order of the representative pattern. 
 
The final component in the development of the demand forecast is the input of the monthly calendar-based energies. The forecasted energies are based on a 44-year average weather assumption. Using the forecasted energies directly in the peak demand model maintains consistency between projected energy sales and the forecast demands.
 
The hourly load forecasting models also expand the monthly sales data to supply level (sales plus losses) for generation required for the projected energy and demands. The models use expansion factors, found in Attachment M, to go from sales to the customer to supply level energy and demands. The expansion factors used in Budget 2025 were derived using the results from the Georgia Power 2021 Loss Study with updated 2019 peak and energy load data. 
 
The results from the peak model are unadjusted for additional cogeneration projects and additional demand side management (“DSM”) programs that were previously approved by the Commission. Estimates for cogeneration and DSM are made for the reduction in demand and incorporated externally into the peak demand forecast.

As compared to the Georgia Power and Southern Company load forecasts presented in the 2025 IRP, the Resource Mix Study identifies the capacity resources needed to meet the aggregate retail operating companies’ needs. The system peak demand forecasts are derived by summing each retail operating companies’ non-coincident peaks (“NCPs”) and then applying a load diversity factor that is based on historical load information. This process is carried out by applying the diversified peak load change set used within the Aurora model. When this change set file is applied within Aurora, hourly loads that correspond to the System (SOCOnoSPC) peak hour will be adjusted proportionally so that the sum of the retail operating companies’ loads will equal the winter diversified System peak demand. For example, in the year 2030 the winter System diversified peak demand is 23,450 MW. For this year, the peak of the hourly System load is 23,612 MW and occurs on January 24 at hour 8.  By applying the diversified peak load change set, the hourly peak load adjusted from 23,612 MW to 23,450 MW to match the winter System diversified peak. This change only applies to hours in each year where the sum of the retail operating companies’ load exceeds the winter System diversified peak demand. Please see STF-PIA-1-16 Attachment S TRADE SECRET for the diversity percentage calculations.

System Reserve Margin and Reserve Sharing: Refer to the Company’s load and resource balance tables provided in “2029-2031 All-Source RFP Certification Application Needs Charts Workpaper.xlsx” and the footnote that states, “(F) Does not consider planning reserve sharing. Reflects GPC’s Target Reserve Margin, resulting from a System Target Reserve Margin of…”

b. Please provide a copy of the latest version of the Southern Company System Intercompany Interchange Contract.
c. Please provide a narrative explanation of the capacity reserve sharing accounting formula found in the IIC document, and as part of this discuss how recent capacity costs have been priced?
d. Why does the Company rely on both a short-term and long-term reserve margin target in developing targets? Please explain.

Response:

No. The Target Reserve Margin will not be evaluated until the next Reserve Margin Study scheduled for 2027. As part of the agreed and signed Stipulation resolving the Company’s 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) in Docket Nos. 56002 and 56003, , the Company has agreed to meet with PIA Staff to discuss and collaborate on the appropriate method to model data center loads in the Reserve Margin Study prior to June 30, 2027.

However, the Company has performed additional model calibrations incorporating the addition of 5,000 MW of high load factor load. This load and related energy was removed from the seasonal peak load forecasts so that it was not subject to hourly load shape adjustments. This adjustment is applicable to the ongoing 2025 ELCC Study as well as all future studies. The magnitude of the high load factor load will be adjusted each year in coordination with load forecast customer assumptions. Nevertheless, this large load adjustment was not applied to the ELCC studies utilized in the evaluation of the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP and supplemental resources.

See STF-PIA-1-12 Attachment.

The capacity reserve sharing accounting formula in the Intercompany Interchange Contract (“IIC”) determines the monthly amount of reserve capacity each Operating Company sells to or purchases from the Pool, ensuring all retail electric Operating Companies reach the system reserve level. This is calculated by comparing each Operating Company’s reserve responsibility, based on peak hour load ratios, with its actual reserve capacity. The formula accounts for available and unavailable portions of generating resources, adjusting for resource additions or deletions and unrecognized capacity outside the coordinated planning process. Through these calculations, each Operating Company aligns its reserve capacity with the overall system reserve level.  

Southern Company’s procedures for pricing capacity in the Reserve Sharing calculation involve procuring a Peaking Plant Equivalent Call Option annually, ensuring all Operating Companies transact at the same capacity rate. The solicitation, conducted by Fleet Operations and Trading, targets reliable and creditworthy counterparties and seeks offers for a capacity product with specific operational terms. Offers are evaluated primarily on price unless circumstances warrant otherwise. The process and results are documented, and relevant stakeholders are notified of the contract details.

As described in each Reserve Margin Study provided in past IRPs, including the most recent in the 2025 IRP, the Company uses short-term (1–3 years) and long-term (4+ years) reserve margin targets to reflect differences in planning uncertainty. Short-Term Targets are slightly lower (by 0.5%) because there is less economic uncertainty in the near term. Forecasts are more accurate, and fewer planning reserves are needed to buffer against load forecast error.

        F. Price Re-fresh
         GPC proposed providing an opportunity for a re-pricing of Bids and Proposals 27 weeks after Bids
are received. GPC explained the reason for the re-fresh as responding to market uncertainty due to the
changing situation with tariffs on Chinese goods and the duration between the Bid submission date and
final selection.
         Permitting a re-pricing of Submissions has been done in prior GPC RFPs where unusual
circumstances have arisen. For example, there will now be a re-fresh of Bid prices in the CARES 2023 RFP
due to the tariff situation. Similarly, a recent Biomass RFP was prolonged, then reissued due to cost
uncertainties. The IE has conducted a number of RFPs where re-pricing was permitted. In some instances,
re-pricing was not a part of the initial RFP design but added due to unforeseen circumstances. In others,
uncertainty about matters that could have a significant cost impact were known, but remained unresolved
at the time the RFP was released. Examples of the latter include potential carbon taxation, a volatile fuel
market, carbon sequestration legislation, and tariff wars with China. Indeed, the most recent tariff on
Chinese solar equipment was initiated over two years ago and caused disruption in a number of RFPs. It
was then suspended only to be recently activated.
        In this RFP, the earliest in-service date is 17 months after the Bid due date, and the latest date is
up to six (6) years after the Bid due date. With the exception of existing projects, the carrying cost of
construction, and a significant contingency component to mitigate cost uncertainty, may well result in
higher than necessary Submission prices.
         Any re-pricing continues the balancing act to get the least cost, most reliable supply for
ratepayers. The IE believes that there are pros and cons to any approach. We explore some of those here
to assist in establishing a record of full consideration of the available options and consequences.

components. The smaller developers, typically, will only have firm commitments for equipment once a
PPA is proffered.
         On the other hand, it is necessary to avoid incentivizing Bidders to “low ball” Bids to be included
as finalists, and then dramatically increase prices during a price re-fresh after competitors have been
eliminated. If a re-pricing is only available to the finalists, other Bidders who could have “sharpened their
pencil” and provided preferable prices would be eliminated.
        If the Commission permits re-pricing in this RFP, the IE recommends the following:
1. Participants should be provided with the relative ranking of their Submissions, relative to other
   Submissions. This information should be provided “blinded,” so that the identity of other Participants
   are not disclosed. While this ranking should be based on the total cost analysis at that stage in
   evaluation, the cost information should not be disclosed.
2. The date for a re-price submission should be part of the established schedule before the Submission
   date and remain fixed. This will advise Participants from the outset of when to be ready to submit
   revised prices, and thereby avoid delays later as Participants request extensions on the submission
   date.
3. The re-fresh should be limited to price only. Changes to the size, technology, etc., which impact
   pricing and/or facility output, should not be permitted as part of a revised Submission. Change to the
   COD of a project should not be permitted.
4. A re-price opportunity should be available to all conforming Submissions. That is, Submissions
   eliminated during the cure period or otherwise to be non-conforming should be excluded from a price
   re-fresh process. Weeks before the re-price submission date Participants should be advised, with
   “blinded” data, of the ranking of their projects. That ranking information should include the imputed
   cost impact of transmission.
5. Regarding whether refreshed prices could be increased or only decreased, the IE has found that so
   long as the competitive evaluation Process remains in effect, and all conforming Submissions are
   permitted to re-price, the re-fresh option has not been abused. Indeed, in the majority of RFPs we
   have conducted where re-pricing was permitted, Submission prices did not increase, even when that
   was an option.

Section 1.1 – Provision for Peak-Period Load Ratios: This article of the Manual establishes and

provides for the annual derivation of Peak-Period Load Ratios (“PPLRs”) that are utilized in energy

and capacity transactions and in other allocations as provided for in the IIC. These ratios are shown

on Informational Schedule No. 1.


Section 1.2 – Methodology for Determining Peak-Period Load Ratios: The Contract Year in the IIC

is defined as January 1st through December 31st. The peak period is defined as the fourteen (14)

hours between 7:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m. (Prevailing Central Time) of each weekday, excluding

holidays.


The Peak-Period Load Ratios for the Contract Year are based upon the prior year’s actual peak

period energy in the months of June, July, and August for each OPERATING COMPANY. The

system peak period energy is equal to the sum of all the OPERATING COMPANIES’ peak period

energy, excluding: (i) opportunity transactions with OTHERS that would begin and end during the

period from the current hour through Friday (midnight) of the following week; and (ii) any energy

sales transactions that are settled on a financial basis.


The Peak-Period Load Ratios are determined by dividing each OPERATING COMPANY's

summation of the June, July, and August actual weekday peak-period energy by the total system

June, July, and August actual weekday peak-period energy.

Question: 

Please provide a summary of the change in status of the following federal legislation and EPA guidance since the 2025 IRP. Explain how the Company’s bid/project evaluation reflects the current status of each:
a. Clean Air Act Section 111(b)(d) Greenhouse Gas (“GHG”) rules;
b. Effluent Limitation Guidelines (“ELG”);
c. Supplemental ELG rules (2024);
d. National Ambient Air Quality Standards (“NAAQS”);
e. Particulate Matter (PM)

Response:

On June 17, 2025, the Environmental Protection Agency (“EPA”) proposed to change the 111(b) and (d) Greenhouse Gas (“GHG”) rules in one of two ways: (1) repeal all 111(b) and (d) GHG rules; or (2) repeal all 111(d) rules and repeal only the 111(b) rules applicable to combustion turbines and based on carbon capture and sequestration (“CCS”). The economic evaluations performed as part of the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP and 2028-2031 Supplemental Resources certification applications were completed for a range of scenarios that included three cases without the 111 GHG Rule and a MG0-111 (moderate gas, zero carbon with 111 GHG Rule) case, since the 111 GHG Rule continues to be applicable while this proposed rule goes through the rulemaking process.

There have been no regulatory changes to the Effluent Limitation Guidelines (“ELG”) rule since the 2025 IRP. However, on March 12, 2025, EPA announced their reconsideration of the 2024 Supplemental ELG Rule. Since that time, EPA, in coordination with the Department of Justice (“DOJ”), has filed motions to hold in abeyance the ongoing litigation in the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. The latest motion, filed on August 11, 2025, stated that EPA has sent a rulemaking extending certain 2024 Supplemental ELG rule compliance deadlines to the Office of Management & Budget (“OMB”) and that EPA anticipates it will issue a proposed rule shortly with a final rule by the end of 2025. The motion also states that EPA intends to issue a second more substantive rulemaking that will potentially revise requirements for both flue gas desulfurization (“FGD”) wastewater and combustion residual leachate. Because the ELG rules are only applicable to steam power plants, the Company’s bids and project evaluations in this proceeding will not be impacted by any rule changes.

See response to Subpart (b). 

In March 2025, EPA announced the agency’s intent to revisit the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (“NAAQS”) for fine particulate matter (PM2.5). EPA has released no additional guidance or rulemaking related to the NAAQS. The state of Georgia is currently designated as in attainment of all NAAQs; therefore, the Company anticipates no impact to the Company’s bids or project evaluations in this proceeding related to NAAQS.

See response to Subpart (d).

DSO Program Description 
CVR Level 1 (Conservation Voltage Reduction Level 1) is a power delivery program where voltage is lowered within allowable standards using Company-installed voltage control devices on distribution circuits.
CVR Level 2 (Conservation Voltage Reduction Level 2) is a power delivery program where voltage is lowered for limited duration events when reliability is at risk.
DER Customer Program (Distributed Energy Resource Customer Program) is a program designed for customers that install and use distributed energy resources to reduce demand on the grid, especially during peaking periods.
DPEC (Demand Plus Energy Credit) is an interruptible load program offered to commercial and industrial customers where Pricing & Rates maintains the capacity available and tracks supply during interruptions.
RTPeDA (Real-Time Pricing Extreme Day Ahead) is a price response demand program for RTP-DA tariff customers that offers dispatchable resource pricing based on day-ahead forecasts.
RTPeHA (Real-Time Pricing Extreme Hour Ahead) is a price response demand program for RTP-HA tariff customers that provides dispatchable resource pricing based on hour-ahead forecasts.
Temp Check (Thermostat Energy Management Program) is an interruptible load program that is a residential air conditioning cycling program and provides dispatchable demand reductions during the summer.

STF-PIA-1-23

Question: 

For the Supplemental project bid evaluations
a. Please explain how the Company determined the inputs for Aurora modeling of the Resources.
b. Compare and contrast the contracted obligations or design specifications to the modeled Aurora inputs. Describe any simplifying assumptions made and provide any intermediary workpapers that were used to develop the assumptions put into Aurora.

Response:

The Company used the operational characteristics defined in the executed agreements for the five NextEra Energy Resources battery energy storage system (“BESS”) power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) for the Aurora modeling of these resources. For the Wadley BESS project, the Company used information from the All-Source RFP Company-Owned Proposal (“COP”) BESS projects to determine round trip efficiency (“RTE”) operational inputs for Aurora. For the Tenaska Heard County PPA, the Company used information from the All-Source RFP bid data previously submitted by Tenaska.

All projects evaluated were modeled in Aurora using 900 MW for the winter capacity size, and as-agreed to seasonal capacity relationships were honored. Modeling a uniform size for all resources avoids any capacity size bias that may be inherent in production cost models and introduces a significantly sized addition to produce more intuitive cost delta results. The resources were modeled to be available for the entire Aurora study period regardless of their actual start and end dates. The annual production cost output from Aurora was then adjusted to account for production cost savings based on the actual start year and month of the resource. For the calculations for the production cost adjustments, please see STF PIA-1-23 Attachment TRADE SECRET. The adjusted production cost savings were then entered into the economic evaluation model, in which the production cost savings are adjusted further to account for the actual resource end dates and to calculate results on a $/kW basis. For the calculations in the economic evaluation model, please see STF PIA-1-2 Attachment TRADE SECRET.

Question: 

For any analyses ranking the RFP bids and/or the supplemental projects,
a. Please explain how the company determined an “order” for applying BESS ELCC tranches.
b. How do the ELCC tranches used in the bid/project evaluations compare to the values used in 2025 IRP Mix Study modeling, and also to those from the ELCC study? Please explain.

Response:

The order for applying Battery Energy Storage System (“BESS”) effective load carrying capability (“ELCC”) tranches was determined based on initial economic ranking. Prior to the application of ELCC on the $/kW economic evaluation, resources were ranked economically using 100% winter capacity equivalence. The top ranked 2,000 MW of unique BESS projects in the pre-ELCC ranking were allocated 100% ELCC to be used in final evaluation. BESS projects that ranked lower than the top 2,000 MW were grouped in the 2,000-4,000 MW tranche and allocated 80% ELCC to be used in final evaluation. This ordering was conducted and evaluated for each unique planning scenario.

There is a difference in budget year vintages between the ELCC analysis for RFP project evaluation and the 2025 IRP Mix Study. The ELCC analysis for RFP project evaluation was performed using budget year 2024 data, whereas the ELCC analysis for the 2025 IRP Mix Study was performed using budget year 2025 data and also included additional generic solar. Expansion ELCC modeling studies for BESS have recently been performed on an annual basis to reflect changes in total resource mix as well as to incorporate new resources that could impact the ELCC value of subsequent incremental resource of the same technology type. That said, the chosen tranches are based on two different modeling data sets, and therefore, will exhibit differences. The tranches chosen for the project evaluation (four in total) were 2,000 MW in size while the 2025 IRP Mix tranches (four in total) ranged in sizes from 3,000 to 6,000 MW. The 2025 IRP Mix tranches were chosen to balance average incremental values with modeling simulation times, which increase for every tranche added.

See attachment STF-PIA-1-17 Attachment A in data request STF-PIA-1-17 for the ELCC values used in the 2025 IRP Mix Study. Note that the 2025 IRP Mix Study did not include options for six- or eight-hour batteries, so those values are not available.

2022 IRP Technical Appendix, Budget 2022 Load and Energy Forecast, Section 6.0PEAK DEMAND FORECAST

Georgia Power used MetrixND and MetrixLT in Budget 2022 to develop peak demand forecasts. These are Itron products used to make regression models and calibrate the hourly models to monthly energy forecasts. These models were originally developed for Budget 2021 forecasts and are re-evaluated each year to track with recent data.  

MetrixND uses historical hourly load research data for each customer class to derive regression models that utilize the day’s weather, day of week, holiday flags, month of year, and other pertinent independent variables. To predict class demands, the following components are used: the relationships of load and weather developed in MetrixND from the historical load research data, the description of “typical” weather and the class monthly energy forecast. Both the coincident and non-coincident demands by class are produced with the model.  

The Georgia Power hourly load forecasting models use load shapes primarily based on class to define its territory. Currently, there are the five primary classes: residential, commercial, industrial, governmental lighting and MARTA; plus two additional categories: electric vehicles and behind the meter solar. Most of the class input historical load shapes use load research historical data from 2015, 2016, 2017, and 2018. The electric vehicle and behind the meter solar categories require several specific load shapes to properly capture their expected behavior. In Budget 2022, four load shapes were used for electric vehicles (representing different charging patterns), and three were used for behind the meter solar. 

The methodology determining “typical” weather uses 41 years of average daily temperatures which were rank ordered and then averaged to form a “temperature duration curve”. The most representative pattern of actual temperatures was chosen using a least sum of squares criteria. The temperature duration curve was then re-sorted to the chronological order of the representative pattern.  

The final component in the development of the demand forecast is the input of the monthly calendar-based energies. The forecasted energies are based on a 41-year average weather assumption. Using the forecasted energies directly in the peak demand model maintains consistency between projected energy sales and the forecast demands.

The hourly load forecasting models also expand the monthly sales data to supply level (sales plus losses) for generation required for the projected energy and demands. The models use expansion factors to go from sales to the customer to the supply level energy and demands. The expansion factors used in Budget 2022 were derived using the results from the Georgia Power 2021 loss study with updated 2019 peak and energy load data.  

The results from the peak model are unadjusted for RTP response, additional cogeneration projects and additional DSM programs that were previously approved by the Commission. Estimates for RTP response, cogeneration, and DSM are made for the reduction in demand and incorporated externally into the peak demand forecast. The estimated adjustment for the RTP Response is derived from a model provided by Christensen Associates Energy Consulting. The Real Time Pricing response model, updated in the Spring of 2021, estimates load response at various prices. As with the previous model developed in 2018, the updated study used metered hourly data for each RTP customer and segmented the responses by Hour Ahead and Day Ahead customers. This model estimates the historical MW response and applies this relationship to estimate the projected MW response. SCS Resource Planning develops an expected RTP forecast that is used in the Christensen model to forecast the projected MW response.

STF-DEA-3-16

Question:

Please identify whether the shift between the short and long-term models requires external adjustments and if yes, identify what approach is used and provide the before and after adjustment data.


Response:

Yes, the shift between short and long-term models requires smoothing. TS STF-DEA-3-16 Attachment outlines the smoothing process for the Residential, Commercial, and Industrial classes. The steps are as follows:

After running the long-term LoadMAP model, its results are calibrated to the short-term model.
The process then smooths the transition to the long-term forecast at the conclusion of the short-term forecast period (2028), ensuring a seamless progression from short-term to long-term projections.
For the year 2028, the smoothed energy figure is derived by averaging the growth rates of 2027 and 2029. This adjusted growth rate is applied to the energy value of 2027 to calculate the smoothed energy for 2028.
Subsequent to the calculation, the total smoothed value for the year is allocated based on housing type (residential) or segment (commercial and industrial) and monthly distribution:
Monthly distribution percentages are ascertained from the distribution data of the preceding year (2027).
Housing type or Segment allocation percentages are likewise derived from the distribution of the prior year (2027).
Using these percentages, the total smoothed value is distributed evenly across each month and housing type or segment for the year 2028.
After smoothing in 2028, projections for 2029 and subsequent years are re-calculated from this adjusted base.

STF-DEA-3-17

Question:
Please provide a written summary of the structure of your load forecast model, explaining the following:
The construction/structure of the model and any structural assumptions made in the model (random drift, fixed effects, transformations of variables, etc.) 
The key drivers for load growth in the non-customer choice portion of load.
The time period of the data underlying the model and the extent to which they vary.

Response:
The load forecast model is formulated as a linear regression using Ordinary Least Squares (“OLS”) estimation. The model aims to predict the load (Yt​) at time t based on a set of predictor variables (X1t​,X2t​,…,Xkt​). The general form of the model is expressed as: Yt​=β0​+β1​X1t​+β2​X2t​+…+βk​Xkt​+ϵt​ where β0​ is the intercept, β1​,β2​,…,βk​ are the estimated coefficients, and ϵt​ represents the error term.
The model relies on key assumptions inherent in OLS regression:
Linearity: The relationship between the load and predictor variables is assumed to be linear.
No endogeneity: There should be no relationship between the errors and the independent variables. 
Normality and homoscedasticity: The error term is normally distributed and should have equal variance. 
No autocorrelation: Residuals are assumed to be uncorrelated.

The key drivers for load growth in the non-customer choice portion of load is the energy sales forecast.

Question:

Please provide each regression equation utilized to complete the short-term energy forecast and show the alternative models not selected with rationale for rejection. For each equation/model, provide summary statistics (mean, median, min, max, std. deviation, variance, R2, etc).


Response:

Please refer to the following attachments for the requested information:
TS STF-DEA-3-9 Attachment A: Residential use per customer and customer models
TS STF-DEA-3-9 Attachment B: Commercial use per customer and customer models
TS STF-DEA-3-9 Attachment C: Industrial energy and customer models
TS STF-DEA-3-9 Attachment D: MARTA energy models
TS STF-DEA-3-9 Attachment E: Governmental Lighting energy and customer models

The Company uses a systematic approach in determining which forecast models to select. A number of different criteria are utilized to identify the best performing model, including:
Variable Coefficients: Models with variables exhibiting an incorrect sign on their coefficients are eliminated from consideration, ensuring consistency with the expected relationships.
Adjusted R-squared: Models with relatively low adjusted R-squared values are excluded.
Growth Rates: Models with unreasonable growth rates for the dependent variable (e.g. customers, energy sales, use per customer) are omitted from consideration.
Insignificant Variables: Models containing variables deemed statistically insignificant are excluded, emphasizing the importance of including only meaningful predictors.
Statistical Tests: Models with inferior overall statistical performance are eliminated, employing various tests to assess the goodness of fit and model validity. Tests include, but are not limited to tests for heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, and unit roots. Other tests (e.g., for outliers, structural breaks, etc.) are used when needed.
The selection of the best model is based on a comprehensive evaluation considering the criteria above. This multi-step process is aimed at ensuring that the chosen model not only demonstrates favorable statistical properties but is also aligned with theoretical expectations and captures the essential features of the data.

Data Request No. STF-PIA-6-1

BASIS FOR THE ASSERTION THAT THE 
INFORMATION SUBMITTED IS A TRADE SECRET

As part of Georgia Power Company’s 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update filed in Docket No. 55378 (“2023 IRP Update”), Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) submits to the Georgia Public Service Commission its response to STF-PIA-6-1 (“Response”). In the Response, the Company has provided detailed information on its peak load forecast. All of such information (the “Information”) constitutes trade secret information of Southern Company, Georgia Power, and its affiliates and is therefore protected from public disclosure under Commission Rule 515-3-1-.11.

The Information derives economic value from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use. Specifically, the Information contains competitively sensitive commercial and strategic information related to Georgia Power’s projected load forecasts and corresponding short- and long-term capacity needs. The Information includes the Company’s peak forecast estimates and impacts, capacity need estimates. Public dissemination of the Information would allow Georgia Power’s competitors, suppliers, and customers access to the Company’s anticipated load strategies and objectives, thereby bestowing insight into the Company’s strategic initiatives and forecasting capabilities. Competitors would obtain an unfair advantage because they are not required to reveal similar information and can structure their offers for competitive choice opportunities based on the Information rather than their own costs, capacity, abilities, strategies, or analysis. Any sensitive budgetary Information, if disseminated, would provide insight to competitors regarding the Company’s financial positions, performance, and status. If the Information were revealed to the public, the Company could lose business to competitors and pay artificially higher prices to bidders, ultimately harming customers as well as Georgia Power.  

The Information is subject to substantial procedures to maintain its secrecy. Only select Georgia Power and Southern Company Services personnel are granted access to the Information. Those personnel receive access only on a “need to know” basis. Parties outside the Company who have been granted access to the Information, if any, have been required to sign confidentiality agreements with respect to the Information.

Provide by total company, and show broken down into all load categories (residential, commercial, industrial, etc.).

For each individual load project, separately show how the load forecast (peak demand and energy forecasts) was created by adding the individual external adjustments. 
For each year, provide the hour of the coincident peak, and for each load category, provide the hour of the non-coincident peak.


Response:
The Budget 2023 energy forecast and peak demand forecast are included in TS STF-PIA-6-1 Attachment. The energy forecast is in the “Energy” tab. The coincident peak demand forecast is in the “Peak – Coincident” tab, while the non coincident peaks are provided in the “Peak – Non Coincident” tab.   

Please refer to the “Peak-Coincident” and “Peak – Non Coincident” tabs in TS STF-PIA-6-1 Attachment.  

Please refer to the “Energy,” “Peak – Coincident,” and “Peak – Non Coincident” tabs in TS STF-PIA-6-1 Attachment. 

The expected data center loads (MW) by project can be found in the “Peak – Coincident” tab in columns L-O. The totals in column O match the referenced 1,100 and 1,200 MW load increases. To calculate coincident and non-coincident peaks, these totals are converted to an hourly frequency using an existing data center load shape. The data center load numbers included in the “Peak – Coincident” and “Peak – Non Coincident” tabs do not match the expected loads in column O since they include losses.   

Please refer to the “Peak – Coincident” and “Peak – Non Coincident” tabs in TS STF-PIA-6-1 Attachment for the date and time of the coincident and non-coincident peaks.

STF-PIA-6-3

Question:
Explain updates or changes from the Budget 2022 included in the Budget 2023 forecast and July 2023 forecast (model inputs, assumptions, regression, end-use considerations, EV load, etc.)

Response:
This question was initially an informal data request. The following response addresses the question as it was answered at the time of posting, with an update provided at the end of the response.

Please note that Georgia Power only does one Budget forecast per year, so the only official forecasts currently available for comparison are Budget 2022 and Budget 2023. The Company is currently developing Budget 2024, which includes the latest new large customer sensitivity, and will provide it to the Commission once it is completed.  

Budget 2022 is the forecast that was used in the 2022 IRP, the assumptions for which were included in the 2022 IRP Load and Energy Forecast found in Technical Appendix Volume 1. Georgia Power’s Budget 2023 forecast used the same methodologies employed in the 2022 IRP. The underlying base forecast was prepared using updated economic outlooks for the United States and Georgia and an updated energy price forecast. The market profiles for class end uses were unchanged between the Budget 2022 and Budget 2023 forecasts. The economic forecast continues to provide a description of the economy for the next 20 years and includes many elements of the economy such as gross product, population, employment, commercial building square footage, and industrial production. The economic and demographic forecasts for Budget 2023 were obtained from S&P Global (formerly IHS Markit), a national provider of economic data and forecasts.  
 
Consistent with the approach taken in the 2022 IRP, the models used to produce both the short- and long-term Budget 2023 energy forecasts include a variety of economic and demographic variables as drivers of energy use. Weather, income, employment, historical load data, and industry standards for electrical equipment are among the variables used in the forecasting models. “Normal” weather is defined as the average of Cooling Degree Hours (“CDH”) and Heating Degree Hours (“HDH”) from 1980-2021. 
 
The Budget 2023 short-term energy projections for residential, commercial, industrial and MARTA are based on linear regression models. The governmental lighting forecast is developed using information from Georgia Power field personnel. Except for governmental lighting and MARTA, the long-term forecast models are end-use models. As in Budget 2022, Budget 2023 uses the Load Management Analysis and Planning (“LoadMAP”) model to produce the long-term residential, commercial, and industrial forecasts. The MARTA long-term forecast uses the linear regression model developed for the short-term forecast, while the governmental lighting long-term forecast is based on information from Georgia Power field personnel. 
 
The short- and long-term models forecast organic growth based on historical trends, including net customer additions; historic adoption of energy efficiency and the future effect of existing energy efficiency standards; changes in economic conditions and demographics; and trends that develop over time that are part of the Company’s historical data, which gets propagated throughout the forecast.  
 
The results of the short-term and long-term models are integrated into a unified forecast. In Budget 2023, the short-term forecast results were used for the years 2023 through 2026 and the long-term results were used for 2027 to 2042. Budget 2023 uses hourly peak demand forecasting models to predict Georgia Power’s weather-normal peak demands.  
   
With respect to the usual forecast adjustments included in each forecast, Budget 2023 includes: an updated forecast of electric vehicles from EPRI; an updated Behind-The-Meter Solar forecast from the Georgia Power Renewables Team; updated RTP prices, an updated DSM forecast, and an updated forecast of cogeneration activity from Georgia Power personnel. 
 
As a result of the unprecedented growth in the development of new businesses across the state, Budget 2023 included three large data center projects. These projects were handled as external adjustments to the commercial energy and peak load forecasts.

STF-PIA-6-4

Question:
Aside from the individually identified new customer load growth opportunities that is expected to add thousands of MWs of new peak demand to the System, does the Company’s underlying load forecast model already include economic development and load growth assumptions? 

Explain how underlying load forecast model may have accounted for economic development already. Explain how underlying load forecast model may have accounted for general load growth already. Identify specific numeric quantities so that a comparison can be made between the three forecasts.
Has the Company added economic development and load growth assumptions for specific customers on top of the development and growth assumed by the load forecast model? If not, please explain how the Company avoided double counting the thousands of MWs of new customer load that has been recently identified. 

Response:
Note: As described in the Company’s response to STF-PIA-6-2, the Company did not develop a July 2023 forecast. In July of 2023, the Company developed a sensitivity to its B2023 forecast to account for how the existing forecast will be impacted by the growth in large new customers that are looking to develop projects in the state of Georgia. 

The Company’s underlying load forecast captures the trends in historical economic development activity via its short- and long-term energy forecasts. The short-term energy forecast is based on econometric models that quantify the relationship between historical sales data and economic and demographic variables. The long-term energy forecast is based on end-use models that account for economic and other assumptions. Like the short-term models, the long-term end-use models capture historical growth in energy sales, including past economic development projects. 

The Company’s energy forecasts are done at the class level: residential, commercial, industrial, governmental lighting, and MARTA. Growth for each customer class captures historical sales trends, including economic development projects, and the forecasted economic and demographic expectations in the Georgia Power service territory. Past economic development projects that have come on-line in recent years are embedded in historical sales. The models capture these developments and trends and extrapolate these at a class-level into the future. The impacts of past economic development projects on sales are not separately tracked, so a comparison of how much economic development is included in past forecasts is not available.     

As explained in the Company’s response to question STF-PIA-2-4(a), the Company’s energy forecasts are done at the class level using historical sales. The models forecast past trends, which include past economic development projects, business closings, impacts of the economy, impacts of energy efficiency, etc. Historical sales are not broken out into growth from economic development, general load growth, or other factors. Therefore, a comparison of how much general load growth is in past forecasts is not available. 

Georgia Power is seeing unprecedented growth in companies looking to expand in the state of Georgia. The expected load sizes (MW) of some of the announced expansions are well above anything the Company has seen before. From 2017-2021, Georgia Power’s Customer Choice program was selected to serve just over 150 MW, on average, in projects per year. In 2022, this total jumped to more than 2,000 MW. Year-to-date 2023, the Company has been selected to serve an additional 1,700 MW and is on track to surpass 2022’s total additions for the full year.  

This growth greatly surpasses recent historical growth. To sufficiently address this growth, the Company must make external adjustments (i.e., adjustments to the baseline forecast)  to its commercial and industrial forecasts. 

The Company takes steps to minimize the potential for double county of economic development load.  

For example, it establishes thresholds to determine which new proposed loads should be considered as possible adjustments to the forecast. These thresholds are set by the expected annual average growth in the commercial and industrial forecasts, excluding any external adjustments. Commercial loads are expected to grow by an average of nearly 115 MW per year through 2027, while industrial loads are expected to grow by approximately 45 MW per year over the same period.

STF-PIA-6-5

Question:
Refer to Informal Data Request 6.

Please provide the analysis used by the Company that demonstrate that the customer choice offers pass the Rate Impact Measure test.


Response:
The Rate Impact Measure (“RIM”) test used by the Company for customer choice offers is conducted in the PRICEM model. This is the same analysis and model used by the company to calculate RIM for non-customer choice load expansions, captive new customers, and demand-side management programs. 
PRICEM is a spreadsheet-based marginal cost model designed by Southern Company Services to predict the change in revenue requirements and other effects attributable to changes in loads and/or revenues. Data inputs are consistent with inputs to AURORA and as such are taken from: (1) revenue requirements stream from Standard Analysis Model (“SAM”); (2) marginal energy costs from AURORA; and (3) technology cost assumptions.
The RIM test is a net present value calculation of the total benefits and total costs over the life of the project. As with demand-side management (“DSM”) and other analyses, a RIM calculation result above zero is considered passing.
Please refer to the Company’s response to STF-DEA-1-2 for examples of RIM test results and PRICEM models.

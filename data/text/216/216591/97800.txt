Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN HOW THE COMPANY DEVELOPED LOAD FORECAST SENSITIVITIES AND HOW THE COMPANY USED THAT INFORMATION PRIOR TO FILING THE 2023 IRP UPDATE.
A.The Company developed its load forecast sensitivities to assess the range and likelihood of future load growth from new large load customers. In developing the sensitivities, the Company used a probabilistic approach to develop external adjustments to its baseline commercial and industrial load and energy forecasts further described in this Section. These external adjustments are necessary to account for the unprecedented magnitude and speed of the economic development now taking place in Georgia, which is not captured in baseline forecasts that rely on historical trends.
In developing the forecast model for large load projects, Georgia Power considers specific project details and acknowledges various uncertainties that are inherent in the forecasting process. The Company’s model incorporates information for each large load project, including the projected commercial operation date (“COD”), the detailed year-by-year load trajectory (i.e., ramp-up), and the announced load or design capacity for the project, which aids in facility sizing and equipment procurement. The Company also classifies projects as Industrial or Commercial and segments projects based on specific business categories such as warehouse, battery manufacturing, cryptocurrency, data center, chemical processing, and solar panel manufacturing, thus further refining the data used to inform its forecast.
The Company’s probabilistic model addresses several dimensions of the uncertainty that is inherent in large load projects, and several uncertainties are included in the model. For example, these uncertainty dimensions include:
Location Decision Uncertainty, which recognizes that potential customers may choose to locate projects in a different state despite showing initial interest in Georgia; 
Provider Selection Uncertainty, which exists due to the competitive selection process for large load customers among multiple electric service providers; 
Execution Risk Uncertainty, which exists even after a customer selects Georgia Power as its electric service provider, because unforeseen circumstances, such as financial or economic conditions, may lead to project abandonment; 
Load Materialization Uncertainty, which acknowledges that the load announced by a customer is only an estimate of the customer’s projected load, which may materialize at a lower level than initially anticipated; and 
COD Delay Uncertainty, which recognizes that the projected commercial operation date of a project may be delayed, thus resulting in the project’s load materializing later than initially expected.
Prior to filing the 2023 IRP Update, external adjustments to the baseline load forecast have been standard practice and were used to account for energy and peak demand resulting from new or ongoing uses that are not fully embedded in historical data or explicitly captured by our models. The Company assesses the need for external load adjustments when large customers are announced, typically introducing them when these customers bring a new line of business to the state or when the implied growth from our models is insufficient to represent the addition of the new load.

Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY ACCOUNT FOR THE LARGE INFLUX OF ANTICIPATED LOADS IN ITS FORECAST?

Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COMPANY’S METHODOLOGY FOR ADJUSTING THE LOAD AND ENERGY FORECAST IN THIS CASE.
A.As described in the Load and Energy Forecast Technical Appendix, the external adjustment process involves several steps:
Known Project Inputs: For each large load customer choice project the Company evaluated and included in the forecast update, the customer requiring electric service provided the following information: COD; ramp-up; announced load; class; and segment.
Modeling Project Uncertainty: As described previously, each customer choice project contains multiple dimensions of uncertainty, which must be considered in developing a forecast. These uncertainties include the customer’s state selection, electric provider selection, project delays, and load materialization. Based upon its knowledge and experience, the Company develops a probability analysis for each point of uncertainty. To account for these uncertainties, the Company then uses a probabilistic approach to determine the success or failure of an individual customer project.
Incorporating the Model: The Company incorporates three probability distributions (project delay, actual/announced load percentage, and project success/failure) into the expected ramp-up schedule for a project. This involves shifting the COD by a COD delay random variable, scaling the shifted year-by-year load trajectory by the actual/announced load percentage random variable, and multiplying the shifted and scaled year-by-year load trajectory by the Bernoulli 0/1 random variable that represents state selection, electric provider selection and completion rate.
Modeling the Portfolio: The Company uses a tiered approach to define the coefficients that describe the interaction among projects in the customer choice portfolio. This approach is driven by the class and segment of the projects instead of being defined one by one.
Model Implementation: The probabilistic approach is implemented in Excel with a third-party add-in called @Risk, which allows for Monte Carlo simulation and analysis. 
This approach allows the Company to adjust the load forecast to account for the large influx of anticipated load in a way that is both realistic and mathematically rigorous, allowing the Company to plan for a probable amount of customer choice load and avoid overestimating load additions that could add unnecessary capacity to the electric system.
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN HOW THE EXTERNAL ADJUSTMENTS ARE DETERMINED AND APPLIED TO THE BASELINE FORECAST. 
A. The external adjustment involves several key steps to refine the baseline load forecast. First, the Monte Carlo model generates a distribution of megawatt (MW) outcomes for the Company’s load portfolio by quarter. Employing a probabilistic approach, this distribution reflects the range and likelihood of future load growth from new large load customers. Next, the total portfolio MW is allocated to each individual project, to capture the contribution of each project to the overall load portfolio outcome. This step ensures that the unique characteristics and scale of each project are considered in the adjustment.
To translate individual project contributions into a form that is compatible with the Company’s baseline energy forecast, the contributions are converted to an energy metric by applying load factor assumptions specific to each project. The resulting energy figures represent the external adjustment (i.e., the additional load attributable to new projects). The calculated external adjustment is then added to the baseline forecast to determine the total load the Company must serve.

III.UPDATED CAPACITY NEEDS
Q.PLEASE IDENTIFY THE PRIMARY DRIVERS IMPACTING THE COMPANY’S UPDATED CAPACITY NEEDS AND CORRESPONDING REQUESTS IN THIS CASE.
A.Georgia Power’s requests are primarily driven by three factors: (1) an increased and accelerated demand for electricity; (2) a decrease in the availability of electricity supply from existing generation resources in the wholesale market; and (3) the time required to build or buy energy from new resources to meet capacity needs arising as early as 2026. The Company must carefully manage these driving forces in a rapidly evolving climate.
The increased and accelerated demand for electricity is the primary driver behind the need for capacity and the Company’s request for additional resources in the 2023 IRP Update. 
The second driver, the decreased supply of electricity, is related to the limited amount of electric generation capacity available in the wholesale market. Several other utilities in the Southeast recently concluded or are actively conducting RFPs seeking additional capacity from wholesale assets in the region. Should these utilities identify any available capacity and procure it, this will further reduce Georgia Power’s opportunity to procure wholesale capacity to meet the needs of its customers. Notably, the Company was one of the first utilities to the solicit market when it procured and certified over 2,300 MW of wholesale market capacity in the 2022 IRP, well in advance of tightening market conditions.
The third driver is the limited time available to procure or construct the resources needed to meet customers’ capacity needs. Georgia Power’s RFP process typically takes two to two-and-a-half years to identify, evaluate, and select capacity resources. Even if an RFP was issued immediately and concluded within that timeframe (i.e., as early as November 2025), only PPAs for existing wholesale generation assets could meet capacity needs in 2026. And that assumes such capacity resources remained available for the entirety of the lengthy RFP process. Any new-build resources selected through such an RFP would need to be constructed within one year to meet capacity needs, which is not a feasible timeline for construction. 
Q.CAN GEORGIA POWER’S RESERVE MARGIN PROVIDE SUFFICIENT CAPACITY TO MEET THE INCREASE IN CUSTOMERS’ NEEDS?
A.No. The Company’s reserve margin accounts for various operational uncertainties, including weather, load forecast, unit performance, and market availability. The load forecast component in the 2021 Reserve Margin Study, filed in support of the Company’s 2022 IRP, appropriately reflected a normal level of uncertainty in the demand of both existing and new customers. This uncertainty was considered in determining the target reserve margin. However, the load forecast uncertainty component of the 2021 Reserve Margin Study was never intended to reflect the significant increase in demand being driven by the extraordinary economic growth addressed by the 2023 IRP Update. Therefore, the target reserve margin established in the 2022 IRP does not provide sufficient reserves to address the substantial load growth projections presented in the 2023 IRP Update. 
Q.DID GEORGIA POWER CONSIDER WHETHER ITS INCREASED CAPACITY NEED COULD BE MET WITH MARKET RESOURCES?

The Company’s last Capacity RFP resulted in contracts with six existing facilities that were approved in the 2022 IRP. These contracted resources will continue to support the energy needs of Georgia Power’s customers during this period of economic growth. 
The recently completed RFI, issued in September 2023, gathered market information regarding the availability of existing capacity resources to help address the Company’s accelerated capacity need for years 2026 through 2028, as well as the expanded capacity needs for years 2029 through 2031, which are to be addressed in the All-Source RFP approved in the 2022 IRP. In response to its RFI, the Company received 44 unique submissions, 32 of which were mutually exclusive opportunities that met resource qualifications sought by Georgia Power. Notably, only two of the 44 submissions were existing facilities. Neither existing facility has capacity available in the winter of 2025/2026 through the winter of 2027/2028. The Company’s knowledge of the wholesale market, as confirmed by the RFI, indicates that existing market resources are not sufficient to address the capacity need identified in the 2023 IRP Update.

Q.CAN THE COMPANY LEVERAGE THE ALL-SOURCE RFP TO MEET THE CAPACITY NEED IDENTIFIED IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE?
A.No. The All-Source RFP cannot acquire resources quickly enough to meet capacity needs identified for the winter of 2025/2026. As noted previously, the RFP process typically takes two to two-and-a-half years from selection of the independent evaluator to the certification of resources. Even if an RFP could be issued immediately and completed within two years (e.g., by November 2025), only PPAs for existing wholesale generation assets would be able to meet capacity needs in the winter of 2025/2026. Moreover, this assumes those resources would be both existing and available, as well as economical and reliable. As demonstrated by the results of the Company’s recent RFI, there are not sufficient existing resources available in the wholesale market to meet the Company’s resource needs identified in the 2023 IRP Update. If the Company relied solely on the RFP process to procure resources, it would be unable to meet its capacity needs in the timeframe addressed in this 2023 IRP Update and would be limited in its ability support the continued economic development of the state. The All-Source RFP that was approved in the 2022 IRP will help the Company address its increased capacity needs in 2029-2031. Notably, the Company plans to increase the number of MW it will seek to procure in the upcoming All-Source RFP to address the increased capacity needs in the 2029-2031 timeframe.

Q.PLEASE ADDRESS POTENTIAL CHALLENGES OR RISKS ASSOCIATED WITH MEETING THE INCREASED CAPACITY NEED.
A.Potential challenges and risks associated with meeting the increased capacity need identified in the 2023 IRP Update include the following:
Limited Resource Availability: There is unlikely to be enough existing firm generation available to meet the increased demand for energy by the winter of 2027/2028. While the Company’s procurement of existing resources secures some capacity, it does not fill or alter the need for new capacity needed to serve customers.
Competition for Resources: Other utilities are also navigating the need for additional capacity based on potential load growth, seeking replacement capacity for retiring coal units, and adding resources to meet their newly established or increased winter target reserve margins. This competition limits the availability of market resources to meet Georgia Power’s needs. 
Reliance on Third Parties: The Company cannot rely solely on third-party independent power producers, who do not have an obligation to serve, to meet its customers’ energy needs. Consistent with Commission policy, the Company must maintain an appropriate balance between Company-owned resources and purchased power to ensure the reliability of the electric system and meet the energy needs of the customers it is obligated to serve. 
Transmission Constraints: Limitations on available transmission capacity could also limit the timeframe in which the Company can leverage existing resources or deploy new resources. Therefore, the Company must carefully plan and coordinate the addition of new transmission improvements to mitigate these constraints. 
Regulatory and Policy Changes: Changes in energy policies or regulations could impact the Company’s plans and operations. For example, changes in environmental regulations could affect the operation of certain types of power plants in the future. 
Technological Challenges: The integration of new technology, such as battery storage systems, can present technical challenges and require significant investment. 
Load Forecast Uncertainty: While the Company’s load forecasts are based on the best available information, they cannot predict the future with absolute certainty, and actual demand could be higher or lower than projected.

Q. HOW HAS THE COMMISSION HISTORICALLY ADDRESSED THE NEED FOR COMPANY OWNERSHIP?

Q.WHY IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING TO DEVELOP BESS PROJECTS AT ROBINS AND MOODY AIR FORCE BASES?
A. The proposed BESS projects at Georgia Power’s Robins and Moody Air Force Base solar facilities will leverage existing interconnection rights and transmission deliverability. These resources can be charged by the Company’s largest owned solar resources located adjacent to each site but may have opportunities to be charged by the grid depending on the availability of firm transmission capacity. As such, these projects save time and avoid additional capital investment that otherwise would be required to construct interconnection facilities and transmission system upgrades to charge and discharge the BESS. The proposed BESS resources will also optimize energy savings by shifting the energy output of the facilities from hours with a relatively low system marginal cost to hours with a relatively high system marginal cost. The BESS will also firm up the winter planning capacity of the existing solar facilities at both sites by storing energy that can then be dispatched by system operators to benefit the grid. Without BESS, the winter planning capacity for the solar resources at each site is 10% of the nameplate capacity of each facility. With the BESS, each facility can provide 100% of the solar nameplate capacity to meet winter capacity needs, thus maximizing the utilization of the existing solar resources.

As with the BESS proposed for the Robins and Moody Air Force Base projects, the BESS plus solar project can leverage existing interconnection and transmission system capabilities that mitigate delays associated with long-lead time transmission system improvements.

Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COMPANY’S CT RESOURCE REQUEST IN THIS IRP UPDATE.

Q.WHY IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING THESE CT RESOURCES RATHER THAN PROPOSING TO BUILD MORE ENERGY STORAGE RESOURCES?
A.As evidenced in recent IRPs as well as the 2023 IRP update, the Company remains very supportive of energy storage and anticipates the continued growth of that technology. However, to preserve system reliability and resilience for all customers during all hours, the Company needs a diverse portfolio of dispatchable resources. During extended periods, or multi-day events, of limited solar generation, such as during rainy periods or cloudy days, CTs will provide dispatchable generation that is not limited to 2- or 4-hour durations, thus supporting system reliability and resilience. These resources complement battery storage resources, which rely on generation from other assets to charge, by providing dispatchable peaking power when it is needed to meet customers’ energy needs. As energy storage resources continue to grow as a percentage of Georgia Power’s portfolio, and to support renewable integration, the Company must maintain a diverse generation portfolio that does not rely too heavily on any single technology, such as short-duration storage, to meet its capacity needs. Notably, energy storage resources do not generate energy and must be charged either from the grid or another resource. 
Thus, at times, energy storage resources act as additional load to the Company’s system. When dispatched, currently economical energy storage is limited to two or four hours of full discharge output. To serve customer load, the Company needs dispatchable resources that are not inherently limited by the specified discharge duration of the resource or a need to charge. While energy storage resources do have high levels of capacity equivalence because they can help to serve load during peak loads, they have limitations and need to be supported by other resources. These characteristics are reflected in the Company’s Resource Mix Study, which selects not only BESS and solar but also CTs and CCs as economically optimal additions to meet system capacity needs. Specifically, CTs are selected beginning as early as the winter of 2026/2027.

A.DER Programs 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE DER COLOCATION PROGRAM.

Through tariff DCL-1, Georgia Power will own, operate, maintain, and control dispatchable DER 10 MW and above at customer premises, and dispatch the resources to provide system benefits to all customers. The DER will be connected to the electric system, thus allowing the Company to transmit the energy they produce to the system. During a local electric service outage, the supply-side DER will continue to provide participating customers with electric energy, enabling more resilient operations. At no time will the DER generation impact the participating customer’s retail billed electric service while participating in DCL-1. In exchange for the enhanced resiliency service they receive, participating customers will make payments such that the resulting rate base value of the DER is below the system value realized over its asset life, thus benefiting all customers. Customers participating under the DCL-1 tariff will also be required to enter a program agreement with Georgia Power that will further establish the terms and conditions of participation. 
Q.WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF THE DER COLOCATION PROGRAM?
A. The DER Colocation Program allows for all stakeholders to benefit. Participating customers receive enhanced resiliency service at a cost below the full price of an on-site asset. Non-participating customers benefit from system capacity resources that are procured below the value received by these customers. The program leverages DER to strengthen grid reliability for all customers, creates a more flexible and diverse resource mix, promotes cleaner DER resources, and increases resilience for participating customers.
Q. HOW WILL THE PARTICIPATING CUSTOMER PAYMENT BE CALCULATED?
A.The participating customer’s payment will be calculated as the total cost of the asset less 75% of the net present system value over the life of the asset. This structure (1) ensures all customers will see a benefit based on the discounted system value credited, (2) mitigates the bad debt risk to non-participants, and (3) ensures that participating customers receive resilience benefits. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE DER CUSTOMER OWNED PROGRAM. 
A.The DER Customer Owned Program, as implemented through the DCO-1 tariff, will be an optional program available to qualifying commercial and industrial customers and will operate much like the DER Colocation program, but with a few key differences. Through DCO-1, Georgia Power will operate and control customer-owned, new dispatchable DER 1 MW or greater, but less than 10 MW, located at customer premises and economically dispatch the resources to provide energy and capacity benefits to all customers. Customers may aggregate assets at multiple accounts to meet the 1 MW minimum requirement, provided that each account’s DER is 250 kW or greater. Like with DCL-1, interconnection of the DER with the Company’s electric system will allow the energy produced by the DCO-1 DER to be transmitted to the electric grid. During times of electric service outage or need, the supply-side DER will continue to provide participating customers with electric energy, enabling more resilient operations. At no time will the DER generation impact the participating customer’s billed retail electric service while participating in DCO-1. Under this program, Georgia Power will provide participating customers with a credit on their electric bills in exchange for the Company’s use of the customer’s DER for dispatch. The DCO-1 credit will be based on the system value of the participating customer’s DER over the period during which they subscribe to the DCO-1 program. As a condition of participation, participating customers will be required to enter into a program agreement with Georgia Power that will further establish the terms and conditions of participation. 
Q. WHAT will BE the TERM LENGTH OF THE DER Customer owned program contracts?
A. The DER Customer Owned Program will allow for contracts through 2031. At that time, the Company will evaluate the ongoing benefits that these resources may continue to provide to the system and all customers. This contractual arrangement will provide great benefits to non-participants, providing firm capacity in the short-term, without committing to longer-term agreements. Participating customers will receive guaranteed benefits and payments through 2031 with future payments and the associated system value for their customer-owned DER being assessed at that time.
Q. How will the Company calculate the credit paid to participating customers in the DCO-1 Program?
A. A levelized credit payment equal to 75% of the system value of the DER asset will be paid over the contract period. This structure ensures that all customers will see a benefit based on the discounted system value credited, and that participating customers receive a fixed payment stream through 2031 on their DER asset, offsetting their investment costs. 
Q.What are the benefits of the DER CUstomer Owned Program?
A. The DER Customer Owned Program allows for all customers to benefit. Participating customers receive a firm credit payment over several years, offsetting the cost of their DER investment. Non-participating customers benefit from capacity resources being procured below the system value. The program leverages DER to strengthen grid reliability for all customers, creates a more flexible and diverse resource mix, promotes cleaner DER resources, and increases resilience for Georgia Power’s participating customers.
Q. What type of assets will be able to participate in the DER Colocation and der customer owned programs?
A. DER technology that may be utilized under DCL-1 includes, but is not limited to combustion turbines, reciprocating internal combustion engines (“RICE”), and other dispatchable technologies with firm fuel supply. The Company will limit eligible DER to dispatchable assets that are certified and permitted for non-emergency use by the Georgia Environmental Protection Division of the Department of Natural Resources. All participating DER assets will be subject to the requirements of interconnection studies.
Q. How Much Generation Capacity will be relied on through the DER Colocation and der customer owned programs?
A. The DER utilized in these programs will be peaking type resources and as such they should not exceed the unfilled peaking capacity needs in the system resource plan. These customer-sited DER programs allow the Company to meet its peaking resource needs by leveraging the same resources participating customers are seeking for their resiliency needs. In meeting this dual purpose, the programs can achieve greater overall economic efficiency. 
q.How will the DER Colocation and der customer owned programs be utilized?
A. The DER in these programs will be dispatched for system use for both economic and reliability purposes, maximizing their value to all customers. Assets will also operate in the event of a local outage to provide resiliency to the customer. 
Q. How will der in the DER Colocation and der customer owned programs be approved? 
A. In this IRP Update filing, the Company is seeking approval of the proposed programs, and the authority to begin developing projects with participating customers. The Company plans to submit the proposed DCL-1 and DCO-1 tariffs, along with the CL-1 tariff, for Commission approval prior to the direct hearings of Georgia Power. Once DCL-1 and DCO-1 projects are fully developed and contracts are executed, they will then be filed for certification and approval with the Commission. 
Q.ARE the DER Colocation and der customer owned programs APPROPRIATE FOR INCLUSION IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE? 
A. Yes. The enrollment of participating customers coupled with the development and construction of DERs require substantial lead time before the DERs become operational. Approving the DER Colocation and DER Customer Owned programs now expedites the realization of capacity benefits that these resources can provide to all customers. Any delay in approving these programs produces a delay in any potential capacity benefits to all customers. 
B.Curtailable Load Program 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED CURTAILABLE LOAD PROGRAM. 
A.The Curtailable Load program will enable additional demand response participation options to commercial and industrial customers and provide corresponding system capacity and reliability benefits to all customers. This optional DCL-1 tariff, through which the program will be implemented, will be available to qualifying commercial and industrial customers able to make multi-year commitments to reduce their load during extreme supply and demand conditions. 
Q. What are the benefits of the Program?
A. The curtailable load program provides significant benefits to both participating customers and the system by reducing consumption during extreme conditions for grid reliability. Participating customers receive lower electric bills and non-participating customers receive the savings of deferred generation capacity infrastructure. The program leverages load flexibility to strengthen grid reliability for all customers, creates a more flexible resource mix, and provides economic benefits for participating customers.
Q.HOW DOES THE CURTAILABLE LOAD PROGRAM DIFFER FROM THE COMPANY’S EXISTING DEMAND RESPONSE PLUS ENERGY CREDIT (“DPEC”) DEMAND RESPONSE PROGRAM? 
A.This program will differ from the Company’s existing DPEC demand response program in several ways. First, it will require multi-year contracts, which provide increased system value and assurance, and resulting increased credit values for participating customers. Second, the Curtailable Load program will reflect a value for capacity in both the winter and summer periods. Third, the curtailable load program will require demonstration on a regular basis to ensure demand response availability. Finally, the program will also have an updated compliance incentive to provide ongoing inducement to participate. 
Q.WHY IS THE CURTAILABLE LOAD PROGRAM APPROPRIATE FOR INCLUSION IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE? 
A. Demand side options have long been a source of capacity for the system reflected in the resource plan. The Curtailable Load program can provide a more reliable source of new carbon-free capacity in an environment where firm capacity assets are becoming scarce. Additionally, since demand response does not necessarily require any construction, realization of benefits can materialize more quickly and defer the procurement of future, additional capacity. 
C. Expansion of Residential Thermostat Demand Response DSM Program 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COMPANY’S RESIDENTIAL THERMOSTAT PROGRAM. 
A.Georgia Power’s Temp Check is a residential thermostat demand response (“TSTAT DR” or “Temp Check”) DSM program that allows the Company to make event calls that adjust participating customers’ thermostat behavior during a limited period. The Company pays participating customers an incentive for this ability. 
Q. WHAT CHANGES IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING TO MAKE IN THE TSTAT DR PROGRAM?
A. The Company proposes expanding customer enrollment in the Temp Check program to 50,000 customers. 
Q. WHY IS EXPANDING THE TSTAT DR PROGRAM APPROPRIATE FOR INCLUSION IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE? 
A.The Temp Check program is a source of demand response potential that translates to capacity value. The program has seen robust customer participation and is currently closed to new enrollment. In addition, the program now reflects positive Total Resource Cost (“TRC”) results for the evaluation life of the program and, therefore, no longer requires a waiver of the TRC requirement within Commission Rule 515-3-4-.04(4)(a)(3) as was approved in the 2022 IRP. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY BELIEVE THAT ADDITIONAL POTENTIAL EXISTS BEYOND THE REQUESTED 50,000 TSTAT PARTICIPANTS?
A. Yes, the Company believes that further residential load flexibility potential exists. The Company believes doubling the existing TSTAT DR program over the remainder of the DSM cycle reflects an aggressive but achievable expansion of the program. The Company will continue to evaluate and expand load flexibility programs where those options can deliver cost-effective value to all customers. 
VI.CUSTOMER IMPACTS
Q.WHAT COSTS ARE ASSOCIATED WITH THE COMPANY’S PLAN PROPOSED IN THIS 2023 IRP UPDATE? 
A.Georgia Power is committed to maintaining affordable rates for all customers and to ensuring that the costs of electricity are appropriately allocated among customers. Regarding the capacity resources requested in the 2023 IRP Update, Georgia Power will continue to adhere to its long-standing requirement that new economic development projects have long-term projected net benefits to Georgia Power customers. Accordingly, each new economic development project has to pass financial and economic screening requirements, including the Ratepayer Impact Measure (“RIM”) test. Georgia Power expects existing and new customers to recognize economic benefits from this energy usage growth, which will help offset and balance the cost of investments needed to serve Georgia’s growing energy needs.
Q.IS THE COMPANY REQUESTING A RATE INCREASE AS PART OF THE 2023 IRP UPDATE?
A.No. Consistent with the Company’s prior IRP filings, Georgia Power is not requesting a rate adjustment in the 2023 IRP Update. 
Q.WILL CUSTOMER BILLS EVENTUALLY INCREASE BECAUSE OF THE REQUESTS INCLUDED IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE?
A.All else being equal, considering the expected load growth and cost of the solutions proposed in this 2023 IRP update, the Company does not expect customer rates to increase. The recovery of costs associated with new facilities that serve customers will continue to be addressed through the rate case process, where the Commission will establish reasonable rates based upon the Company’s overall cost to serve. As noted above, each economic development project must pass financial and economic screening requirements, including the RIM test. 
VII. FLEX CAPACITY
Q.IF THE COMMISSION APPROVES THE REQUESTS IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE, WILL THE STATE’S ENERGY NEEDS BE MET UNTIL THE 2025 IRP? 
A.If approved, the requests in the 2023 IRP Update should allow Georgia Power to meet the needs of its customers and the state based on current projections. Given the extraordinary economic growth in Georgia, the Company is closely monitoring the energy needs of customers and will work with the Commission to address future changes should they be necessary. To that end, the 2023 IRP Update establishes a framework within which the Company can request approval to acquire additional capacity (“Flex Capacity”) ahead of the 2025 IRP if an updated load forecast projects additional capacity needs. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED FLEX CAPACITY FRAMEWORK.
A.The Flex Capacity framework would authorize Georgia Power to undertake and recover the cost for preliminary development activities in connection with the development, operation, and ownership of new capacity resources. Additionally, the Company would be authorized to continue to explore PPA or acquisition options that could be presented for Commission approval and certification prior to the filing of the Company’s 2025 IRP and recover any incremental cost incurred for this effort. Georgia Power proposes that the following criteria must be met before the Company could pursue such options: (1) an update to the 2023 IRP Update load forecast that results in a 500 MW or greater capacity need prior to the winter of 2028/2029; and (2) the Company submits the proposed resource(s) for certification with an updated capacity needs chart and associated load forecast update.
Q.WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED FLEX CAPACITY FRAMEWORK?
A.The Flex Capacity framework allows for continued resource development flexibility in the coming years. Georgia Power plans to continue to identify supplemental or “Flex Capacity” resource options that could help meet additional capacity needs prior to the winter of 2028/2029, if necessary. For example, Georgia Power may identify BESS capacity opportunities in addition to the 1,000 MW proposed in this 2023 IRP Update. Additionally, the Company could continue to explore the possibility of additional PPA options associated with limited market resources, although none were identified by the Company’s RFI issued in September and filed in the Company’s Supplemental Filing on December 4, 2023, in this proceeding. If approved by the Commission, the Flex Capacity framework would help Georgia Power address rapid increases in load prior to the Company filing its 2025 IRP and continue to support economic growth in Georgia.
Q. WHAT REGULATORY AND RATEMAKING TREATMENT IS THE COMPANY SEEKING FOR ITS FLEX CAPACITY FRAMEWORK?
A.Under this proposal, the Company would be approved to defer any developmental costs for such activities that would otherwise be expensed into a regulatory asset, with the amortization period to be determined in the Company’s next base rate case. Once resources are identified under this framework, the Company will seek expedited certification for such resources. 
VIII.TRANSMISSION
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE SCREENING ANALYSES THE COMPANY USED TO EVALUATE THE TRANSMISSION IMPACTS OF THE RESOURCE SOLUTIONS INCLUDED IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE.
Georgia Power performed transmission screens for various generation resource options considered by the Company to support its generation resource needs as described in the 2023 IRP Update. The screens utilized transmission power flow analyses across various scenarios of incremental resource additions. Each scenario included all the large load projects that have selected Georgia Power as well as prior queued projects. The screens provided estimated costs for transmission improvements that are attributable to resources proposed in this 2023 IRP Update, and they identified temporary capacity limitations until certain transmission improvements are completed.
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN THE COMPANY’S REQUEST RELATED TO TRANSMISSION.
A.In the 2023 IRP Update filing, the Company requested approval of “expansion of the transmission system to accommodate the above-requested resources and the Company’s Load and Energy Forecast as described in the Technical Appendix.” Following discussions with Commission Staff, the Company seeks to clarify this request. Specifically, the Company requests approval of the transmission improvements necessary to accommodate the resources proposed in the 2023 IRP Update. Such improvements were identified in the preliminary transmission screening analyses filed in the Technical Appendix to the 2023 IRP Update and are consistent with the types of transmission improvements typically required to accommodate new generation added to the System. 
Q.HAS THE COMPANY PROVIDED ANY ADDITIONAL SUPPORT OR ANALYSIS SUPPORTING THE TRANSMISSION REQUEST?
A.The Company is currently conducting additional, more detailed transmission studies on the transmission impacts of the 2023 IRP Update. The Company will submit a 2023 IRP Update Supplemental Filing in January 2024 that will include this additional analysis and support. Importantly, the supplemental transmission studies will incorporate potential new large loads consistent with the 2023 IRP Update Load and Energy Forecast in addition to the committed loads included in the preliminary transmission screening analyses described above. The Company does not anticipate the results from the supplemental transmission studies to materially deviate from the results of the screening analyses.
Q.WHY IS TRANSMISSION CRITICAL FOR AN EFFECTIVE AND RELIABLE FLEET?
A.The Company’s generation fleet does not operate in a vacuum and cannot reliably serve customers without a reliable transmission system. Georgia Power must consider how resource planning decisions impact the transmission system, especially considering the planned fleet transition and retirement of the Company’s coal resources. The Company identified transmission constraints relevant to fleet transition both in the ten-year plan and other unit retirement scenarios that were evaluated. 
Q.ARE THERE TRANSMISSION IMPACTS AND RELATED COSTS ASSOCIATED WITH THE ADDITION OF THE NEW LARGE LOAD PROJECTS?
A.Yes. The Company studies and evaluates the transmission infrastructure necessary to accommodate the additional large load projects as they are proposed to be added to the System. The costs for such transmission impacts will be direct assigned, local, or upstream from the project. 
Q.HOW ARE TRANSMISSION COST ASSOCIATED WITH NEW LARGE LOAD PROJECTS RECOVERED?
A.Direct assigned and local costs will be paid for by the customer either directly, through upfront payments, or through their rate offering and resulting revenue. Any upstream transmission costs caused by the addition of the new large load customer are built into the customer’s rate and will be paid for by the new customer over time for the life of the project. Prior to making an offer to serve a customer choice opportunity, the Company analyzes the cost to serve the customer including the necessary transmission upgrades and investments needed to accommodate the load addition. The Company only extends competitive offers to eligible customers that pass financial and economic screening requirements, including the RIM test.

A.Yes. The balanced portfolio of resources proposed in the 2023 IRP Update provides reliable power to meet Georgia Power’s capacity needs starting in the winter of 2025/2026 and provides a foundation for the Company’s long-term capacity needs and the continued transition of its generation fleet to cleaner resources. The Company expects that the projected revenues associated with incremental customer load will offset the costs of the resources requested in this filing and put downward pressure on overall retail rates as Georgia Power continues to provide clean, safe, reliable, and affordable power for its 2.7 million customers.

14   Q.   PLEASE        DESCRIBE            THE       COMPANY’S              METHODOLOGY              FOR
15        ADJUSTING THE LOAD AND ENERGY FORECAST IN THIS CASE.

16   A.   As described in the Load and Energy Forecast Technical Appendix, the external
17        adjustment process involves several steps:

18        1. Known Project Inputs: For each large load customer choice project the
19           Company evaluated and included in the forecast update, the customer requiring
20           electric service provided the following information: COD; ramp-up; announced
21           load; class; and segment.
22        2. Modeling Project Uncertainty: As described previously, each customer
23           choice project contains multiple dimensions of uncertainty, which must be
24           considered in developing a forecast. These uncertainties include the customer’s
25           state selection, electric provider selection, project delays, and load
26           materialization. Based upon its knowledge and experience, the Company
27           develops a probability analysis for each point of uncertainty. To account for

              Direct Testimony of Jeffrey R. Grubb, Francisco Valle, Lee Evans, and Michael A. Bush
                                       On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                                Docket No. 55378
                                                   Page 18 of 56

 1           these uncertainties, the Company then uses a probabilistic approach to
 2           determine the success or failure of an individual customer project.
 3        3. Incorporating the Model: The Company incorporates three probability
 4           distributions (project delay, actual/announced load percentage, and project
 5           success/failure) into the expected ramp-up schedule for a project. This involves
 6           shifting the COD by a COD delay random variable, scaling the shifted year-by-
 7           year load trajectory by the actual/announced load percentage random variable,
 8           and multiplying the shifted and scaled year-by-year load trajectory by the
 9           Bernoulli 0/1 random variable that represents state selection, electric provider
10           selection and completion rate.
11        4. Modeling the Portfolio: The Company uses a tiered approach to define the
12           coefficients that describe the interaction among projects in the customer choice
13           portfolio. This approach is driven by the class and segment of the projects
14           instead of being defined one by one.
15        5. Model Implementation: The probabilistic approach is implemented in Excel
16           with a third-party add-in called @Risk, which allows for Monte Carlo
17           simulation and analysis.

18        This approach allows the Company to adjust the load forecast to account for the
19        large influx of anticipated load in a way that is both realistic and mathematically
20        rigorous, allowing the Company to plan for a probable amount of customer choice
21        load and avoid overestimating load additions that could add unnecessary capacity
22        to the electric system.

23   Q.   PLEASE EXPLAIN HOW THE EXTERNAL ADJUSTMENTS ARE
24        DETERMINED AND APPLIED TO THE BASELINE FORECAST.

25   A.   The external adjustment involves several key steps to refine the baseline load
26        forecast. First, the Monte Carlo model generates a distribution of megawatt (MW)
27        outcomes for the Company’s load portfolio by quarter. Employing a probabilistic
28        approach, this distribution reflects the range and likelihood of future load growth

              Direct Testimony of Jeffrey R. Grubb, Francisco Valle, Lee Evans, and Michael A. Bush
                                       On behalf of Georgia Power Company
                                                Docket No. 55378
                                                   Page 19 of 56

 1        from new large load customers. Next, the total portfolio MW is allocated to each
 2        individual project, to capture the contribution of each project to the overall load
 3        portfolio outcome. This step ensures that the unique characteristics and scale of
 4        each project are considered in the adjustment.

 5        To translate individual project contributions into a form that is compatible with the
 6        Company’s baseline energy forecast, the contributions are converted to an energy
 7        metric by applying load factor assumptions specific to each project. The resulting
 8        energy figures represent the external adjustment (i.e., the additional load
 9        attributable to new projects). The calculated external adjustment is then added to
10        the baseline forecast to determine the total load the Company must serve.

17        especially considering the planned fleet transition and retirement of the Company’s
18        coal resources. The Company identified transmission constraints relevant to fleet
19        transition both in the ten-year plan and other unit retirement scenarios that were
20        evaluated.

26        to the System. The costs for such transmission impacts will be direct assigned,
27        local, or upstream from the project.

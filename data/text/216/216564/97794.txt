MOTION CHALLENGING THE TRADE SECRET DESIGNATION OF BASIC IDENTIFYING INFORMATION ABOUT GEORGIA POWER COMPANY’S POTENTIAL PURCHASE OF A PUBLICLY UNDISCLOSED ASSET
The Southern Environmental Law Center, on behalf of intervenor in Docket No. 55378, Georgia Interfaith Power & Light (“GIPL”), moves the Georgia Public Service Commission to order Georgia Power Company to disclose to the public critical information about the generation resources it wants to buy. That information has been inappropriately withheld from the public as “trade secret.” Specifically, at a minimum Georgia Power should reveal the basic identifying information about the electricity generation it is trying to buy in its 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update (“IRP Amendment”), which is currently publicly identified solely as an “additional ownership interest in an existing generating asset” for the Commission’s “review and approval.” 
Georgia Power surprised the public with this IRP Amendment just over a year after the resolution of the 2022 IRP and weeks before the Vogtle prudency hearings. Georgia Power proposes Commission consideration of several company-owned generating assets for which Georgia Power has already either signed agreements or is negotiating agreements. Georgia Power further asks this Commission to find an “exception” to the Request for Proposal process by which the Company usually identifies least-cost resources. Breaking with the Commission’s long history of a public procurement process, Georgia Power has marked essentially all identifying information about one of these potential generating assets as “trade secret.” Though Georgia Power claims this mystery asset “provides necessary capacity,” Georgia Power fails to disclose publicly from where and how that capacity will be generated. IRP Amendment, Main Doc. (“IRP Am.”) at 18; Public Disclosure of Technical App’x 3 (attached as Ex. 1). GIPL challenges Georgia Power’s withholding of this basic information as “trade secret.”
GIPL files this motion pursuant to Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(7), which requires the Commission to hold a hearing and an in-camera (private) inspection of the challenged information. Petitioner asks the Commission to either order Georgia Power to disclose basic identifying information about the mystery asset, or in the alternative, withdraw its consideration from this IRP Amendment and order Georgia Power to file a new IRP Amendment if and when it can disclose to the public what it wants its customers to buy.
Undisclosed Assets are Incompatible with Georgia’s IRP Process, which is Designed to be a Public Process with Public Involvement to Promote the Public Interest.

Georgia Power’s Integrated Resource Planning process is an important, contested proceeding in which the Commission determines, among other things, how Georgia Power’s 2.7 million billpayers will invest in sources of energy “to satisfy future energy service demands . . . while balancing the interests of utility customers, utility shareholders and society-at-large.” Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.02(25). The Commission’s rules require extensive information disclosures, which allow the public to scrutinize and comment on this critical process. See, e.g., Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.06, et seq.; O.C.G.A. § 46-3A-2(b) (“At the hearing any interested person may make comments to the commission regarding the contents and adequacy of the plan.”). And at the end of the IRP process, the Commission is charged with determining “which combinations of resource options . . . best serve the public interest. . . .” Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.05(4).
As a member of the public, there is only one way to access legitimately withheld “trade secret” information: Intervention. See Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(3). Georgia Power can (and has) refused to disclose alleged trade secret information even to interested organizations who sign non-disclosure agreements. But there are significant barriers to intervention, including the requirement that a person who wants to intervene has to submit a timely, “verified” application in writing that “set[s] forth with particularity the facts pertaining to his interest and the grounds upon which his application for intervention are based.” O.C.G.A. § 46-2-59(c). It is difficult to imagine how a particular person or organization in Georgia would be aware of their interest in a potential purchase of an electricity generator without first being told basic identifying information, defined here to mean the name, location, type of electricity generation (i.e., solar, gas, hydro, etc.), the capacity of electricity it can produce, and the anticipated life of the generator.
In this case, Georgia Power asks to amend its 2022 IRP. Georgia Power notes that “negotiations are ongoing” to buy an additional stake in an “existing generating asset.” IRP Am. at 18. In other words, Georgia Power anticipates making a “commitment for the acquisition . . . of a demand resource or supply resource excepted from the RFP process.” Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.06(5)(b). And yet, Georgia Power reveals nothing to the public about what this potential acquisition entails, other than it promises to “supplement” its filing after it reaches an agreement to purchase this mystery asset on behalf of its customers. IRP Am. at 18. This black-box procurement flips the design of the public IRP process on its head, allowing Georgia Power to buy assets first, and ask for permission later. The public vetting of an IRP—and the competitive, market-based bidding process of an RFP—are skipped entirely. Transparency is the key to good policy, and Georgia Power does not have a right to maintain the secrecy of the assets it is buying on behalf of its customers.

LEGAL STANDARD
A trade secret in Georgia is information “which is not commonly known by or available to the public and which information”:
(A) Derives economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by, other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use; and
(B) Is the subject of efforts that are reasonable under the circumstances to maintain its secrecy.
O.C.G.A. § 10-1-761(4). Trade secrets are protected “because the secret gives the trade secret owner a competitive advantage in the industry.” Vito v. Inman, 286 Ga. App. 646, 650 (2007).
When Georgia Power files information that it believes contains trade secrets, it must provide the legal and factual justification for that assertion in a written affidavit. Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(1)(c). Commission rules, tracking Georgia law, require affidavits to explain: why the alleged “trade secret” information derives economic value from not being generally known to others; how “others” can obtain economic value from its disclosure; and the procedures the utility uses to maintain its secrecy. Id. 
Georgia Power has the burden to prove that the information challenged in this motion is actually a trade secret. Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(7). If Georgia Power fails to substantiate any of the three requirements of a trade secret assertion for any of the challenged information, that information should be publicly disclosed. Id.

ARGUMENT

Georgia Power fails to meet its burden to establish “trade secret” protection.

The Commission should order Georgia Power to publicly disclose basic identifying information about the mystery asset for three reasons:
Georgia Power has not and cannot explain why the basic identifying information about the asset itself is a trade secret. Concealing from the public what you are negotiating to buy—during a process designed to publicly approve the attempted purchase of that asset—goes against the basic principle of the IRP process. 
There is no economic value being protected. Georgia Power’s concerns about market manipulation if such information were revealed are meritless; if the market could be manipulated by knowledge of a potential resource acquisition, that would be true in all IRP proceedings. 
Georgia Power has failed to establish that it had adequate procedures in place to maintain the secrecy of the information. Two sentences in Georgia Power’s affidavit about a “need to know” basis is not sufficient.
Georgia Power fails to explain why disclosing the mystery asset would reveal a “trade secret.”
Georgia Power failed to meet its requirement to file an affidavit to justify withholding nearly all information about this mystery asset from the public. See Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(1)(c); Affidavit and Basis for the [Trade Secret] Assertion (attached as Ex. 2).  Georgia Power attached to its IRP Amendment a general affidavit that does not explain why certain basic identifying information is trade secret. In its affidavit, Georgia Power claims trade secret protection for various categories of information, including: 
“sensitive pricing and operational capability data, including project development timelines”; 
“competitively sensitive cost information related to available technology options, processes and data used . . . in analyzing resource addition schedules. . .”;
“load and energy forecast data assumptions such as projections of new customer load . . .”; and
“specific details related to the Company’s transmission infrastructure.”
But these generic assertions clearly do not cover basic identifying information about the mystery asset the Company will ask customers to buy. Georgia Power has failed to meet its burden and the Commission should lift the overbroad trade secret designation.
Past practice indicates that the type of the sources of the electricity Georgia Power wants to buy should not be “trade secret.” A review of the last several IRPs failed to uncover a single instance when the type of electricity requested was treated as a trade secret. This holds true even when a facility is the subject of active negotiations during an IRP proceeding, as demonstrated by this IRP Amendment filing in which Georgia Power reveals the kind of electricity generation that would be purchased from another resource currently under negotiations. Technical App’x 5 (“[Georgia Power] is presently in negotiations with [REDACTED] (“Seller”) for the acquisition and construction of solar and battery energy storage systems. . . .”); see also 2010 IRP Main Doc. at 10-3, 11-6 (negotiating several potential resources at Plant Barry and at various landfill methane gas sites); compare Public Disclosure of Technical App’x 3 (attached as Ex. 1). 
Keeping basic identifying information secret regarding the mystery asset does not give Georgia Power a competitive advantage.
Trade secrets are protected “because the secret gives the trade secret owner a competitive advantage in the industry.” Vito, 286 Ga. App. at 650; Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-1-.11(1). Georgia Power fails to even allege that the basic identifying information it is withholding from public review in this IRP Amendment gives the Company a competitive advantage. As described above, none of the categories of alleged trade secrets encompass the basic identifying information of a potential asset.
Even if one tried to squeeze the basic identifying information about a resource into Georgia Power’s assertions regarding “pricing and operational capability data,” any possible alleged harm would be unconvincing. Georgia Power alleges that if “pricing and operational capability data” were revealed:
[C]ompetitors, bidders, and suppliers could use the Information to unfairly manipulate the request for proposals process and competitive market to structure future bids and set an artificial price floor to arbitrarily increase prices to the detriment of the Company and its customers. 
Public dissemination of the Information would undermine Georgia Power’s ability to negotiate the best price and contract terms and could harm the Company’s ability to secure the best cost bids and resources for the benefit of customers.
Assertion at 1.
GIPL notes that references to “bidding” manipulation ring hollow in a filing which asks the Commission to circumvent the market bidding process designed to protect ratepayers. Still, GIPL agrees that pricing information revealed mid-negotiation could derive economic value and does not ask the Commission to lift trade secret designation for any such information.
 On the other hand, it is highly unlikely that public disclosure of non-financial information about a resource Georgia Power “expect[s]” to ask the Commission to certify at some later point during this IRP would cause competitors to manipulate the market to the detriment of billpayers. And the supplier, with whom Georgia Power is presumably negotiating, is clearly already aware of this information.
The Commission has lifted trade secret designation based on unlikely assertions by Georgia Power before. In the Plant McDonough case in 2007, Georgia Power failed to meet its burden to show that a map of alternative routes for a pipeline warranted trade secret protection. The Commission held that the information in the map was “of little value” to competitors because it was “unlikely” that any competitor of Georgia Power would be interested in locating a pipeline terminating at Plant McDonough. Here too, the most basic information about this ongoing negotiation is of little value.
There is simply no economic value for purposes of trade secret protection in basic identifying information about potential resources in the public IRP process. 
Georgia Power has failed to establish that it uses adequate procedures to maintain secrecy. 
Although the Georgia Power describes its secrecy procedures as “substantial,” the only mentioned procedure for Georgia Power and Southern Company is the following: “Only select Georgia Power and Southern Company personnel are granted access to the trade secret portions of the Information. Those personnel receive access only on a ‘need to know’ basis.” Assertion at 2. The affidavit does not provide any other details about how or whether these employees have been directed to maintain the secrecy of its alleged trade secrets. Further, the affidavit offers scant assurances about how such information is treated between the Company and the seller with whom it is currently in negotiations.
These assertions are insufficient to establish the existence of a trade secret. Georgia courts have found that the existence of a general non-disclosure agreement, without more, can be insufficient to maintain secrecy for purposes of trade secret analysis. Equifax Servs., Inc. v. Examination Mgmt. Servs., Inc., 216 Ga. App. 35, 39–40 (1994). Similarly, the level of guidance given to employees about how to treat information, how information is stored on computers, and how information is marked on documents is all relevant to whether Georgia Power has met its burden. See Diamond Power Int'l, Inc. v. Davidson, 540 F. Supp. 2d 1322, 1335–37 (N.D. Ga. 2007).
If Georgia Power justifies its trade secret designation over the mystery asset, the Commission should not consider the asset in this IRP Amendment.
If Georgia Power manages to present information that justifies its expansive trade secret designation over the mystery asset, then Georgia Power should remove the asset from this IRP Amendment and file a new amendment when it can withstand the full public scrutiny required before billpayers are asked to invest. See supra pages 2-4; see also O.C.G.A. § 46-3A-1(7) (defining integrated resource plan as one which “contains the utility’s analysis of all capacity resource options . . . [and] practical alternatives to the fuel type and method of generation of the proposed electric generating facilities . . .”). Commission regulations are clear that Georgia Power cannot make a “binding commitment” for a supply resource without first getting approval for that asset by filing an IRP or IRP Amendment. See, e.g. Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.06(5) (Georgia Power is required to file an IRP Amendment if it “anticipates the need to . . . make a binding commitment for the acquisition . . . of a . . . supply resource excepted from the RFP process . . .”). Merely “supplementing” an existing filing with critical new information, as Georgia Power intends to do—after key deadlines have passed and potentially after the period for intervention has expired—is insufficient. See generally id.; see, e.g., Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.06(4)(b).
Commission regulations and practice are equally clear that an IRP process is designed to be a public process with public input, which is not possible if the very purchases in question cannot be shared with the public. See e.g., supra, pages 2-4. Proceeding with an IRP in which the public could not effectively be involved or make an informed decision about whether to intervene is contrary to this Commission’s practice of valuing public scrutiny and Georgia’s strong public policy against “closed door” politics. Williams v. DeKalb County, 308 Ga. 265, 275 (2020) (interpreting the purpose of the Open Meetings Act); see also O.C.G.A § 50-18-70(a) (reiterating “that the strong public policy of this state is in favor of open government . . .”).

have found that the existence of a general non-disclosure agreement, without more, can be

insufficient to maintain secrecy for purposes of trade secret analysis. Equifax Servs., Inc. v.

Examination Mgmt. Servs., Inc., 216 Ga. App. 35, 39–40 (1994). Similarly, the level of guidance

given to employees about how to treat information, how information is stored on computers, and

how information is marked on documents is all relevant to whether Georgia Power has met its

burden. See Diamond Power Int'l, Inc. v. Davidson, 540 F. Supp. 2d 1322, 1335–37 (N.D. Ga.

2007).

II.      If Georgia Power justifies its trade secret designation over the mystery asset, the
         Commission should not consider the asset in this IRP Amendment.

         If Georgia Power manages to present information that justifies its expansive trade secret

designation over the mystery asset, then Georgia Power should remove the asset from this IRP

Amendment and file a new amendment when it can withstand the full public scrutiny required

before billpayers are asked to invest. See supra pages 2-4; see also O.C.G.A. § 46-3A-1(7)

(defining integrated resource plan as one which “contains the utility’s analysis of all capacity

resource options . . . [and] practical alternatives to the fuel type and method of generation of the

proposed electric generating facilities . . .”). Commission regulations are clear that Georgia Power

cannot make a “binding commitment” for a supply resource without first getting approval for that

asset by filing an IRP or IRP Amendment. See, e.g. Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. 515-3-4-.06(5) (Georgia

Power is required to file an IRP Amendment if it “anticipates the need to . . . make a binding

                  AFFIDAVIT AND BASIS FOR THE ASSERTION THAT PORTIONS OF THE
                     INFORMATION SUBMITTED ARE PROTECTED TRADE SECRETS

         As part of its 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update (“2023 IRP Update”), filed in Docket No. 55378,
Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) submits to the Georgia Public Service
Commission a Technical Appendix, which contains power purchase agreements and data supporting
proposed projects including an acquisition and Company-owned proposals, which include sensitive pricing,
resource operational capability data, and development timelines, and also certain information regarding the
Company’s proprietary planning processes, financial data, technology screening assumptions, generic
technology and resource cost information, specific resource, technology and avoided cost information,
economic analyses, load and energy forecast data assumptions, and other data, including confidential details
regarding current and future transmission projects (the “Information”). Certain portions of the Information
are trade secrets of Georgia Power and Southern Company and their affiliates and is therefore protected from
public disclosure under Commission Rule 515-3-1-.11.

         The trade secret portions of the Information derive economic value from not being generally
known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by other persons who can obtain
economic value from their disclosure or use. Specifically, the trade secret portions of the Information
contained herein include competitively sensitive pricing and operational capability data, including project
development timelines where applicable, specific to the resources under contract within each power
purchase agreement or proposed to be acquired or developed. If the Information were made public,
competitors, bidders, and suppliers could use the Information to unfairly manipulate the request for
proposals process and competitive market to structure future bids and set an artificial price floor to
arbitrarily increase prices to the detriment of the Company and its customers. Public dissemination of the
Information would undermine Georgia Power’s ability to negotiate the best price and contract terms and
could harm the Company’s ability to secure the best cost bids and resources for the benefit of customers.
In addition, the terms of the power purchase agreements themselves provides for the confidentiality and
protection of the Information. Compromising the confidentiality of such Information could harm Georgia
Power in future PPA negotiations, as counterparties may fear compelled disclosure of key contractual
terms. Lastly, the Company’s competitors are not required to reveal or publish similar information.

         The trade secret portions of the Information also contain competitively sensitive cost information
related to available technology options, processes and data used by Georgia Power in analyzing resource
addition schedules, financial data used in the Company’s resource analyses, and other confidential details
including data supporting resource economic evaluations. Public dissemination of the trade secret
portions of the Information would allow Georgia Power’s competitors and suppliers to have access to such
processes and strategies and thereby gain an unfair competitive advantage in the marketplace.
Competitors would obtain an unfair advantage because they are not required to reveal similar information
and can utilize such trade secret portions of the Information to manipulate pricing and timing of supply
to the disadvantage of Georgia Power. Competitors would also unfairly benefit in having access and insight
into the Company’s planning processes and methodologies. This competitive advantage for the
Company’s suppliers and competitors would mean that Georgia Power will potentially pay higher prices
to suppliers, ultimately harming Georgia Power and its customers.

STF-ASR-2-1

Question:

Has Georgia Power Company, or any of its affiliates, conducted any activities (e.g., research, a pilot program, investigations, drilling and collecting sediment core samples) in Georgia to assess the feasibility of storing carbon underground? If so, provide the location of each activity and a description of the activity. 

Response:

Georgia Power has constructed four stratigraphic test borings in Georgia in an effort to determine the feasibility of injecting CO2 underground for permanent storage. The purpose of each of these test borings is solely to better understand the geologic formations deep underground and none of the test borings are intended for CO2 storage. Upon completion of the test, each test boring is safely decommissioned, and the sites are appropriately restored. The locations of these borings, and a brief description of the work conducted at each site, are provided below:
2021 – Cassville, Georgia, Bartow County – Latitude: 34.240542, Longitude: -84.905123
One stratigraphic test boring was drilled to a depth of 6,030 feet below land surface. A surface casing was set and cemented to a depth of 606 feet to isolate the overlying zone. Upon completion of drilling, the following activities were performed: borehole logging for physical and chemical properties and sidewall rock coring and analysis. Upon completion of logging and coring, the borehole was cemented from the total depth to land surface. A final report was received from the contractor in March 2022. Data collected will be used in conjunction with 2024 data from the nearby Kingston, GA site to provide a geologic feasibility-level evaluation of CO2 sequestration in this area.
2024 – Kingston, Georgia, Bartow County – Latitude: 34.217222; Longitude: -84.920000
One stratigraphic test boring was drilled to a depth of 9,055 feet in June 2024. A surface casing was set and cemented to a depth of 1,005 feet to isolate the overlying zone. Upon completion of drilling, the following activities were performed: borehole logging for physical and chemical properties, sidewall rock coring and analysis, fluid sampling, and one pressure fall-off test in the formation. Upon completion of logging, coring, and testing, the borehole was cemented from the total depth to land surface. Additionally, 2D seismic acquisition of the area was conducted to evaluate the regional geology, correlate the Cassville and Kingston borehole geology, and evaluate regional CO2 storage capacities. Data collected from this effort will be used in conjunction with 2021 data from the nearby Cassville, GA site to provide a feasibility-level evaluation of CO2 sequestration in this area. A final report is expected in October 2024.
2024 – Thalmann, Georgia, Wayne County – Latitude: 31.356435; Longitude: -81.723228
One stratigraphic test boring was drilled to a depth of 6,634 feet. A surface casing was set and cemented to a depth of 3,235 feet to isolate the overlying zone. Upon completion of drilling, the following activities were performed: borehole logging for physical and chemical properties, sidewall rock coring and analysis, fluid sampling, and one pressure fall-off test in the formation. The Thalmann borehole was cemented from total depth to land surface. Additionally, historical 2D seismic data from the area will be reprocessed to evaluate geology regionally (i.e., between the Thalmann and Waverly drilling locations). Data will be used to provide a feasibility-level evaluation of CO2 sequestration in this area. A final report is expected in January 2025.
2024 – Waverly, Georgia, Brantley County – Latitude: 31.102499; Longitude: -81.869340
Drilling at the Waverly location commenced on August 28. One stratigraphic test boring will be drilled to a depth of approximately 6,000 feet. Upon completion of drilling, the following activities will be performed: borehole logging for physical and chemical properties, rock coring and analysis, fluid sampling, and pressure fall-off testing in the formations. Additionally, historical 2D seismic data from the area will be reprocessed to evaluate geology regionally (i.e., between the Thalmann and Waverly drilling locations). Data will be used to provide a feasibility-level evaluation of CO2 sequestration in this area. A final report is expected in February 2025.

INTRODUCTION

        The Cassville #1 Stratigraphic Test Borehole was drilled in the Valley and Ridge
geologic province on Bartow County, Georgia, to explore the potential for geologic
storage of carbon dioxide proximal to Plant Bowen and Plant Hammond. Located on a
flood plain at -84°54’21” W, 34°14’22” N, the borehole was drilled at an elevation of 719
feet. Total depth of 6,030 feet below ground surface was reached on 9/3/2021, but logging
was conducted from 5,000 feet to 100 feet below ground surface due to operational issues
caused by the regional structure and detailed in the report sections below. Georgia Power
contracted Advanced Resources International to drill the test boring location, collect well
logs and sidewall core samples, and analyze the collected data.


        This report presents in the following sections a description of the drilling, logging,
log analysis, regional geology, and geologic structure. Characteristics of a potential target

Narrative on Cassville Georgia Strat Test Operations
        The Cassville #1 Stratigraphic Test Borehole was drilled in Bartow County,
Georgia to explore the potential for geologic storage of carbon dioxide proximal to Plant
Bowen and Plant Hammond. Located on a flood plain at -84°54’21” W, 34°14’22” N, the
borehole was drilled at an elevation of 719 feet. The Cassville Georgia deep geologic
borehole is drilled in Kingston, Georgia within Bartow County. Its purpose is to collect
lithologic, stratigraphic, and petrophysical data on the subsurface geology to determine
the feasibility of CO2 storage in northwestern Georgia. The borehole was spud on August
10th, 2021, following site preparation and mobilization of the drill rig to Kingston, Georgia.
Conductor casing was set at 40 feet below ground surface (BGS), where hard rock was
quickly encountered. Initially, the near-surface bedrocks consisted of limestone,
dolostone, and chert. Because of the lack of pore space, highly cemented hard crystalline
lithologies, and chert content, the drilling rate averaged approximately 2 feet per hour for
the first portion of drilling with a 12 1/4” bit. Immediately after commencing drilling, natural
fractures were encountered at a depth of 65 feet. The drilling mud was lost to the
formation, and operations stopped until a cement truck arrived on location to spot cement
and seal off the fractures to prevent further loss of drilling mud and contamination.

        After allowing the cement to set, drilling continued with a slow Rate of Penetration
(ROP) between 2 and 4 feet per hour until another network of fractures was encountered
at 261 feet on August 14th. Drilling mud was lost, and two slugs of Lost Circulation
Material (LCM) were pumped to develop a mud cake and seal off the fractures. These
efforts proved unsuccessful, and cement was called back to location to fully seal the

fractures. The first slug of cement was absorbed into the highly fractured formation, and
a second batch of cement was mixed and pumped into the borehole. After the cement
had set, the borehole was drilled out until new hole could be made below the now-sealed
fractures. As the drill pipe reached greater depths, the drill string weight increased which
allowed more weight to be put on the bit and subsequent incremental increases in ROP.
However, the increased weight on bit coupled with the hard rock wore out drill bits faster,
so they were changed frequently as necessary to maximize efficiency and maintain
improved ROP.

        Circulation of the drilling mud was lost at a depth of 607 feet. With surface casing
on location, the decision was made to set surface casing to a depth of 606 feet, one foot
above total depth. The surface casing was prepared, welded, and run in the hole. The
Blowout Preventer (BOP) was welded to the casing, providing a safety measure to
maintain borehole control. Cement was pumped through surface casing and into the
annular space between the surface casing and the open borehole. Due to the formation
fractures, the cement level slowly fell from surface, and additional cement was placed in
the annulus from surface. After allowing the cement to set, a Cement Bond Log (CBL)
was deployed on wireline and determined adequate coverage behind surface casing.
Afterward, the BOP was nippled up and successfully pressure tested to ensure
operational safety during continued drilling.

        The larger 12 1/4” bit was swapped for a smaller 8 3/4” bit to fit through the surface
casing, which enabled higher drilling rates. With shallower geologic zones isolated by
surface casing and a BOP in place, rotary rotation was increased along with mud
circulation rates. Once out of the hard rock formations at a depth of approximately 825
feet, the tri-cone bit was swapped for a PDC bit, to improve drilling in the limestone and
shale intervals. At this depth, chert was no longer encountered, suggesting a stratigraphic
transition from the Knox Group into the Conasauga Group. The combination of these
factors increased the drilling rate to 30 feet per hour, with periods exceeding 60 feet per
hour. Drilling continued at this increased speed to a depth of approximately 1,600 feet
and the borehole was noted to have a slight deviation.

        The deviation of the borehole was an anticipated complexity. Geologic beds in this
area are steeply dipping, which creates an operational challenge to maintain a vertical
borehole trajectory. In preparation for the steeply dipping beds, the Bottomhole Assembly
(BHA) was packed with stabilizers to help maintain a straight hole and was equipped with
a Teledrift tool which surveys the inclination of the borehole. Once the inclination was
measured above 4 degrees, Weight on Bit (WOB) was decreased, and the stabilizers
were placed further away from the bit. These efforts decreased the drilling rate, and a
directional drilling crew was brought to site to straighten the borehole and return the
borehole to a vertical trajectory. Once rigged up, the directional drillers straightened the
borehole while maintaining ROPs averaging around or above 30 feet per hour. At a depth
of approximately 2,435 feet, clastic rocks were encountered in the form of siltstone. This
transitioned to sandstone starting at a depth of 2,580 feet. For another 700 feet, the
sandstone package continued until shale and limestone was encountered at a depth of
3,280 feet. Shale dominates the section until 3,500 feet, after which sandstone and
siltstone dominate the lithology with shale and limestone. Shale becomes the dominant
lithology starting at 4,820 feet with a minor component of sandstone. Limestone and
sandstone return at a depth of approximately 5,720 feet, and slate is encountered around
and below the target depth of 6,000 feet. The borehole reached a Total Depth (TD) of
6,030 feet.

        After the borehole was drilled, logging tools were trucked to location and run in the
hole by Schlumberger. First, a triple combo was run in, which consists of resistivity
measurements, gamma ray, spontaneous potential (SP), and porosity. The SP
measurements encountered issues while logging and was not re-run to correct.
Afterwards, the sonic tool and Formation Micro-Imager (FMI) tool were run together.
Locations were selected for sidewall coring based on the triple combo suite, and the
sidewall coring tool was run. Of the 25 samples attempted, 17 remained in suitable
condition and were sent to Schlumberger’s laboratory for Routine Core Analysis (RCA).

        With the targeted information collected from the borehole, cement was mixed and
pumped into the open hole to plug and abandon the borehole. Cement was pumped in
500-foot sections, allowing 2 hours between plugs to allow the cement to set. Once the

WELL LOG, CORE ANALYSIS, AND X-RAY DIFFRACTION ANALYSIS

By: Dr. Jack Pashin, Pashin Geoscience, LLC

Introduction
        The Cassville #1 well was drilled in the southern Appalachian thrust belt near
Cassville, Georgia to explore for potential geologic CO2 sinks in Cambrian strata. The
location of the well is -84°54’21” W, 34°14’22” N. The well was drilled at a surface
elevation of 719 ft and reached a total depth of 6,030 ft. The lower section of the well is
tortuous because of structural deformation, and so the well was logged from a depth of
5,000 ft to a near-surface depth of 100 ft. The mud log and geophysical well logs record
a thick succession of Cambrian-Ordovician carbonate and siliciclastic strata, including the
Rome Formation, Conasauga Group, and the basal part of the Knox Group (Copper
Ridge Dolomite). Surface casing was set at to a depth of 606 ft, in the Maynardville
Limestone, which forms the top of the Conasauga Group, and the log suite includes SP,
resistivity, density, density and neutron porosity, sonic, and FMI logs and records a broad
range of rock properties between 4,980 and 606 ft. After logging, 18 sidewall cores were
recovered from depths ranging from 4,421 to 2,234 ft. Routine core analysis was
performed to determine basic reservoir properties (porosity, permeability, fluid
saturation), and X-ray diffraction analysis was performed to determine the mineralogy of
selected samples.

        This report summarizes the results of geophysical log analysis and core analysis
and focuses on geologic interpretation of these results. The report begins with a
discussion of the geophysical well logs, including interpretation of rock types, rock
properties, stratigraphic interpretation, and structural interpretation. The FMI log was
critical for structural interpretation and yields high-quality borehole images in most
intervals, as well as data on structural dip and natural fracture systems. Routine core
analysis was performed by the Dean-Stark method by Schlumberger, and this report
presents and interprets the results of this analysis. X-ray diffraction results, and thin
section analysis also are discussed, and the report concludes with interpretation of the
CO2 storage potential of the Cassville area based on the drilling and analytical results.

Geophysical Well Logs: Triple Combo
        Interpretation of the deepest part of the geophysical well log is complicated by
heterogeneous stratigraphy and wellbore expansion beyond a depth of 4,180 ft. The basal
part of the well contains interbedded limestone and shale, which may be part of the
Nolichucky Shale, which is in the upper part of the Conasauga Group (Figure 2). A major

FMI Log
        The imaging quality of the FMI log is exceptional throughout most of the wellbore.
Bedding is generally shown clearly, and numerous fracture and fault systems are imaged.
Imaging problems were encountered mainly in zones of borehole expansion, particularly
in the lower Rome section. Plotting the azimuth and dip of bedding against the gamma
ray and porosity logs shows the major patterns of bedding attitude in the Cassville #1 well
(Figure 4). Shear zones with abundant fractures are common in the wellbore, and most
of the fractures have low resistivity fills, suggestive of clay or fluid fill. Many fractures in
the region are filled with carbonate, and these types of veins are difficult to image in FMI
logs because of low resistivity contrast with the host rocks, which are dominated by
carbonate.

FMI images

        An FMI image showing well-bedded dolomite and shale in the upper part of the
Pumpkin Valley Shale (2,481-2,508 ft) shows bedding that generally dips <10° and is
gently folded, with dip generally oriented NE and SW (Figure 5). Two fracture zones are
shown in the image that consist of abundant short-length high-angle fractures generally
dipping between 71 and 83° N-NE and S-SW. The fractures commonly cross-cut, defining
a conjugate shear system.

        Another image from the upper part of the Pumpkin Valley Shale (2,822-2848 ft)
shows a higher proportion of dolomite (Figure 6). Shear fractures dipping 60-80° increase
in abundance downward in the image and dip dominantly N-NE with dip magnitude
ranging from 60-80°. Bedding is gently folded, and bedding dip is generally <10° and dip
direction in the upper part of the changes from S-SW to S-SE in the upper part of the
image. In the lower part of the image, shear fractures swarm around a fold hinge in which
dip changes from about 5° toward the E to about 20° toward the NW.

        Faults are commonly expressed in the FMI log as discontinuities in dip and bedding
across shear fractures and shear zones. Faults are especially well imaged in the lower
part of the Pumpkin Valley Shale from 3,303-3,330 ft (Figure 11). A prominent fault
dipping 70° toward the NE is imaged at 3,312 ft where a low-resistivity shale is juxtaposed
with high-resistivity limestone. A satellite fault at 3,309 ft dipping 51° toward the NE is
imaged faintly in the static FMI image and is distinctly shown by bedding discontinuities
in the high-contrast dynamic image. Bedding dip, however, has a distinct westward
component and ranges between 3 and 20° throughout the image.

        Fold hinges typically are characterized by dip changes in the FMI log and are most
distinct where they contain abundant shear fractures; many of the hinges appear faulted.
A simple fold nose is imaged near the top of the Nolichucky Shale (984-997 ft) (Figure
12). The fold nose images distinctly at 990 ft and may contain overturned bedding in one
of the limbs. Strata below the nose dip about 10° toward the SE, whereas strata above
the nose dip up to 40° S-SW, suggesting that the fold has a conical kink-band geometry.

Structural interpretation

        Plotting dip azimuth, dip magnitude, and the positions of shear zones adjacent to
the stratigraphic section reveals some of the major structural trends in the Cassville #1
well (Figure 4). In the apparent Nolichucky Shale section at the bottom of the well, strata
dip > 50° toward the SE. Above the interpreted thrust fault that places the lower Rome
Formation above the Nolichucky (4,700-4,870 ft), strata dip erratically between 15 and
50° toward the E-NE, indicating complex folding and faulting above the main thrust fault.
From 4,700-4,250 ft, dip magnitude in the lower Rome varies greatly from ~10-50°, and
dip azimuth also is variable, indicating even more complex folding and faulting. Strata are
pervasively sheared between depths of 4,200 and 4,300 ft, and bedding dip is difficult to
detect.

        Structural dip in the Pumpkin Valley Shale is typically 10° or less, save for a few
zones where dip reaches a maximum value of 32° (Figure 4). Dip direction clusters in the
NE and NW quadrants in the interbedded limestone, sandstone, and shale succession
near the base of the formation. In the shale-limestone interval between 3,151 and 3,311
ft, strata tend to dip NW. The thick dolomite interval in the upper part of the Pumpkin
Valley exhibits highly variable dip direction indicating that the strata are broadly folded
and undulatory; numerous shear zones are present and commonly separate dipping
panels. The shale forming the top of the Pumpkin Valley Formation is strongly sheared in
places.

        The basal part of the dolomite unit constituting the Rutledge Limestone contains
strata dipping steeper than 60°, and dip direction is variable but most commonly N-NE
(Figure 4). The middle part of the limestone is intensely sheared between 2,100 and
2,200 ft, and it is difficult to distinguish between bedding and shear fracture networks.
Strata in the upper part of the Rutledge, however, dip westward, and dip decreases
upward from about 40-25°.

        Structural dip remains low in the Rogersville Shale and Maryville Limestone.
Rogersville strata dip between 10 and 20°, whereas Maryville strata commonly dip <10°
(Figure 4). Dip direction in the Rogersville is generally NE to S., whereas that in the
Maryville is typically SW, although NE values are common near the top of the limestone.
In the basal part of the Nolichucky Shale, dip is generally <20°, and dip values are
concentrated in the NE and SE quadrants, indicating significant folding. Shear zones are
abundant near the middle of the Nolichucky, and dip is variable in the middle and upper
Nolichucky, reaching maximum values around 50°. In the Maynardville Limestone, Dip is
commonly on the order of 20°, and dip direction is variable, with measurements clustering
in the NE and NW quadrants.

X-Ray Diffraction and thin section Analysis
        X-ray diffraction analysis was performed on 9 samples at depths of 3,508-3,894 ft
(Tables 2, 3). The shallowest sample (3,508 ft) is from near the base of the Pumpkin
Valley Shale and is arkosic sandstone with 41% quartz, 21% K-feldspar, 6% calcite, and
11% dolomite (Table 2; Figure 18). The sample contains 19% clay minerals, including
illite-mica (15%) and kaolinite (4%) (Table 3). Quartz and feldspar form the framework of
the sandstone, and porosity is present as intergranular and what appears to be vuggy
porosity (Figure 19). Sand grains are very fine- to fine-grained and angular to subround.
Porosity comprises 6.5% of the rock volume; sparry calcite fills the vuggy pores, and
dolomite crystals are dispersed in the matrix. Interparticle pores are common in the rock
matrix, and many of the voids may be a product of dissolution of feldspar and other labile
minerals; kaolinite is probably a product of felspar dissolution. No plagioclase is in the
sample, suggesting that it may have been dissolved, thus contributing to interparticle
porosity. Clay-filled pressure solution seams also are present in the sandstone.
Permeability if the sandstone is only 0.096 mD, indicating poor interconnection of the
pores, which reflects the high clay volume.

        The remainder of the samples are from the upper part of the Rome Formation, and
all but the deepest sample are from the porous dolomite interval (Figure 2). A sample
from 3,713 ft is dolomite containing 6% quartz, 4% potassium feldspar, 88% dolomite
(Table 2; Figure 18). Clay forms only 3% of the sample is composed of illite-mica (Tables
2, 3). The rock is dominated by finely crystalline dolomite, and detrital quartz and feldspar
grains are irregularly distributed in the sample; the grains are mainly supported by the
dolomite matrix (Figure 20). Interstitial clay also is irregularly distributed in the sample.
Porosity of the sample is 11.8%, and the thin section indicates that vuggy porosity
dominates and is irregularly distributed. Klinkenberg permeability is 59 mD, indicating that
the pore system is well interconnected.

Conclusions
        The Cassville #1 well was drilled into a thick succession of Cambrian strata in the
Appalachian thrust belt of Bartow County, Georgia that includes the Rome Formation, the
Conasauga Group, and the basal Knox Group. Geophysical well logs record rock
properties in Rome and Conasauga strata. The lower Rome Formation contains mainly
interbedded shale, sandstone, and limestone, whereas the upper Rome is dominated by
dolomite and shale. The Pumpkin Valley Shale forms the base of the Conasauga Group
and is composed mainly of carbonate rocks at this location. The lower Pumpkin Valley
contains interbedded limestone, sandstone, shale, and dolomite, whereas the upper part
of the Pumpkin Valley contains a thick dolomite unit that is overlain by a thin radioactive
shale interval. Above the Pumpkin Valley is the Rutledge Limestone, which is composed
mainly of dolomite. The Rutledge is overlain by glauconitic Rogersville Shale, which is in
turn overlain by the Maryville Limestone and interbedded shale and limestone of the
Nolichucky Shale. The Nolichucky is overlain by a thick dolomite unit assigned to the
Maynardville Limestone, which is in turn overlain chert-bearing limestone and dolomite
that is characteristic of the Copper Ridge Dolomite of the Knox Group.

        The bottom of the well apparently penetrated the Nolichucky Shale below a
significant thrust fault (~3.870 ft) that may be a thrust ramp or upper-level detachment
that is developed above imbricated Cambrian-Ordovician carbonate rocks. Above the
Nolichucky, Rome-Conasauga units appear to be in normal stratigraphic order. However,
FMI imaging and interpretation indicate that this section is internally structurally complex.
Strata in the Rome Formation are complexly folded and sheared, commonly dipping
steeper than 50°, establishing the Rome as a structurally weak layer. Structural dip is
generally <10° in the Pumpkin Valley Shale, and abundant shear zones and variable dip
direction indicate significant internal deformation including layer-parallel shear. The basal
part of the Rutledge Limestone dips steeper than 60°, indicating a significant structural
discontinuity, such as a detachment at the base. Dip decreases upward in the Rutledge,
and the Rogersville-Maryville section forms a gently dipping structural panel similar to that
in the Pumpkin Valley Shale. Strata in the Nolichucky Shale are complexly folded and
sheared, with dip up to 50°, constituting an upper-level weak layer that is in some respects

similar to the lower Rome. Above this deformation zone, Maynardville and younger strata
dip ~20°with variable dip direction, and shear zones are distributed throughout the
section, underscoring the complexity of internal deformation in the Cambrian section.
Shear related fractures have variable orientations but tend to cluster in the SE quadrant,
reflecting northwestward translation of strata in the thrust belt.

        Core analysis and geophysical log analysis reveal the development of significant
porosity and permeability in the upper Rome dolomite section between depths of 3,713
and 3,803 ft. Porosity is developed principally in dolomite and is locally as high as 19.9%.
Porosity values in candidate reservoir dolomite range from 8.0-17.4%, and Klinkenberg
permeability ranges from 4.1-135 mD. Water saturation in the candidate reservoir zone
ranges from 34.0-67.2%. Oil saturation varies from 7.0-36.8%, but there are significant
errors in determination of fluid saturations because of limited sample pore volume.
Regardless, some hydrocarbon production potential may exist in the upper Rome porous
dolomite interval.

        X-ray diffraction and thin section analysis were used to establish rock composition,
rock fabric, and the nature of upper Rome pore systems. Upper Rome strata can be
classified principally as dolomite and siliceous dolomite, and some evaporitic strata were
identified that contain gypsum and anhydrite. Pore systems include vuggy porosity,
fracture porosity, and intercrystalline porosity, and Klinkenberg permeability values
ranging from 4-135 mD indicate a moderately connected to well-connected pore system.
The porous dolomite interval has structural dip of about 40°, and relatively little is known
about the overall geometry and continuity of these strata. Accordingly, additional
investigation including seismic exploration is recommended to better understand and
predict reservoir properties and structural continuity in the Rome Formation.

Introduction and Site Selection
        Advanced Resources International (ARI) is working on a project to assess the
potential for carbon sequestration in the lower Cambrian stratigraphy of northwest
Georgia. ARI subcontracted Petrologic Solutions Inc. (Petrologic) in December 2020 to
help identify potential sites for drilling a stratigraphic test boring to evaluate the presence
of suitable host rock. ARI provided the following criteria to Petrologic for siting the test
boring:

     Proximity to and accessibility to Plant Bowen and Plant Hammond, both of which
        are large producers of CO2;

     A potentially porous reservoir (most likely sandstone and/or siltstone of the Rome
        Formation and/or Chilhowee Group that have the potential for primary and
        secondary permeability and porosity);

     An impermeable cap rock (most likely shale of the Conasauga Group);

     Well site should avoid protracted drilling in the Knox Group that overlies the
        Conasauga because of the high chert content of the Knox lithologies;

     Have limited up dip connection to ground surface to avoid leakage the porous
        reservoir;

     The target unit should be overlain by at least 2500 feet (760 meters) of
        stratigraphy to avoid the potential for hydrofracking the rock; and,

     The property should preferably be owned by either Southern Company Services
        (SCS) or Georgia Power.

Regional Geology

        Bartow County is located within the Blue Ridge and Valley and Ridge
physiographic provinces with the southeastern portion of Bartow County occurring in the
Blue Ridge. The oldest exposed rocks in this area occur within the Corbin Massif (Corbin
Granite, Rowland Springs Formation and Red Top Mountain Formation), which is a
complex of Proterozoic aged rocks. Rocks within Corbin Massif occur within the hanging
wall of the Great Smoky Fault. The Great Smoky Fault represents the structural boundary
between the Blue Ridge and Valley and Ridge physiographic provinces, with the Valley
and Ridge occupying much of the west/northwest portions of the County.

        Valley and Ridge rocks of the Chilhowee Group, Shady Dolomite, and Conasauga
Group are exposed west of the Great Smoky Fault in a series of imbricate thrust faults,
including the Cloverleaf, White and Cassville faults (Kath et al., 2015). These Paleozoic-
age sedimentary rocks have been folded into a series of anticlines and synclines broken
by numerous thrust (reverse) faults and lateral strike-slip faults. The ridges are primarily
held up by layers of sedimentary rock more resistant to weathering, such as sandstone,
whereas the valleys are underlain by shale and limestone that readily weathers.

        The area within and around Cassville is primarily underlain by rocks of the middle
to upper Cambrian Conasauga Group and Cambro-Ordovician Knox Group. The oldest
sedimentary rocks exposed near the site occur within the middle and upper Conasauga
Group. The Conasauga Group consists primarily of sandstone and siltstone near the
base (lower) which grades upward into calcareous shale and limestone/dolostone (middle
and upper).    The overlying Knox Group is comprised of variably cherty dolostone,

Valley and Ridge

Lower Cambrian Chilhowee Group-

        Early workers mapped the Lower Cambrian clastic rocks below the Shady
Dolomite as a single unit referred to as the Weisner Formation and named for Mount
Weisner, the type area in southern Cherokee County, Alabama. These rocks have since
been correlated with the Chilhowee Group (Mack, 1980; Bearce, 1977, 1985), and
consequently are referred to as such in this summary.

        The Chilhowee Group clastics comprise the oldest sediments deposited on the
southeast margin of the North American craton (Laurentian margin) at the beginning of
the Cambrian transgression. Mack (1980) observed this sequence of clastics in the
Indian Mountain area in exposures east of Piedmont, Alabama, through Maxwell Gap
near Borden Springs, Alabama, and subdivided the exposure of Chilhowee in Maxwell
Gap into four formations based on the occurrence of depositional facies. Total thickness
of the Chilhowee in this section was measured to be 2165 ft (660 m); the base of the
Chilhowee is not present in this exposure, however, indicating that the Chilhowee is
thicker than reported. The four formations delineated by Mack (1980) include (from oldest
to youngest):

Lower Cambrian Shady Dolomite-

Cartersville Mining District. Where exposed, the Shady is light-gray to light-bluish gray to
medium dark-gray, thick- to massive-bedded dolostone and dolomitic limestone. The
Shady is locally siliceous and weathers to an umber- or ocher-colored “chocolate brown”
clay residuum which is often associated with iron oxide deposits. Because of intense
silicification associated with mineralization, weathering of the Shady commonly yields
jasperiod, also closely associated with iron ore deposits of the Shady.

Lower Cambrian Rome Formation-

        The Rome Formation was named by Hayes (1891) for exposures on Walker
Mountain in Floyd County, Georgia, near Rome, Georgia, and has been assigned to the
lower and middle Cambrian. The Rome consists of gray silty clay shale that weathers to
a characteristic tan, orange, pink or maroon color and commonly displays a distinctive
silvery sheen. Silty shale beds are commonly dolomitic, and in fresh exposures they have
the appearance of massive dolostone. Thin to thick layers of gray, purple, white, pale-
red, and green siltstone and sandstone beds, some of which are dolomitic, are also
scattered throughout the shale, particularly in the upper part of the formation. The Rome
is relatively resistant to weathering and generally forms low-relief ridges. Where present
in the exposures, the red and green siltstones and sandstones are characteristic.
Cressler (1970) estimates the Rome to be between 490 and 985 ft thick (150 to 300m).

Middle and Upper Cambrian Conasauga Group-

        Hayes (1891) named the Conasauga Formation for exposures along the
Conasauga River in Whitfield and Murray Counties, Georgia. The Conasauga has been
given group status and is comprised of Maynardville Limestone, Nolichucky Shale,
Maryville Limestone, Rogersville Shale, Rutledge Limestone and Pumpkin Valley Shale
in Tennessee. Cressler (1970) divided the Conasauga into an eastern and western belt
in Floyd County and Polk County, Georgia. Lithologically, the two belts differ significantly;
the eastern belt consists of limestone and shale in nearly equal proportions, whereas the

Regional Structural Geology

        Published geologic maps around Cassville (Croft, 1963; Cressler, 1970; Georgia
State Geologic Map, 1976; Kath and Crawford, 2009) show that the area is underlain by
the Knox Group and various lithologies of the Conasauga Group. Croft (1963) subdivided
the Conasauga Group into lower, middle, and upper stratigraphy, which is also reflected
on the 1976 State Geologic Map.           The lower unit is mainly dolostone with some
interbedded limestone and shale; the middle units is mainly shale and calcareous shale;
and the upper unit is mainly interbedded shale and limestone. Cressler (1970) and Kath
and Crawford (2009) show the Conasauga Group rocks mostly comprised of three
lithologies. The lower most lithology is mainly thin- to thick-bedded gray limestone; the

of limestone; and the upper unit is thinly to massively bedded gray dolostone that contains

        The structural interpretations of the areal distribution of lithologic units in the
Cassville area are variable. Croft (1963) shows the site to be near the axis of an
overturned anticline with an overturned syncline immediately to the east of the site.
Cressler (1970) and Kath and Crawford (2009) show the site to be near the western end
of either a normal stratigraphic inlier and/or a gently westward plunging anticline.

Cassville Site Geologic Conditions

        The detailed geologic mapping was performed within and around the site on April
14, 2021, and April 16, 2021 using the Kingston, GA USGS 7.5-minute topographic
quadrangle as a base map. In general, the mapping was focused on geologic features
exposed on and around the substation property. Information recorded at each map
station includes: lithology and mineralogy; orientation and characteristics of structural

discontinuities including, faulting, jointing, cleavage, and compositional layering; and
depth and type of weathering characteristics of the rock. Map station locations were
recorded using a hand-held, Wide Area Augmentation System (WAAS)-enabled Global
Positioning System (GPS) and the Avenza Maps application.

        The Cassville site occurs within the Valley and Ridge physiographic province,
which occupies most of the county and is characterized by a series of long, narrow
northeast/southwest-trending ridges separated by wide parallel valleys. Topographic
relief within this province is about 800 to 1000 feet, with topographic lows of about 650 to
700 feet above mean sea level (ft. msl) occurring in the river valleys and topographic
highs up to 1,600 ft. msl on nearby ridges.

        Contacts between three different geologic units were observed during the geologic
mapping. Two mappable lithologic units associated with the Conasauga Group were
observed in the central and eastern portions of the site. The central unit, at and west of
the drill site, is comprised primarily of calcareous and silty shale that is generally
characterized as being deeply weathered, fine-grained, and locally fissile. Due to the
structural complexity and deeply to completely weathered nature of this shale, no
structural data was obtained during mapping.

        A Conasauga limestone unit occurs at and east of the drill site and is in
stratigraphic contact with the Conasauga shale. This unit is characterized by relatively
fresh, coarsely crystalline, very pure, limestone and dolomitic limestone that is exposed
along the banks and in the stream bed of Two Run Creek and associated tributaries, as
well as in cuts along the railroad south of the drill site.

        The Knox Group underlies the site west of the Conasauga shale units. Due to
physical and chemical weathering, the Knox typically weathers deeply and
heterogeneously, leaving a variably thick horizon of residual soil and locally, a
transitionally weathered zone between the residual soils and fresh bedrock.                              No
exposures of the Knox Group rocks were found in the immediate vicinity of the drill site.

Structural Interpretation Based on Borehole Stratigraphy

         The structural interpretations by Thomas and Bayona (2005) and Whisner (2010),
presented in the Regional Structural Geology section above, appear to be based on a
seismic section that was collected in 1981 along Highway 411/GA-20 from I-75 near
Cartersville, Georgia to Centre, Alabama. Although the seismic section does not transect
the site, the part of the section near the site runs nearly east-west along Highway 411/GA-
20 approximately 1.25 miles south of the site. Given the near proximity to the site, the
depth to basement shown in the two cross sections should be relatively accurate.
However, the lower Cambrian stratigraphy typically does not show up well in seismic
sections, especially the sections using older seismic data where the profiles were
collected using thumper trucks. Additionally, the rheologically weaker layers (i.e., shale)
are highly strained and deformed and do not show up well on the seismic profiles. In
contrast, the rheologically stiffer layers (i.e., Knox Group) show up very well on seismic
sections.

         Based on the stratigraphic interpretation of the mud logs and regional stratigraphy,
the Conasauga Group lithologies that were encountered in the upper part of the test
boring (40 to 3610 ft. BGS) are considered to be in a normal stratigraphic contact with the
underlaying Rome Formation.                   The boundary between the Rome Formation and

Conasauga Group was identified at approximately 3610 ft.BGS (1100 m) based on the
first occurrence of red beds. However, given the complexity of the Formation Microimager
(FMI) logs, this normal stratigraphic contact appears to have been subjected to bedding-

different lithologies.

        Shale units that were encountered above 3610 ft.BGS (1100 m) appear to be
highly contorted. Given that these units are interbedded with thick carbonate units, the
rheologic contrast between the shale- and carbonate-dominated units within the
Conasauga Group would cause the less competent shale units to be highly contorted and
deformed.

        Within the Rome Formation, between 3610 and 4740 ft. BGS, there appear to be
several highly deformed zones. Given that the Rome Formation is very shaley, there are
probably several bedding-parallel faults similar to the imbricates seen within the Rome
Formation near Rome, Georgia. Surface geologic mapping west of the drill site shows
that these bedding-parallel faults have dissected and structurally thickened and thinned
the Rome Formation. Historically, faults in the area that carry Rome Formation lithologies
in the hanging wall are associated with the Coosa Fault system.

        With the contrast is lithologies between the base on the Rome Formation and the
Conasauga Group lithologies encountered between 4740 and 6030 ft. BGS (1840 m), the
Rome/Conasauga observed at 4740 ft, BGS is also probably highly sheared. Because
of the juxtaposition of the stratigraphy, this contact is interpreted as a major thrust fault,
emplacing Rome Formation over the Conasauga Group.

Discussion

        As described above, the Cassville site test boring encountered relatively high
porosity/high permeability lithologies within the Rome Formation which is considered to
be a favorable host rock. ARI has expressed concern regarding the potential for the
injected gas to escape to the surface where the target host rock crops out. Based on
review of published and unpublished geologic mapping, the Rome Formation is shown to
occur at depth between the Great Smoky Fault which is located approximately 9 miles

March, 2022                                      62

                                                                                            STF-ASR-2-4 Attachment

                                                Cassville #1 Stratigraphic Test Borehole, Bartow County, GA


(14.5 km) to the east and the Coosa Fault which is located approximately 16 miles (26
km) west of the test boring, Figure 30.

        Based on detailed geologic mapping by Kath et al., (2015) of the Cartersville
Mining district, the Rome Formation has been highly imbricated along the Cassville, White
and Cloverleaf Faults. Any sequestered carbon injected in the subsurface at the Cassville
test site would be trapped in the Rome Formation, east of the site, due to stratigraphic
termination of the Rome Formation along the Cassville Fault.

        To the west, the Rome Formation is interpreted to crop out along the trace of the
Coosa Fault and a smaller fault splay associated with the Coosa Fault. The formation is
interpreted to be semi-planar between the test boring and the Coosa Fault, as shown on
Figure 30; however, there is a high probability that the Rome Formation is dismembered
along several possible blind thrusts which are represented at the surface as small
asymmetric anticlines bringing the Conasauga lithologies to the surface between
Cassville and Rome, GA.

        Based on our interpretation of the regional geology, there are several imbricate
thrust faults between the Cassville test boring and the area of outcrop near the Coosa
Fault, disrupting continuity of any bedding planes within the formation.                       This, in
combination with the significant distance between the test boring and where the host rock
is exposed (~16 miles), suggests that it will be highly unlikely for gas to migrate from the
test boring location and be released at ground surface near the Coosa Fault.

Assessment
        Despite the operational challenges encountered while drilling, the results of routine
core analysis and log analysis prove encouraging for further investigation of the CO2
storage potential of northwest Georgia Valley and Ridge province. Specifically, the upper
Rome Formation, which may form a duplex beneath the Conasauga Group encountered,
demonstrated preferential petrophysical characteristics for CO2 storage.

        Log results record a thick succession of Cambrian-Ordovician carbonate and
siliciclastic strata, including the Rome Formation, Conasauga Group, and the basal part

                                                                                                STF-ASR-2-4 Attachment

of the Knox Group (Copper Ridge Dolomite), in ascending order. Shear zones containing
complex networks of fractures are distributed through much of the wellbore and beds dips
are steep, as shown by the FMI log interpretatin. The core analysis summary
demonstrated that porosities higher than 10.0% are present in dolomites in the upper
Rome Formation (3,713-3,798 ft) and the associated Klinkenberg permeabilities range
from 54.1-135 mD. This apparently porous and permeable zone warrants further
investigation as a storage interval. The upper Rome section is classified by x-ray
diffraction analysis as dolomite and siliceous dolomite, with some evaporitic strata
identified to contain gypsum and anhydrite. The permeability values from core analysis
indicates a moderately connected to well-connected pore system which is composed of
vuggy, fracture, and intercrystalline porosities.

        Structural analysis indicates that the area within and around Cassville is primarily
underlain by rocks of the middle to upper Cambrian Conasauga Group and Cambro-
Ordovician Knox Group. The site is located within the Valley and Ridge physiographic
province, which is characterized by long, narrow series of northeast/southwest-trending
ridges separated by wide parallel valleys. Structural interpretations of the region are
variable, Croft (1963) shows the location of the Cassvile site to be near the axis of an
overturned anticline with an overturned syncline immediately east of the site while
Cressler (1970) and Crawford (2009) show the site to be near the western end of a normal
stratigraphic inlier and/or a westward plunging anticline. The Rome Formation occurs at
depth between the Great Smoky Fault which is approximately 9 miles to the east and the
Coosa Fault which is located approximately 16 miles west of the test boring. Stratigraphic
termination of the Rome Formation along the Cassville Fault east of the site would
potentially trap injected CO2. To the west, the formation is interpreted as semi-planar but
there is a high probability it is dismembered along several possible blind thrusts which
are represented at surface as small asymmetric anticlines. Based on the interpretation by
Petrologic Solutions, there are several imbricated thrust faults between the Cassville test
boring site and the area of outcrop near the Coosa fault. The stratigraphic interpretation
coupled with the extensive regional distance (~16 miles) suggests a low probability of
injected CO2 to migrate to surface.

        The upper Rome Formation contains high regional porosity and permeability,
which suggest it may be favorable for the permanent geologic storage of CO2. The strata
overlying the Rome Formation, the Conasauga Group, contains shale likely with low
porosity and permeability, which could prevent the migration of CO2 into overlying
formations and into underground sources of drinking water. The structural complexity
provides apparent containment to the east by stratigraphic trap, and to the west by lateral
distance and imbricated thrust faults. Further structural investigation is recommended to
confirm lateral continuity and confinement above the upper Rome Formation. This would
require the acquisition and interpretation of several 2D seismic lines. Additionally, more
testing is recommended to confirm the injectivity of the discussed potential target of the
Rome Formation. Despite the regional geologic complexity of the Valley and Ridge
province, the log and core analysis show promising results for the development of a
potential CO2 storage site.

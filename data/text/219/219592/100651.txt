INTRODUCTION

        The testimony and evidence presented to the Georgia Public Service Commission

(“Commission”) over the course of these proceedings demonstrates that the proposed

combustion turbine (“CT”) units 8, 9, and 10 at Plant Yates should not be certified. Instead, the

Commission should:

     1) Defer certification until after the results of the ongoing capacity request for proposal

        process are available, so that the Commission can ascertain whether capacity resources

        that are lower cost, less risky, and more timely available than the Yates CTs can be

        procured to meet the needs of Georgia Power’s customers;

     2) If the Commission ultimately does certify the Yates CTs, it should implement sufficient

        guardrails to prevent fuel costs from being entirely passed on to customers.

ARGUMENT

A.      The Commission Should Defer Certification of the Proposed Yates CT Units

        As demonstrated by the testimony of Albert Lin, the Commission should defer

certification of the proposed Yates CT units. As Georgia Power concedes, the units will not be

available until well after the winter 2026/2027 capacity shortfall the Company projects;

moreover, Georgia Power has underway right now a Request for Proposal (“RFP”) process to

acquire additional capacity, specifically including bids for resources available more than a year

before Yates 9 and 10 and as much as 11 months before Yates 8, would come online. In face of

this, the Company’s demand that this Commission suspend the normal procedures for capacity

resource certification in favor of the Yates CTs is unsupported.

       All-source competitive solicitation processes are beneficial ways of ensuring that “all

possible options to serve anticipated load growth have been adequately examined and analyzed,”

SC-SACE Ex. 2, Direct Testimony and Exhibits of Albert Lin 5:13-14 (hereinafter “Lin Direct”),

see also Lin Direct Exhibit AL-2, and are in fact the default process for acquiring capacity

resources. See Commission Rule 151-3-4-.04(3)(b). Georgia Power is currently undertaking an

all-source RFP process (indeed, bids in that process were finalized before the July 24, 2024

hearing in this matter), and that process specifically contemplates resources available as early as

January 1, 2026:

       2.1.1. GPC seeks offers to sell capacity and energy from Capacity Resources that
       can commence delivery in the Winter 2029 – Winter 2031 timeframe (December
       2028 – December 2030). Participant may propose a Capacity Resource that is
       available earlier than Winter 2029, but no earlier than January 1, 2026, and
       GPC will determine if the proposed option is an economical option that meets
       customer needs.

Georgia Power Company, All-Source Capacity Request for Proposals for 2029-2031, at 20

(emphasis added). By contrast, the Yates CT units’ commercial operation dates are not until

December 1, 2026, May 1, 2027, and August 1, 2027 for Yates Units 8, 9, and 10, respectively.

Direct Testimony of Jeffrey R. Grubb and Michael A. Bush (May 21, 2024) 7:18-21 (hereinafter


                                                                                                      2

“Company Direct”). As such, the RFP process could identify resources that are available

between 11 and 19 months before the Yates CT units come online.

       This time period matters: the Company perceives a capacity need starting much earlier

than the Yates CTs would be available to meet that need, and as such, the Yates units will not be

able to fulfill the purpose for which Georgia Power claims they are necessary. As Georgia

Power testified, the “1,300 megawatts” of Yates Unites 8-10 “is required as early as the winter of

2026/2027.”1 Company Direct 4:21-22. Resources coming online in the spring and summer of

2027 are obviously not available to meet a need starting in the winter of the preceding year.

       Moreover, the Yates CT units are unlikely to actually provide the promised 1,300

megawatts of capacity even if they do reach commercial operation. No gas supply (firm, or

otherwise) has been secured for the CTs, meaning that they will compete with other Georgia

Power assets—including more efficient combined cycle units—for gas in times of high capacity

needs. When there isn’t gas for the CTs,2 Georgia Power intends to run them on diesel oil

instead, which reduces their generation capacity by nearly a fifth. See Company Direct. 6:2-7

(noting that switching from gas to diesel lowers the capacity of each unit from 441 megawatts to

just 357).

       Accordingly, there is an opportunity before this Commission to defer certification of the

have already received bids for resources less costly and/or available sooner than the Yates CTs,

B.     The Commission should not allow full pass-through of fuel costs for the Yates CTs

       As Albert Lin demonstrated in his testimony, the policy of passing through all fuel costs

to ratepayers presents definite and increasing customer cost risks as well as a moral hazard

condition. This is particularly true where Georgia Power intends to operate the Yates CTs on far

less efficient fuel oil at possibly higher price per unit of energy for any significant amount of the

total time they would be utilized as capacity resources. Moreover, while the 2023 IRP Update

Stipulation seeks to shield ratepayers from some of the risks associated with the construction of

the Yates CTs, it does nothing to protect ratepayers from the cost risks associated with the

operation of the units, which Staff conceded at hearing were the most significant costs.

Accordingly, if the Commission certifies the Yates CT units, it should disallow full pass-through

of the associated fuel costs.

       1.      Passing through all fuel costs presents significant moral hazard

       Automatic pass-throughs of fuel prices are a relic of an earlier age when nearly all

generation sources had fuel costs; particularly today, where gas represents an increasingly


                                                                                                    4

volatile and disproportionate share of the utility space’s costs, such total pass-throughs increase

the size and frequency of costs impacting only the customer.

       As Albert Lin testified, fuel cost recovery mechanisms that place all of the risk on

ratepayers are a product of the 1970s, but the “generation, management, and conservation of

electricity has changed and evolved materially since 1979.” Lin Direct 6:13-14. Indeed, whereas

half a century ago “virtually all technologies to generate electricity required some type of fuel,”

that is not the case today, where “various forms of technology” such as solar and wind, “require

little or no fuel.” Lin Direct 6:16-18. As a result, some technologies—such as natural gas plants

in general and the proposed Yates CTs in particular—benefit from fuel cost pass-throughs, while

other technologies do not; such “asymmetries run the risk of producing poor economic and

commercial choices where the cost and risk of major investments will not be evaluated

correctly.” 8:10-13. Specifically, such policies invite moral hazard in situations like the present

Yates CT units certification request:

       [M]oral hazard is mostly focused on the risk to customers for fuel costs to operate
       the Yates generation assets once constructed. With a [fuel cost recovery policy]
       that shifts the largest operating cost item to another party, the utility may be
       increasing the financial cost to the electricity system’s customers whether
       intentionally or unintentionally . . . thereby leaving the customers to absorb the
       price volatility and total fuel cost.

Lin Direct 10:14-20.

       This problem is exacerbated by the increasingly volatile gas market and increasing

overreliance on gas that Georgia Power seeks. As Mr. Lin testified, natural gas is the top fuel by

a wide margin for generating electricity. This concentration of natural gas used by electricity

generators has made the electricity industry the biggest customer for natural gas, up from an

insignificant party just 15 years ago. Further, the highly cyclical nature of electricity load is

CONCLUSION

         For the foregoing reasons, the Commission should defer certification of the Yates CT

units, at least until the results of the RFP can be evaluated to determine whether or not there are

less risky and lower cost resources available to meet the Company’s asserted capacity needs. In

the alternative, if the Yates CT units are certified, the Commission should disallow cost recovery

for fuel costs associated with the Yates CT units if those costs exceed the “moderate view” gas

prices that the Company relied on in its 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update Resource Mix

Study.

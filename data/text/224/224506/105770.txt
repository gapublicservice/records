A.Figure 1 shows Staff’s estimate of Georgia Power’s annual incremental revenue requirement build up through 2034.

Figure 1: Revenue Requirement of Requested Resources

Q.ARE THERE ADDITIONAL FINANCIAL IMPACTS THE COMMISSION SHOULD CONSIDER WHEN DECIDING HOW MUCH CAPACITY TO CERTIFY IN THIS PROCEEDING?
A.Yes. The Company’s capital expenditure plan for 2025 – 2027 will nearly double its current rate base. The Company will also make significant capital expenditures in 2028 – 2031 further increasing rate base. The increase in the Company’s prospective rate base for the 2028 rate case will increase annual revenue requirements by billions of dollars. The Commission can mitigate this upward pressure on revenue requirement and customers’ bills by limiting the approval of new resources to only those necessary to serve large-load customers that have executed contracts under new rules and regulations.

Other Recommendations
10. Staff recommends the Commission grant an Additional Sum amount of 2.30/kW- yr for PPA projects, and that it only be applied to the winter reliable capacity value of the PPAs, adjusted by the resource’s ELCC rating and/or the resource’s available capacity constrained by transmission limits.  

11. Staff recommends that the Commission limit the recovery of capital cost including AFUDC and ad valorem, to the estimates provided by the Company in this proceeding to ensure the projects are efficiently managed and ratepayers are protected.  

12.Staff recommends rejecting the Company’s “portfolio” based contingency proposal. Under the Company’s proposal, any savings on an individual project would be used to offset cost overruns on another project. Staff recommends rejecting the Company’s proposal as savings from completing an individual project under the certified amount should accrue to ratepayers’ benefit, not the Company’s benefit. In other words, savings from one project should not be used to eliminate examination and review of cost overruns from another project. In addition, Staff has been advised by legal counsel that the Company’s portfolio approach is not consistent with Georgia law.  

13.Staff recommends the Company be required to comply with additional reporting and construction monitoring requirements. Staff recommends monitoring requirements for the COP thermal projects be consistent with what was required for the recent Yates CT project, and the reports the Company files should include details regarding the status of third party firm transportation (“FT”) of natural gas to insure the Company’s natural gas resources can be dispatched to meet system peak demands reliably and economically. Staff recommends monitoring requirements for the BESS projects be consistent with what was required for the initial BESS projects. 

14.Staff recommends the Commission reject the Company’s request to automatically place any pre-construction costs incurred that are not useful or transferable to other potential projects in a regulatory asset. However, if the Commission considers allowing regulatory asset treatment, Staff recommends the Commission defer such decision and require the Company to make a separate filing, which would allow Staff the opportunity to review the costs and circumstances.

RESOURCE NEED AND RFP TIMELINE
Q.HOW DID THE COMPANY’S LOAD FORECAST AND CAPACITY NEED CHANGE BETWEEN THE 2022 IRP, 2023 IRP UPDATE, AND 2025 IRP?
A.The 2022 IRP was filed on January 31, 2022, and at that time, the Company did not identify any significant amount of data center load growth that would be added to the system in future years. One year later, the Company’s load forecast changed dramatically based on the Company’s recognition of a surge in the number of large-load data centers that considered locating in Georgia. As a result, the Company filed the 2023 IRP Update on October 27, 2023 to address its increased capacity needs. 
In the 2023 IRP Update, the Commission certified new capacity to address new large load customers. This new capacity consisted of the 750 MW Mississippi Power Company (“MPC”) PPA for the term of January 1, 2024, through December 31, 2028, and the 230 MW Santa Rosa Energy Center PPA for the term that began right after certification through December 31, 2028. Also connected with the 2023 IRP Update, the Commission certified 1,300 MWs of Yates Units 8-10 CT capacity on August 29, 2024, and 765 MWs of BESS capacity associated with the Robins, Moody, Hammond, and McGrau Ford Phase I and II BESS projects on December 12, 2024. In addition to this capacity, a separate 500 MW renewable RFP was approved in the 2022 IRP proceeding, and is current being conducted for resources that would be available as early as 2028. The Company also identified 136 MW of winter capacity through the 2023 CARES RFP and 180 MW through the 2027-2029 BESS RFP. Finally, from the 2025 IRP, the Commission approved postponement of retirements at Scherer 3 and the Gaston Plant, which added approximately 928 MW through 2034, it approved the Company’s plans to perform uprates at Plant McIntosh and Plant Vogtle, and it approved the Company’s request to certify capacity from Plant Scherer Unit 3, which had previously been wholesale capacity. Altogether, the Commission has certified the addition of approximately 4,080 MW of winter capacity by 2031.
The Company’s load forecast increased even more when the 2025 IRP was filed on January 31, 2025, and it was revised further in a February 2025 load forecast update.  The 2025 IRP Settlement Order stated that in the 2029-2031 AS RFP the Company and Staff would be allowed to update the load forecast methodologies as they each determine to be appropriate, and it stated Staff could “…. propose that the Commission modify the methodology to include the materialization of executed Contracts for Electric Service in determining the Load Forecast.”  The Stipulation also provided that the 2029-2031 AS RFP, which was approved in the 2022 IRP, should identify between 6,000 - 8,500 MW of resources. Paragraph 2c of the Stipulation stated:
The Company shall be authorized to seek certification of up to 8,500 MW of capacity from the 2029-2031 All Source Capacity RFP. The Company agrees to provide generation procurement options to meet generation needs of at least 6,000 MW and up to 8,500 MW necessary to meet Georgia Power system demand. Upon consideration of the Company's updated October 2025 Load Forecast, as well as consideration of any Staff-proposed Load Forecast, the Commission can then appropriately determine necessary generation resources to certify as part of the 2029-2031 All-Source Capacity RFP certification proceeding.
While Paragraph 2c mentioned the range of capacity the Company could seek through the RFP (6,000 to 8,500 MW), a determination of the amount of resource capacity to certify requires consideration of other factors. Paragraph 2c notes that the Commission should take into consideration both the Company’s updated October 2025 Load Forecast, as well as Staff’s Load Forecast in deciding about the resources to certify. Staff’s preferred load forecast is Scenario 1, which only includes signed large-load contracts in the forecast.

Q.BEFORE INCLUDING NEW RESOURCES, WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S CAPACITY NEED BASED ON THE COMPANY’S LATEST LOAD FORECAST (B2026)?
A.The Company’s forecast indicates there is a capacity need of approximately 8,865 MW by the Winter of 2030/2031 and 7,790 MW of resources by the Summer of 2031, assuming the Company’s B2026 load forecast, reserve margin assumptions, and starting capacity position. 
Q.DOES STAFF HAVE ANY CONCERNS REGARDING THE COMPANY’S CAPACITY NEED ASSUMPTIONS USED IN THIS PROCEEDING?
A.Yes. Staff has significant concerns with the Company’s large load materialization assumptions, and the Company’s TRM assumption. Staff also has concerns with the Company’s Tenaska Transmission availability assumption, the Company’s plans for future RFP procurements, and the Company’s desire to acquire capacity (9,886 MW), which is above the currently estimated 2031 capacity need and is above the upper bound (8,500 MW) amount of capacity that was approved to be identified in the 2029-2031 AS RFP.
Q.HAS STAFF DEVELOPED ITS OWN LOAD FORECAST, TARGET RESERVE MARGIN, AND RESOURCE PLANS?
A.Yes. Staff’s load forecast panel developed two load forecast scenarios that our panel used in our modeling analyses. Also, in the 2025 IRP, Staff reviewed Georgia Power’s Reserve Margin Study and identified issues that led to our recommendation for a lower TRM, 24.5% compared to the Company’s 26%. Staff ’s TRM is more reasonable as it took into consideration other factors such as data center insensitivity to cold weather conditions. In our modeling, we used Staff’s load forecasts, Staff’s TRM, and other changes to assumptions described below to develop alternative resource acquisition recommendations.

Q.HAVE UTILITIES IN OTHER STATES ATTEMPTED TO BALANCE THE RISK OF LOAD MATERIALIZATION WITH THE RISK OF CUSTOMERS HAVING TO PAY FOR EXCESS CAPACITY COSTS?
A.Yes. Other utilities have had to deal with similar situations, and their regulatory authorities have recognized that their existing customers may be at risk if the large loads do not materialize. In those jurisdictions, the regulators have imposed large-load materialization monitoring requirements and have aligned utility new resource investment and cost recovery with the materialization of load contracts. For example, a new American Electric Power (“AEP”) Ohio data center tariff has been approved that will require large load contract commitments before new transmission investments are made. Kentucky Utilities / Louisville Gas and Electric (“KU/LGE”) entered into a stipulation in its latest certification case that would require executed electric service agreements as a cost-recovery condition for a proposed CC resource. Dominion Energy South Carolina, Inc. stated that it only relies on contracts in developing its reference planning forecasts. These examples demonstrate that tying new infrastructure investment to new load materialization is a reasonable approach to planning for large loads and limits risks to existing customers.
Q.THE COMPANY HAS REQUESTED APPROVAL TO DEFER CERTAIN DEVELOPMENT COSTS FOR RESOURCES NOT CERTIFIED INTO A REGULATORY ASSET. WHAT IS STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION?
A.The Company has indicated it will seek recovery of development costs, including financing costs, for resources not certified, by deferring those costs to a regulatory asset for recovery in a future base rate case. Staff’s position is the Company should be treated the same as other bidders in the RFP who also incur development costs. Those bidders are not allowed to recover their development costs from unsuccessful bids and neither should the Company. It should be noted this massive expansion of Georgia Power’s system will increase the Company’s profits tremendously and should be considered when decisions about the recovery of stranded pre-construction costs are made.

Q.DID STAFF IDENTIFY ANY CONCERNS WITH THE COMPANY’S RANKING ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY?
A.Yes, Staff identified four concerns, which include: 
(1) Equalization Methodology – Since the Company performed independent analyses of the RFP resources, it decided to apply adjustments to “equalize” the term length and capacity size of the resource bids. Staff’s Aurora analysis addressed equalization impacts by accounting for resource lives and resource sizes in the optimal selection process. 
(2) ELCC Tranche Evaluation - The Company’s evaluation assumed a tranche approach to modeling ELCC values for different BESS resources. The problem with the Company’s approach is that some projects that were phased in had a higher ELCC value for the second phase of the project compared to the first phase. In Staff’s analysis, the two phases had equivalent ELCC values.   
(3) Omission of Additional Sum on PPAs - The Company’s ranking analysis did not account for Additional Sum costs GPC customers will have to pay when PPA resources are selected. Staff accounted for these costs in its resource selection process. 
(4) Portfolio Approach and Timing of Resource Need - The Company’s ranking analysis evaluated resources one at a time and did not evaluate resources based on a comprehensive portfolio approach. The problem with the Company’s approach is that resources that were selected earlier in the study period did not influence the dispatch of resources selected later. Staff’s Aurora analysis addressed this problem by considering the relative economics of the projects together.

OTHER RISKS AND COST CONSIDERATIONS
Additional Sum
Q.WHAT IS THE BASIS FOR THE COMPANY’S REQUEST FOR A $3/KW-YEAR ADDITIONAL SUM FOR ALL PPAS? 
A.The O.C.G.A. § 46-3A-8 provides for the Company to receive an Additional Sum for long-term power purchases, and requires lost revenues, changed risks, and an equitable sharing of benefits between the utility and its retail customers to be considered in establishing the Additional Sum amount. The sole reason the Company’s testimony provided for selecting $3/kW-year as the Additional Sum was that it had been approved for certain other PPAs that were previously certified.       
Q.DOES STAFF AGREE THAT $3/KW-YEAR IS THE VALUE THAT SHOULD BE USED AS THE ADDITIONAL SUM FOR ALL PPAS TO BE CERTIFIED?
A.No. In light of the significant increase in revenue requirements that customers will be asked to pay for all of the new resources, Staff does not believe that $3/kW-year represents an equitable sharing of benefits between customers and the Company, and instead Staff recommends that $2.30/kW-year be used for all PPAs. 
Q.WHY DOES STAFF BELIEVE IT IS FAIR TO USE $2.30/KW AS THE ADDITIONAL SUM VALUE?
The Company selected $3/kW-year as the Additional Sum value because certain prior PPAs used that value. As it turns out, several of the PPAs that the Company is seeking certification in this proceeding, including Tenaska Heard, Dahlberg 4, and Harris 1, were previously certified as PPAs in prior proceedings, and for those PPAs the Company was approved to charge customers an Additional Sum value of $2.30/kW-year. In addition, the Company’s response to STF-PIA-3-13 indicates that out of 18 PPA contracts that are currently in existence and that have a firm Additional Sum value, 56% of them have an Additional Sum value of $2.30, which again is lower than the $3/kW-year the Company is requesting in this proceeding.
Q.WHAT OTHER RECOMMENDATIONS DOES STAFF HAVE REGARDING THE ADDITIONAL SUM CUSTOMER CHARGE?
A.As a result of Staff’s ranking analysis, discussed above, Staff recommends that the MPC PPA be rejected. However, should the Commission ultimately decide to accept the MPC PPA, Staff recommends that customers should not be charged an Additional Sum for that resource. The reason is that the O.C.G.A. § 46-3A-8 provides for the Company to receive an Additional Sum for “long-term power purchases,” and the MPC PPA is a one-year transaction, which makes it a short-term power purchase. Therefore, if the Commission were to certify the MPC PPA, customers should not be charged an Additional Sum for that PPA. 
Q.DOES STAFF HAVE AN ADDITIONAL SUM RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE NEER BESS SUPPLEMENTAL RESOURCES?
A.Yes. As a result of Staff’s ranking analysis, Staff recommended the NEER BESS resources be rejected. However, should the Commission ultimately decide to accept any of the NEER BESS resources, Staff recommends that customers should only be required to pay a $2.30/ kw-year Additional Sum charge based on the reliable capacity associated with those resources. Staff’s reason is that BESS resources are typically discounted in the Company’s load and resource balance tables because they can only be dispatched for four hours at a time, or because the amount of capacity they can deliver is limited based on an interconnection limit.  As a result, some BESS resources are unable to fully provide the same reliable capacity value as other resources. As an example, a 100 MW BESS resource may only provide 80% capacity value or 80 MW, so the Additional Sum should be calculated based on 80 MW rather than 100 MW. For this reason, Staff recommends that for any NEER BESS PPA resource the Commission certifies, the Additional Sum amount for those PPAs should be derived based on the ELCC adjusted capacity value.

Q.IS STAFF CONCERNED ABOUT THE COSTS GEORGIA POWER PROPOSED FOR THE COP CC UNITS, WHICH WERE THE ONLY CC RESOURCES BID IN THE RFP?  
A.Yes. While Staff understands that the CC units were selected based on a competitive solicitation process, it is still informative to compare the CC costs to other similar CC projects. For example, KU/LGE recently requested certification two build two new 1x1 CCs (Brown 12 and Mill Creek 6) for approximately $1.4 billion each, including a 10% contingency. Also, Oglethorpe Power Company has announced its intention to build two 1x1 CCs in Monroe County for more than $3 billion. Staff understands that there may be differences between projects in what is included in the reported costs; however, the large difference in these costs raises a concern that Georgia Power’s CC project costs may be relatively high.
Q.ARE THERE REASONS THAT COST OVERRUNS AND/OR DELAYS COULD OCCUR BEYOND THE IDENTIFIED CERTIFIED COST?  
A.Yes, Georgia Power’s has had an extensive history of delays and cost overruns on generation projects and given the magnitude of the Company’s proposed build program, and the current highly inflationary environment for generating equipment and labor costs, Staff is concerned that there is a significant chance that delays and cost overruns could occur at these projects. Limiting the amount of new construction provides an opportunity for the Company to more closely focus on these projects and prevent cost overruns. 
As many utilities in the U.S. are currently pursuing new gas-fired resources, labor and capital costs for new combustion turbines could continue to increase. In addition to the KU/LGE and Oglethorpe CC projects mentioned above, other CC projects in the Southeast include three 1x1 CCs that Entergy plans to serve new data center load, three 1x1 CCs that DESC and Santee Cooper have proposed in South Carolina, a 2x1 CC Duke Energy Progress plans to construct in Person County, North Carolina, and a 2x1 CC Duke Energy Carolinas plans to construct in Anderson County, South Carolina. 
Given the risks of delays and potential for cost overruns, and the fact that the Company has already embedded contingencies in its cost estimates, Staff recommends the Company’s RFP and Supplemental projects should be held to the same standard for cost overruns that third party bids are held to, and should be required to follow the same monitoring requirements that were approved for the Plant Yates CTs in the Commission Order in Docket No. 55378, signed August 29, 2024.

BESS Resources
Q.DOES STAFF HAVE ANY CONCERNS ABOUT THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED BESS RESOURCE PROJECTS? 
A.Yes. Staff is concerned that while the Company is seeking certification for nine RFP BESS projects, it has not yet entered into System Sale and Purchase Agreements (“SSPA”) and Long-Term Commitment Agreements (“LTCA”) for four of the projects, including the Bowen Phase II, Thomson, Hammond Phase II, and McIntosh BESS projects. Despite having been selected in the RFP process based on costs the Company bid, the four projects are just in the early stages of development and there could be significant risks that affect the costs customers will have to pay for the projects. 
Q.DOES STAFF HAVE ANY CONCERN ABOUT THE BESS TRANSMISSION INTERCONNECTIONS?
A. Yes. The Company has indicated it is “…. currently performing outage coordination studies to determine the feasibility of implementing all interconnection and delivery projects required to meet the commercial operating dates of all of the bids in the All-Source RFP.”  If the resources cannot be interconnected on a timely basis, or the BESS projects encounter higher interconnection costs than expected, then this is another risk that could affect customer bills.

Q.IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING TO MANAGE CONTINGENCY COSTS ON A PORTFOLIO BASIS FOR THE PROPOSED RESOURCES IN THIS CASE?
A. Yes.  The Company explained its preference for managing contingency at the portfolio level rather than by each individual project in discovery response STF-PIA-10-6. The Company explained that in managing contingency at the portfolio level, “the Company tracks all spending at the project level, but all projects collectively are accountable to the portfolio certified amount.” 
Q.WHAT IS STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE COMPANY’S “PORTFOLIO CONTINGENCY” PROPOSAL?
A. Staff recommends rejecting the Company’s “portfolio” based contingency proposal. The savings from completing an individual project under the certified amount should accrue to the ratepayers benefit and not to the Company’s benefit. Under the Company’s proposal, any savings on an individual project would be used to offset cost overruns on another project. Savings from a project should not be used to eliminate examination and review of cost overruns on another project. Staff recommends the Company be held to the certified cost estimates on an individual project basis.
High Load Factor Load Impacts
Q.COULD HIGH LOAD FACTOR CUSTOMERS CAUSE AN INCREASE IN AVERAGE FUEL COSTS?
A. Yes. Georgia Power and the Southern Company System’s base load capacity needs are expected to increase with the addition of the high load factor load.  Figure 3 provides a historical view and a projection of Georgia Power and the Southern Company’s load and load factor, which shows that both will increase significantly over time.  
Figure 3: Historic vs. Project Load Factor 


Higher load factors may result in less fuel-efficient generators such as CTs running more and increasing fuel costs for all customers.  There may also be a shift in the energy needs of the system as new high load factor loads are added, and the impacts should be investigated in future Fuel Cost Recovery (“FCR”) proceedings.
Q.IS IT POSSIBLE THAT HIGH LOAD FACTOR CUSTOMERS COULD REDUCE IMPACTS ON FUEL COSTS AND CAPACITY INVESTMENTS THROUGH PARTICIPATION IN FLEXIBLE LOAD PROGRAMS?
A.  Yes. The Company’s requests in this case did not consider the possibility of large load customers participating in flexible load, DSM, curtailment programs, or behind the meter generation programs.  If the anticipated high load factor data center loads were to agree to be curtailable or be considered non-firm for planning purposes, these customers could likely be added to the system more quickly, reduce fixed costs and capacity investment, and help to reduce high energy costs in peak hours. The Company indicated there may be opportunities for these customers to participate in flexible load programs, and the Company stated it is optimistic that its discussions with large load customers may lead to additional program participation.

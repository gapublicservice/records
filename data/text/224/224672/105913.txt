I.INTRODUCTION
Q.PLEASE STATE YOUR NAMES, TITLES, AND BUSINESS ADDRESSES.
A.My name is Michael A. Bush. I am a Director of Generation Planning and Development for Southern Company Services, Inc. (“SCS”). I primarily oversee the planning and development of storage, renewable, and distributed energy resources. My business address is 600 North 18th Street, Birmingham, Alabama 35203.

Q.DID THIS PANEL PREVIOUSLY PRESENT DIRECT TESTIMONY IN THESE PROCEEDINGS?
A.Yes.
Q.WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF THE PANEL’S REBUTTAL TESTIMONY?
A.The purpose of our testimony is to respond to the testimony of the Georgia Public Service Commission (“Commission”) Public Interest Advocacy Staff (“Staff”) and various Intervenors filed in Docket Nos. 56298 and 56310. Our testimony focuses on several key issues without attempting to address every issue or recommendation raised in Staff or Intervenor testimony. However, the fact that Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) may not respond to a particular position of Staff or an Intervenor should not be considered as acceptance of such position.
Q.HOW IS YOUR TESTIMONY ORGANIZED? 
A.Following this introduction, the remainder of our testimony is organized as follows: 
Section II addresses Georgia Power’s ability to construct and manage self-built resources;
Section III addresses certification of resources using a portfolio approach; 
Section IV discusses Staff’s recommended adjustments to the proposed Company-Owned Proposals (“COPs”) that differ from what was submitted in the All-Source Request for Proposals (“RFP”); and 
Section V responds to criticism about resource costs.
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE TESTIMONY OF THE PANEL.
A.Our rebuttal testimony addresses Georgia Power’s responses to certain Staff and Intervenor critiques and recommendations regarding: the Company’s ability to construct generation resources on time and within the expected budget; Georgia Power’s proposed certification of resources as a portfolio; and the reasonableness and comparability of the proposed COP thermal units. 
Contrary to Staff’s allegations, the Company has high confidence in its ability to construct and manage the portfolios of COP Battery Energy Storage Systems (“BESS”), BESS + Solar, and thermal resources proposed for certification. SCS has had significant recent success in building Plant Barry Unit 8 for Alabama Power, which was completed on schedule and below budget. In addition, the Company is leveraging that experience and applying it for Plant Yates Units 8-10 as well as for the 765 MW portfolio of BESS resources certified by the Commission last fall. The Plant Yates combustion turbines (“CTs”), BESS resources near the Robins and Moody Air Force Bases, Plant Hammond BESS, and McGrau Ford Phase I and II BESS are all projected to be completed below budget and ahead of schedule.
In addition, the Company’s request to certify the COP resources in two portfolios is consistent with how Plant Yates, the 765 MW BESS portfolio, and each of the military self-build solar portfolios were certified. Certifying resources as a portfolio offers cost savings and efficiency opportunities, mirrors how the Company manages construction in practice, and permits the Company to implement lessons learned in real time. Contrary to Staff’s allegation, nothing about the proposed portfolio approach limits the Commission or its Staff’s ability to evaluate, audit, or oversee the construction of each resource on an individual basis and measure its progress against expectations.
Finally, other than the Wadley project, all the COPs submitted for certification were selected through the All-Source RFP process as competitive resources to meet customer needs. The costs for the COP thermal units were developed through a competitive inquiry and cost estimation process by subject matter experts. Although direct cost comparisons are challenging due to project specific differences, the costs for the COP thermal projects are in line with costs for combined cycles (“CCs”) recently reported elsewhere in the country. Intervenors challenging the reasonableness of our assumed costs rely on incorrect or outdated data sets. The Company stands behind the value to customers and its ability to timely complete each COP resource requested for certification.
In summary, the Commission should approve the Company’s request as filed.
II.ABILITY TO CONSTRUCT
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF’S ALLEGATION THAT THE COMPANY ONLY HAS A HISTORY OF CONSTRUCTING RESOURCES THAT ARE OVER BUDGET AND BEHIND SCHEDULE.
A.We do not agree with Staff’s position as we have high confidence regarding the Company’s ability to construct and manage self-built resources on budget and on schedule. Staff’s focus on the Company’s experience with Plant Vogtle is misplaced. Vogtle Units 3 & 4 were first-of-a-kind technology and were the first new nuclear units to be built in the country in 30 years. The nuclear construction workforce had to be trained, the supply chain had to be established, and the project faced uphill battles due to the Westinghouse bankruptcy and the challenges of building a mega-plant during an international pandemic.
In contrast, SCS has had significant recent success in building Plant Barry Unit 8 for Alabama Power, which was completed on schedule and below budget. The Company is leveraging that experience and applying it at Plant Yates Units 8-10 as well as the 765 MW portfolio of BESS resources certified by the Commission last fall, including the BESS resources near the Robins and Moody Air Force Bases, Plant Hammond, and McGrau Ford Phase I and II. Notably, all these projects are projected to be completed below budget and ahead of schedule.
Further, the Company has a demonstrated history of successfully developing self-built solar facilities, including those approved by this Commission as part of the 3x30 military portfolio and subsequent military and university portfolio of solar resources, as described further in our testimony.
III.PORTFOLIO APPROACH
Q. WHY IS THE COMPANY SEEKING TO CERTIFY THE COP RESOURCES AS PORTFOLIOS?
A.Certifying the COP resources as portfolios is efficient and reflects how Georgia Power plans to manage these projects. In addition, certifying Company-owned resources as a portfolio is consistent with past practice and recent certifications from this Commission. For these reasons, the Company requests that the Commission approve the certification of the two proposed portfolios, one for COP BESS, including Wadley, plus BESS + Solar, and a second for the COP thermal projects.
Q.HAS THE COMMISSION PREVIOUSLY CERTIFIED COMPANY-OWNED RESOURCES AS A PORTFOLIO?
A.Yes. As recently as last fall, the Commission approved the certification of the 765 MW portfolio of BESS resources near the Robins and Moody Air Force Bases and Plant Hammond, along with McGrau Ford Phase I and II. The Commission also approved the development of three portfolios of self-build solar projects at military bases, universities, and community solar sites. The first portfolio included solar projects at Fort Benning, Fort Gordon, and Fort Stewart. The solar facilities at Kings Bay Naval Base and Marine Corps Logistics Base (“MCLB”) Albany were later added to that portfolio. The second portfolio consisted of one military installation and three community solar projects. The third portfolio consisted of one military installation and a solar facility at Fort Valley State University. Each of these self-build resources was certified or approved as a portfolio of similar resources.
In working with our military partners, the self-build solar projects were certified in a portfolio with a single certified cost. The Company was able to manage multiple portfolios of self-build solar projects, all of which came in under budget.
Q.ARE THERE BENEFITS OR COST SAVINGS REALIZED FOR CUSTOMERS AS A RESULT OF CERTIFYING THE RESOURCES AS A PORTFOLIO?
A.Yes. While the projects are developed individually and the costs are individually defined, a portfolio approach provides the best mechanism to lower costs for customers. With staggered commercial operation dates across projects and portfolios, the Company structures construction and cost management strategies to implement lessons learned in real time to reduce costs to customers.
Q.DOES A PORTFOLIO APPROACH REDUCE THE LEVEL OF TRANSPARENCY NEEDED FOR COMMISSION OVERSIGHT?
A.No. The Company is committed to providing adequate transparency and oversight for each of the COP resources it is seeking to certify. Certifying the resources as a portfolio does not detract from the ability of the Company, Staff, or the Commission to oversee the construction of the resources. There are important efficiencies to be gained in reporting, organizing, and managing the projects on a portfolio basis. However, it is important to understand on a project-by-project basis, as well as on a portfolio basis, how construction is progressing and how the projects are performing relative to expectations. Thus, even with a portfolio approach, budgets, expenditures, and schedule will still be reviewable on a project-by-project basis.

V.RESPONSE TO CRITICISM OF RESOURCE COST
Q.HOW DID THE COMPANY DEVELOP THE COSTS FOR THE COP RESOURCES?
A.The costs for the COP resources were developed through a competitive inquiry process and cost estimation by subject matter experts. Proposed and estimated costs were appropriately escalated and summarized into each project’s cost expenditure plan. In addition to the cost expenditure plans, all EPC and major equipment contracts were provided to Staff for review. When possible, project costs were compared against market research. Additionally, each project contained contingency to manage risks that are inherent to these types of projects. Any unused contingency will not be included in the final project costs and will be reported to the Commission. 
Q.HOW DO THE COSTS FOR THE COP THERMAL RESOURCES COMPARE TO CC UNITS UNDER CONSTRUCTION ELSEWHERE IN THE COUNTRY?
A.Although direct cost comparisons cannot be made due to project-specific differences in location, technology, and vendors, generally, the costs for the COP thermal resources are in line with costs reported elsewhere in the country. John Ketchum, CEO of NextEra Energy, stated that the cost of gas-fired generation has gone up three-fold, citing a cost of $2,400/kW today to replicate their latest gas-fired unit, which was finished in 2022. Wood Mackenzie’s new report shows that rising capital costs could continue to elevate costs, potentially reaching $2,800/kW by 2028. Due to the expansive need and limited production supply of turbines, costs are forecasted to continue rising to meet demand. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO SIERRA CLUB/SACE WITNESS METZ’ ALLEGATION THAT THE COMPANY’S COP THERMAL RESOURCES ARE MORE EXPENSIVE THAN MARKET?
A.Witness Metz makes several mistakes in her analysis of the COPs. For example, Table 5 of her testimony has a column entitled “Project Overnight Capital Cost (2025$).” The values shown in that column are neither overnight costs nor are they in 2025 dollars. The Company assumes Witness Metz is intending to use project in-service costs without Allowance for Funds Used During Construction (“AFUDC”) and Ad Valorem tax as a proxy for overnight costs. While this is not the utility planning definition of overnight costs, it is true that overnight costs do not include AFUDC or Ad Valorem taxes. However, in the instance of the proposed CC at Plant McIntosh as an example, the resource will be placed in-service in 2030. Therefore, the dollars shown in the column are certainly not 2025 dollars.
The value in the column is the anticipated construction cost for Plant McIntosh Unit 12 that will be placed into rate base in 2030, the year the unit achieves commercial operation. Figure 6 of Witness Metz’s testimony is entitled “Overnight capital costs data from recent combined-cycle projects across the country (2025$).” If the values from the referenced column in Table 5 were to be  adjusted for inflation back to 2025 dollars and then plotted onto Figure 6 based on the unit’s dollar per kilowatt ($/kW) value, the McIntosh CC $/kW value would be within the range shown on Figure 6 for a 2030 in-service facility. Once adjusted in this manner, it is apparent that all the proposed thermal COPs are within the range of comparable units that Witness Metz cited in her testimony. While the method of comparison employed by Witness Metz does not consider site-specific and project-specific differences that make comparisons of this type difficult and imprecise. The appropriate adjustment for inflation demonstrates that the Company’s thermal resources are, in fact, comparable to other reported costs.
VI.CONCLUSION
Q.DOES THIS CONCLUDE YOUR TESTIMONY? 
A.Yes.

II.LOAD FORECAST
Q.HAVE THERE BEEN CHANGES TO THE COMPANY’S LOAD FORECAST OR LARGE LOAD CUSTOMER COMMITMENTS SINCE THE B2026 LOAD FORECAST WAS FILED IN SEPTEMBER?
A.Yes. Since the Company filed the Direct Testimony of Francisco Valle and the Budget 2026 Load and Energy Forecast (“B2026 Load Forecast”) on September 17, 2025, Georgia Power has executed and filed with the Commission three large load customer contracts for approximately 1,900 MW. Consistent with the Commission’s April 17, 2025, Order on Georgia Power Company’s Revision to Rules and Regulations Tariff Compliance Filing, Staff reviewed these three contracts prior to execution. Although the Company has not made any official changes to the B2026 Load Forecast, the Company continues making demonstrable progress on executing large load contracts, confirming the large load customer projections included in the forecast. This load is in addition to the 6.5 GW of announced load under executed Contracts for Electric Service (“CES”), of which 4.6 GW are signed under contracts prior to the change in the Company’s Rules and Regulations and approximately 1.9 GW (soon to be 3.3 GW) are signed under contracts executed after the changes to the Rules and Regulations. Georgia Power filed its fourth large load contract for a 1.4 GW customer for Staff’s review on November 26, 2025, which would bring the total load under contract to 7.9 GW. Based on current negotiations, Georgia Power anticipates that additional execution-ready contracts for several GW of additional large load will be filed with the Commission in early 2026. 
Q.WHAT LARGE LOADS ARE INCLUDED IN THE COMPANY’S LOAD FORECAST?
A.As previously discussed, the Company adjusts its organic load forecast with the output of the LRM. The LRM risk-adjusts project loads in the economic development pipeline to adequately plan for the subset of customer load expected to materialize and be served by the Company. The Company’s B2026 Load Forecast accounts for uncertainties related to new large load projects, including the likelihood of signing a CES (i.e., the P1, P2, and P3 analysis), potential project delays, and load materialization. The Company also works directly with customers to better understand their specific needs in terms of electric service and project timing to incorporate updated information in each iteration of the load forecast. The LRM evaluates thousands of potential combinations of existing and potential economic development loads, which the Company then sorts and ranks to create a probability distribution. Georgia Power uses this probability distribution to assess and forecast the loads it will need to serve. The results from the LRM support the external adjustment applied to the Company’s organic commercial and industrial load and energy forecasts. 
Q.IS IT PRACTICAL TO ONLY PLAN FOR LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS THAT HAVE EXECUTED CONTRACTS AS RECOMMENDED BY STAFF?
A.No. Planning only to executed contracts is unreasonable as it would significantly inhibit Georgia Power’s ability to contract with new large load customers, stifle economic development growth in Georgia, and reduce the opportunity to place downward pressure on rates for all customers. Large load customers are interested in locating in Georgia for multiple reasons, including the reliability of the electric system and the availability of generating capacity within their desired timeframe. Accepting Staff’s proposal of planning capacity resource additions only after the execution of new contracts would eliminate the availability of the electric service that large load customers seek. Staff’s proposal would create a self-fulfilling prophecy whereby the load will not materialize in Georgia because a key reason that customers are choosing to come to the state has been eliminated. To further support the economic development of the state of Georgia, Georgia Power must be able to develop and procure generation resources in anticipation of the large load customers planning to invest and operate within Georgia Power’s service area.
In addition, Georgia Power does not procure resources dedicated to serving one or more individual customers; system generation is designed to serve all customers. As a result, the Georgia resource planning and regulatory framework is designed sequentially such that the IRP and RFP processes forecast customer demand, identify the Company’s capacity needs, approve the resource procurement process to fill the need, and then certify the resources needed based on updated projections. Any approach that delays procuring or certifying generation resources until contract execution undermines the statutory IRP framework and Commission-approved RFP process, introduces substantial regulatory lag, and creates undue delay in Georgia Power’s ability to serve customers. If the Company were required to wait until large load contracts are executed before requesting approval for the necessary generation and transmission, then Georgia Power would have to contract now for in-service dates roughly five to seven years in the future. Such a delay will make it virtually impossible to meet customer timelines and would place Georgia at a severe competitive disadvantage, likely resulting in those projects and the associated economic development locating to other states. 
Using the LRM methodology to forecast load, rather than capping available resources to match only contracted large loads, provides Georgia Power with the necessary contracting flexibility and lead time for contracted in-service dates. This approach also supports and continues to support the existing load forecasting and resource planning processes, which are the bedrock of the IRP and RFP processes overseen by the Commission.

A.Staff’s claim that most of the Company’s request for resource certification consists “entirely of speculative growth that is not covered by any contracts” is false and misleading. The Company procures resources to reliably serve all customers, rather than only those associated with certain types of contracts. Georgia Power therefore rejects Staff’s characterization of the certification application as addressing “speculative growth.” Moreover, Staff does not define “speculative,” and it is also equally incorrect to characterize Georgia Power’s forecasting efforts as speculative. 
Figure 10 fails to disclose the flawed assumptions Staff employs to make the Company’s request look overstated. Proposed Modifications to the LRM
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE THAT RELATIVE TO THE B2025 LOAD FORECAST AND THE FEBRUARY 2025 LOAD FORECAST, THE NEAR-TERM FORECAST FOR LARGE LOADS HAS DECREASED?
A.Yes. The near-term forecast (2026–2027) for large load customers has decreased but the longer-term forecast for the same loads has increased, as supported by recently signed CES and RFS. The decrease in the near term is not a shortcoming in the Company’s forecasting methodology or execution but rather a refinement of the LRM assumptions to more critically assess the likelihood of loads in Georgia Power’s load forecast.
Q.DO YOU AGREE WITH STAFF’S PROPOSED CHANGES TO THE LRM AS PART OF ITS SCENARIO 2 RECOMMENDATION?
A.No. As an alternative to the recommendation to certify only resources for contracted load, Staff has proposed three modifications to the LRM: (1) revise load ramp assumptions in the LRM to match actual load ramps included in executed contracts; (2) reduce the P1 assumption to a lower percentage likelihood of selecting the state of Georgia for all customers who have not outright purchased land for their project; and (3) adopt a uniform materialization assumption for all large load customers. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF’S CLAIM THAT THE LRM CONTAINS ERRORS BECAUSE THE LOAD RAMPS IN THE B2026 LRM DO NOT MATCH WHAT IS SPECIFIED IN CUSTOMER CONTRACTS. 
A.Staff criticizes the way the load ramp in the LRM does not match the load ramps included in customer contracts and claims that this is an error in the LRM for not being updated with the “latest and greatest” information. As a result, Staff concludes that the Company’s LRM overestimates load. However, the difference between the load ramps in the LRM and executed contracts is not an error. In fact, the contracted load ramps are lower than what appears in the LRM because certain customers have requested increases in their load ramps since their contracts were executed. Thus, while these increases have not yet been updated in the contracts, they have already been picked up in the LRM. Thus, Staff’s recommendation that the Company use the most recent information in the LRM is exactly what the Company is doing. The LRM captures the most recent changes and revisions to customer load ramps. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION TO REDUCE THE P1 PROBABILITY BASED ON THE TYPE OF SITE CONTROL DEMONSTRATED BY A CUSTOMER?
A.No. Staff criticizes the Company’s P1 probability—i.e., whether the customer has or will select Georgia as its location (as demonstrated by site control)—for its lack of differentiation compared to how the Company treats the P2 variable. Unlike the P2 variables, however, the distinction between types of site control has not materially impacted a large load customer’s likelihood of success. Rather, the distinguishing factor is whether a project has site control, no matter the type. Therefore, a binary outcome for the P1 probability remains appropriate. 
As clarified in the Company’s Errata to data request STF-PIA-5-2, the P1 probability reflects a customer’s first decision, whether to site its project in Georgia, which is evidenced by the identification of a specific site within the state. Evidence of this decision serves as an indicator of readiness to proceed with the Company’s technical review and marks the transition from preliminary activities with Georgia Power’s Economic Development department to formal processing within the Company’s Customer Choice department. Historically, customers have demonstrated state selection by providing location-specific information. As of May 2025, the Company requires site control documentation at intake for all new data center projects, meaning executed documentation that establishes legal rights to control of the site.
For data centers specifically, the Company applies a binary approach for assigning P1 values. A probability of 100% is assigned when the customer provides executed documentation demonstrating legally enforceable site control. Qualifying instruments include an executed lease, an executed option to purchase or lease, an executed purchase and sale agreement, or another legally enforceable instrument that provides rights to develop and operate on the site, such as an easement or license. If a data center customer has identified a preferred site and communicated location information but has not provided executed documentation evidencing enforceable site control, the Company assigns a P1 probability of 50%. To date, there is no evidence to suggest that differentiating among the type of site control documentation influences a project’s readiness to advance for further evaluation in the Company’s technical review process. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY SUPPORT STAFF’S PROPOSED UNIFORM MATERIALIZATION ASSUMPTION?
A.No. The Company does not support Staff’s proposal to alter the materialization assumption for data centers and cryptocurrency currently included in the LRM to align with the materialization assumption for other industry segments (“Uniform Materialization”). Staff’s proposal here is consistent with Staff’s previous recommendations in the 2023 IRP Update and 2025 IRP, which were ultimately not adopted. Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol used a single year snapshot of eight data center projects—which are still in active development—and relied upon that early load data to justify a change in the materialization assumption for all data centers. The Company does not agree that this small sample size of only eight projects, all of which are still ramping up to their full load, justifies changing the materialization assumption as proposed by Staff. The Company continues to agree that, when an adequate sample size of mature projects (those that have achieved their full load) becomes available, the LRM materialization assumptions for all industry segments should be reevaluated and adjusted, as needed. 
With more than 73 data centers currently on the Company’s System, the Company has significant experience with the operational characteristics and the load factors of data centers. Although not a substitute for actual load materialization data, operational and load forecast data further inform the Company’s materialization assumptions. In the absence of more mature data on materialization of the full load of new large economic development customers, this operational experience and load factor data are helpful in supporting the differentiation between the assumptions used for data centers and other industry segments. As such, no change to the materialization assumptions is needed at this time. 
Q.WHAT IS THE IMPACT TO THE LRM AND LOAD FORECAST IF STAFF’S MODIFICATIONS ARE ADOPTED?
A.If Staff’s recommended modifications to the LRM are adopted, Georgia Power would be unable to plan for future large load customers, introducing regulatory lag and making it virtually impossible to meet current and potential customer timelines. Such constraints could stifle economic development and reduce opportunities to place downward pressure on rates for all customers through the addition of new large commercial and industrial projects. Additionally, planning for low materialization of contracted customers, which the Company has an obligation to serve, jeopardizes reliability for all customers. 
Load Flexibility, Demand Response, and DERs
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE THAT LOAD FLEXIBILITY MAY HELP THE COMPANY MEET SOME NEEDS OF LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS?
A.Yes. There is some capacity value to each MW of load that can be reduced or served through load flexibility, demand response, or behind-the-meter distributed energy resources (“DER”), all of which can help reduce the MW needed to be purchased or built by the Company. The Company acknowledges this point, as demonstrated by the Company’s proactive request for, and the Commission’s approval of, five load flexibility programs in the past three IRPs.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY SUPPORT ADJUSTING ITS LOAD FORECAST NOW BASED ON ASSUMED LEVELS OF LOAD FLEXIBILITY?
A.No. The Company does not support incorporating any changes to the load forecast to account for load flexibility because any adjustment at this time would be arbitrary and optimistic. Also, it is important to note that any adjustment for such a program would be captured in the Company’s generation plans, not in the load forecast. Georgia Power offers a robust portfolio of demand response, DER, and resiliency options. The Commission has recently approved expansions of these programs, which have been made available to large load customers. The availability of these programs is part of the discussion Georgia Power has with each large load customer under active contract negotiation. Each customer is unique, but to date, no large load customer has elected to participate in any of these program offerings. 
To the extent customers participate in the Company’s load flexibility program offerings, Georgia Power has a flexible framework to account for such participation in its resource planning processes. As the Commission knows, while energy efficiency programs reduce the forecast, dispatchable program options are reflected in the Company’s supply-side resources plan. 
Unless and until participation in the Company’s existing load flexibility programs increases, including any resources in the Company’s resource plan for anticipated participation would simply be guesswork and would ultimately jeopardize Georgia Power’s ability to meet customer needs reliably with sufficient capacity and energy resources. If large load customers participate in the Company’s load flexibility programs in the future, the Company will incorporate such load reductions and revise its capacity plans appropriately. 
Q.IS LOW CUSTOMER PARTICIPATION IN THE COMPANY’S DER PROGRAMS A SIGN THAT NOT ENOUGH IS BEING DONE TO ENCOURAGE LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS TO COMMIT TO DEMAND RESPONSE, DERS, OR OTHER LOAD FLEXIBILITY OPTIONS?
A.No. While no large load customer has yet to sign up to participate in one of Georgia Power’s DER programs, the Company has seen data center participation in the Company’s commercial energy efficiency programs. Additionally, many data center customers participate and take service under the Company’s real time pricing (“RTP”) rate where they receive dynamic pricing signals, which provides insights on the best time to reduce usage to maximize efficiency of the grid. The Company anticipates that additional data center customers will show interest in taking advantage of the RTP rate. 
Q.IS A LOAD FLEXIBILITY STUDY RECOMMENDED BY STAFF NECESSARY?
A.No. The Company agrees that load flexibility is important and already has a robust portfolio of options available to meet a variety of customer needs. Georgia Power has proactively filed programs to meet that objective in each of the last three IRPs before the Commission. In addition, the Company includes load flexibility in its supply side resource planning rather than through a reduction in the load forecast.
Q.WHAT WOULD HAPPEN IF THE COMPANY OVER-RELIED ON DERS TO SERVE LOAD IN LIEU OF ADDING ADDITIONAL ENERGY PRODUCING RESOURCES?
A.If the Company were to over-rely on existing generation resources and additional DER to reduce the load to be served, the natural result would be to run existing resources more. However, many of the combustion turbines (“CTs”) and steam units are peaking resources that were not designed or planned to run like baseload resources. This could put additional strain on these resources; result in additional, longer duration outages; increase maintenance costs; and raise fuel and operational expenses beyond what is optimal for the System and each unit. In addition, some units are limited in the number of hours they can operate due to technical limits, fuel constraints, or environmental restrictions. Finally, while load flexibility and DERs involve customer participation, their performance can vary depending on operational conditions and customer response. The Company continues to implement programs that include contractual obligations to enhance predictability. 
The Company acknowledges that there is value to DERs and load flexibility in their ability to mitigate over-procurement. Demand side options can be a valuable part of the overall solution to meet customer needs in a reliable and cost-effective manner. However, load flexibility and DERs are not in and of themselves a substitute or a singular solution to the challenge of reliably serving new and existing customer needs.
III.RESOURCES RECOMMENDED FOR CERTIFICATION
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE STAFF’S RECOMMENDATIONS FOR WHICH RESOURCES SHOULD BE CERTIFIED.
A.First, Staff proposes to limit the resources certified to only those backed by large load contracts using Staff’s proposed 24.5% TRM. Under Staff’s preferred Scenario 1, only seven projects, a total of 3,125 MW, would be certified. This includes the four PPAs procured through the All-Source RFP, the Thomson and Wansley BESS COPs evaluated through the All-Source RFP, and the Tenaska Heard County PPA contracted through the Supplemental Resources procurement. If additional large load contracts are executed by March 16, 2026, and more resources are needed, Staff has prepared a conditionally approved list of resources that they argue can be used to serve the new demand. Again, under Scenario 2, the conditional approval list would be used to support serving any new large load contracts executed after March 16, 2026.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH THIS APPROACH?
A.No. Staff’s focus on serving only executed large load contracts ignores the fact that the Company is also seeking certification for additional generation resources for several reasons separate and apart from the growth in large load customers, such as to replace expiring PPAs, to serve organic load growth, and to meet the Company’s approved TRM.
Q.IS IT APPROPRIATE TO CONDITIONALLY CERTIFY A SUBSET OF RESOURCES BASED ON WHETHER ADDITIONAL LARGE LOAD CONTRACTS CAN BE EXECUTED BY MARCH 16, 2026?
A.No. While conditionally certifying resources may provide more time for large load contracts to be signed prior to moving forward with the requested resources, a conditional certification does not signal confidence and commitment to the Company’s PPA counterparties; engineering, procurement, and construction (“EPC”) contractors; equipment manufacturers; and large load customers. Rather, conditional certification implies a delay that potentially throws the likelihood of certification in question. Additionally, there are substantial milestones in several of the Company’s contracts for various COP projects that must be committed to by year-end and become significantly more costly to reverse if certification is delayed into 2026 or beyond. A conditional certification is also inconsistent with the Commission’s IRP process that is driven by meeting long-term needs utilizing the Company’s load forecasting, planning, and RFP processes, which ultimately result in resource certifications. If certification is contingent on future contracts, the regulatory process is upended and the integrity of the planning process is harmed. 
Q.IF STAFF’S APPROACH IS ADOPTED, ARE THERE ADDITIONAL RISKS TO THE NEW CONSTRUCTION PROJECTS ON THE CONDITIONALLY APPROVED LIST BY PRIORITIZING EXISTING RESOURCES FOR FULL CERTIFICATION?
A.Yes. While certifying the projects with non-construction risk and only “conditionally” certifying the projects requiring procurement and construction may sound reasonable, it actually exacerbates the risk of the new construction projects by adding delay and uncertainty. A certification approach that increases project execution risk by design is poorly thought out and does not establish the best path towards successfully executing projects to serve customers effectively.
Q.IS CONDITIONAL CERTIFICATION NECESSARY TO PROTECT CUSTOMERS IF THE PROJECTED LOAD GROWTH MATERIALIZES SLOWER THAN ANTICIPATED OR SOME OF THE LOAD DOESN’T MATERIALIZE AT ALL? 
A.No. As part of its normal system planning processes, and at least as often as its annual IRP process, the Company reviews its capacity needs based on its updated load forecast and resource assumptions. If the projected load growth materializes slower than anticipated in this filing or some of the load does not materialize at all, the Company will determine if any resource actions should be taken considering reliability, costs, and impacts to customers. Depending on the timing of any change in projected load growth, the Company would evaluate all available options, including but not limited to the following, and select the most appropriate action(s), if any:
Seek to increase or accelerate the load ramps from existing customers who are capable of using more energy earlier but were not planned for in the forecast; 
Reduce the size of needs being procured in future RFPs;
Delay the timing of future RFPs;
Pause, delay, or cancel resources being constructed;
Consider load changes when evaluating existing resources or programs; or
Sell capacity beyond what is needed to satisfy the TRM to other entities in the Southeast through Southern Wholesale Energy (“SWE”) for the period of time that such resources exceed the approved reserve margin.

IV.CRITICISMS OF THE RFP EVALUATION
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE STAFF’S CRITICISMS OF THE COMPANY’S RFP EVALUATION METHODOLOGY.
A.Generally, Staff’s testimony suggests a preference for an evaluation where all submissions are run through the Aurora model simultaneously and the resulting output is used for ranking resources. While using the portfolio expansion functionality of Aurora is a reasonable method of selecting resources, it is not the best or only way to effectively evaluate resources during a sequenced, real-time procurement. In fact, the resource rankings established by Staff are not materially different than the rankings from the Company’s evaluation. Notwithstanding the similarity of results, Staff provided several critiques of the Company’s evaluation methodology regarding the term equalization approach, incremental ELCC reduction assumption, exclusion of the additional sum from the evaluation of purchased power resources, and use of an individual rather than a portfolio approach to the evaluation. Notably, the source of these criticisms stem from Staff’s consultant’s preference for using the Aurora model’s portfolio expansion functionality to evaluate and select resources. In contrast, the Company’s evaluation approach provides a detailed ranking, which allows for careful review of each cost and benefit category and its contribution to the net cost, that is easily accessible to the Company, Staff, and the IE at each stage of the RFP process.
Q.WAS THE COMPANY’S EVALUATION METHODOLOGY LOCKED DOWN AND AGREED TO WITH STAFF AND THE IE BEFORE BID DATA WAS RELEASED TO THE COMPANY?
A.Yes. Staff and others are free to criticize the Company’s evaluation process and methodology in hindsight, but consistent with every Georgia Power RFP, the Company, Staff, and the IE discussed and agreed to the evaluation methodology and locked it down before any bid data was released by the IE to the Company. Thus, the evaluation methodology was vetted and pre-approved in advance before any bids were evaluated.
Q.WHY IS IT INAPPROPRIATE TO INCLUDE AN ADDITIONAL SUM IN THE RFP EVALUATION AS SUGGESTED BY STAFF?
A.The purpose of an additional sum is to incentivize the Company to pursue power purchases in lieu of building resources itself. The additional sum is a regulatory construct and should not be treated as a bid cost. Therefore, factoring it into competitive bid evaluations distorts the integrity of the RFP evaluation. If the Company included an additional sum as part of its evaluation of third-party resources, it would increase those bid costs in the evaluation process, thus negatively affecting their rankings. Ultimately, including the additional sum in the evaluation process could disincentivize the Company from selecting a third-party resource. In addition, unless the additional sum is set in advance, the Company would not have an additional sum value to apply to all market bids.

Q.IS IT REASONABLE TO COMPARE THE TERMS AND CONDITIONS OF THE 2029-2031 ALL-SOURCE RFP TO THOSE OF THE ESS RFP?
A.No. Criticizing a Company RFP for not incorporating the terms and conditions of a later RFP inappropriately applies hindsight as a criticism and fails to consider both market shifts as well as the implementation of lessons learned in the later RFP.
VI.COST RECOVERY
Cost Recovery
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE THAT IT IS APPROPRIATE TO CAP COSTS ON THE COP RESOURCES AT THE CERTIFIED AMOUNT?
A.No. Staff recommended that the Commission limit the recovery of capital cost to the estimates provided by the Company in this proceeding to incent the Company to efficiently manage projects for the protection of customers. However, Staff’s recommendation fails to acknowledge that the Company is incentivized under the current regulatory structure to efficiently manage these projects and that it is in the Company’s best interest to deploy resources at the best cost for customers. Under the current regulatory framework, before Georgia Power can recover on any amount in excess of the certified amount, the Company has the burden to show that any amount above the certified amount is reasonable and prudent. Moreover, the Commission, through its Staff, routinely monitors construction on generation projects and has ample oversight to ensure that the Company efficiently manages the construction of new generation projects. 
The resources proposed for certification in this case are being developed to serve retail customer needs, and the revenues that will be generated from large load customers are expected to more than offset the incremental cost to serve those customers; thus, providing benefits to all customers. The projects requested for certification are necessary to ensuring the Company can serve load and that the benefits of providing that service materialize. In that regard, there is nothing in this case that suggests that the existing regulatory structure is not protective of customers, and there is no justification for altering it in a manner inconsistent with Georgia law. Finally, the heightened customer protections in contracts executed after the new Commission-approved Rules and Regulations, such as the termination payments, collateral requirements, minimum bills designed to recover 100% of incremental costs, and longer contract term requirements, provide additional protections for existing customers.
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF’S REJECTION OF THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED PORTFOLIO APPROACH.
A.Staff recommends that the Commission reject the Company’s portfolio-based certification costs proposal based on a belief that savings from completing an individual project under the certified amount should accrue to customers’ benefit rather than offset potential cost overruns on other projects. Staff states that “savings from one project should not be used to eliminate examination and review of cost overruns from another project.” However, this characterization misunderstands the Company’s proposal and disregards the benefits that customers receive from managing project construction and contingency across a portfolio of like resources.
No aspect of the Company’s proposal seeks to eliminate audit and review of potential cost overruns on an individual project basis. As proposed, the Company seeks certification of the COP resources by technology type, meaning the portfolio of COP BESS and BESS + Solar resources and the portfolio of COP Thermal resources. The costs of each COP thermal project and COP BESS project are still known, identified, and certified within the broader portfolios. As stated in the Direct Hearing, “we provide construction monitoring with all the details of those projects, and we have filed costs by project. But it’s more efficient for us to manage and report out on those portfolios because they’re all being developed together by the same team, [and] there’s efficiencies that can be gained there.”
The Company’s request to certify the resources requested for certification as a portfolio more closely aligns with how the Company plans to manage the development and construction of the proposed COP projects. Further, this approach mirrors how the Company has sought and received certification for prior Company-owned resources. Witness Bush and Witness Hayes elaborate more on the management of existing COP projects as a portfolio and detail the reasons why that is a critical part of Georgia Power’s request in this case.
Q.IS IT TRUE THAT CUSTOMERS WILL NOT SEE COST SAVINGS IF THE REQUESTED RESOURCES ARE CERTIFIED AS A PORTFOLIO?
A.No. Georgia Power will only seek cost recovery for, and customers will only be asked to pay for, the actual costs incurred to construct these generation facilities. If actual costs spent on these projects are less than the certified amount, those are tangible cost savings passed on to all customers. Certification of the portfolio of individual resources does not remove that savings opportunity for customers.
Q.WHAT IS STAFF’S POSITION REGARDING THE RECOVERY OF PRECONSTRUCTION COSTS RELATED TO PROJECTS THAT MAY NOT BE CERTIFIED AND THAT CANNOT BE TRANSFERRED TO OTHER PROJECTS?
A.Staff has stated that they oppose the deferral of such costs because “the Company should be treated the same as other bidders in the RFP who also incur development costs.”
Q.DOES THE COMPANY HAVE CONCERNS WITH STAFF’S POSITION?
A.Yes. This justification is short sighted and ignores the fact that Georgia Power has an obligation to serve its customers. No developer in an RFP has this obligation, nor does any developer in the RFP have 2.8 million customers depending upon it to provide reliable service. There is no disputing that the Company has utilized the generation development markets to benefit customers through numerous RFPs, but utilization of the RFP process does not substitute or relieve the Company of its obligations to ensure that adequate resources are available to reliably serve its retail customers. This means that the Company must stand ready to take the actions necessary to ensure resources are available, like the preconstruction activities the Company is seeking to defer and recover in this case. These activities are often taken far in advance of when the resources are ultimately approved, but failing to take these actions could have detrimental impacts to reliability. 
Moreover, as the Company has explained previously, there is a unique opportunity for the Company to foster the economic development in the state of Georgia so that it can provide benefits to all customers. The preconstruction activities undertaken by the Company associated with the resources brought forward for certification in this case were incurred to meet the capacity needs of our retail customers. Accordingly, the Company has reasonably requested to recover these costs. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY HAVE ANY CONCERNS WITH THE COMMISSION REVIEWING THESE COSTS?
A.No. The Company would welcome Commission review of any amounts deferred consistent with this request. Indeed, Staff in its own testimony indicated that if a deferral was approved in this case for preconstruction costs that a review of those costs take place. The Company is not opposed to that review and would anticipate that the Commission would consider the deferred preconstruction costs at the time that those costs are being considered for recovery.
Q.IS GEORGIA POWER COMMITTED TO FOLLOWING THE RULES AND REGULATIONS AND ORDERS OF THE COMMISSION TO ENSURE THAT LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS PAY THE INCREMENTAL COST TO SERVE THEM? 
A.Yes. As provided in the Commission’s January 28, 2025, Order Approving Revisions to Georgia Power Company’s Rules and Regulations in Docket No. 44280, the Company is required to exercise its discretion under the Rules and Regulations changes in a manner designed to protect existing customers from bearing any of the costs of adding large load customers. Staff and Intervenors assert that there should be a guarantee “that costs associated with unmaterialized load would not be passed on to existing customers.” However, this criticism fails to acknowledge the prior orders of this Commission regarding large load contracting as well as the various regulatory processes in place—such as rate cases, IRPs, certifications, and fuel cases—that govern cost recovery. Under the current constructive regulatory framework, adequate mechanisms are in place to protect existing customers and to ensure that the Commission has appropriate oversight.  
Estimated Customer Impact 
Q.DO YOU HAVE CONCERNS REGARDING STAFF’S CALCULATION OF THE IMPACT ON CUSTOMERS FROM THE CERTIFICATION OF THE RESOURCES REQUESTED BY THE COMPANY TO SERVE RETAIL LOADS IN THIS CASE?
A.Yes, we have significant concerns. The Newsome Panel concludes, through Figure 1 in their testimony, that approval of the Company’s request would significantly increase the Company’s revenue requirement from 2029–2031, resulting in an increase to the average residential bill of $20/month by 2031. Staff ignores the revenues from large load customers that would offset the costs of the additional resources, a factor that cannot be omitted when projecting rate impacts. Staff’s conclusion also assumes that the revenue requirement will be equally spread across all energy demand for both existing customers and new large load customers. This calculation makes incorrect assumptions and is not reliable. 
Q.PLEASE ELABORATE ON YOUR CONCERNS WITH STAFF’S RESIDENTIAL RATE IMPACT CALCULATION. 
A.Staff’s rate impact calculation assumes the Company would completely ignore the Commission’s directive, as well as the Company’s long-standing approach to marginal cost pricing, and simply charge all customers, new and existing, equally for the cost of the new resources. This is flatly incorrect, and Staff’s testimony provides no rationale for why the Company would deviate from its current marginal pricing approach. Staff’s calculation also ignores the prior orders from this Commission approving the revisions to the Company’s Rules and Regulations, which are designed to ensure that large load customers are held responsible for the incremental cost incurred to serve them. Under this regulatory construct, an assumed equal allocation of cost is flatly unsupported and not a reliable approach to determining future rate impacts from this certification. Further, the absence of any meaningful representation of revenues in Figure 1 is also deeply concerning. As the Company has explained for more than two years, these large load customers have the potential to bring significant economic benefit to the System and the opportunity to not only cover the incremental cost to serve themselves but also put downward pressure on rates for all customers. Staff’s Figure 1 simply omits this important detail. 
Q.WHY IS STAFF’S OMMISSION OF REVENUES IN FIGURE 1 MISLEADING? 
A.Just as projected costs factor into future revenue requirements, so do projected revenues. Indeed, Staff’s own testimony makes a footnote reference to the Company’s incremental large load revenue forecast, but then Staff chooses to completely ignore those revenues and instead depict rate impacts without including a critical component to that calculation. Had Staff chosen to acknowledge the projected revenues that support this certification request—even using their own cost projections—those projected revenues would have exceeded the projected cost for the resources requested for certification through 2031. In this regard, Staff’s analysis of rate impacts is incomplete and one-sided, failing to tell the entire story. In addition, by refusing to acknowledge revenues in their calculation, Staff’s simplistic calculation fails to account for the overall impact that not serving new economic loads may have on the state and ultimately all Georgia Power customers—assuming Staff’s positions in this case were adopted and economic development stalled in Georgia.
Additional Sum Amount
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION TO REDUCE THE ADDITIONAL SUM FOR THE PPAS TO $2.30/kW?
A.No. Staff rejects the Company’s proposed $3.00/kW-year additional sum and sidesteps consistency with the Commission’s recent orders to advocate for an additional sum level approved 15 years ago for “consistency purposes.” This is not only inconsistent but out of date. The only difference between the justification supporting the Company and Staff’s requests is the date in time for which the additional sum was approved. Staff has provided no reason why the Commission should ignore its more recent precedent. In terms of precedential value, the Commission should consider its more recent decisions determinative. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH SETTING THE ADDITIONAL SUM BASED ON THE ELCC CONTRIBUTION RATHER THAN THE NAMEPLATE CAPACITY?
A.No. The additional sum for power purchases has always been based on the nameplate capacity of the resource. To reduce the additional sum awarded to the Company based on the capacity contribution of the resource undermines the purpose for which the additional sum is intended to serve, reduces the incentive for the Company to purchase power rather than build it itself, and is inconsistent with the calculation of additional sum for BESS resources in the Company’s renewable resource RFPs. There is no justification for changing the methodology for supply side additional sums in this case. 
VII. CONCLUSION
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE COMPANY’S REQUESTS IN THIS CASE.
A.The Company seeks Commission approval of the 18 diverse resources selected from the All-Source RFP as well as the eight resources procured as Supplemental Resources to meet capacity needs in 2028-2031. The portfolio of resources consists of PPAs for capacity from existing natural gas resources; COP thermal, BESS, and BESS + Solar resources; as well as PPAs for new BESS resources collocated at existing solar sites already under contract with the Company. The Company’s request is based on need, not speculation. 
Approval of Georgia Power’s All-Source Application and Supplemental Resources Application will ensure the continued provision of clean, safe, reliable, and affordable electric service to all customers, support the continued economic growth of the state of Georgia, and support the continued diversification of energy resources in its portfolio without harming existing customers. Moreover, the Commission has clearly ordered that cost recovery be designed in that manner to protect existing customers from paying for the incremental costs of serving new large load customers. Accordingly, certification of these resources will contribute to the large load customer growth, putting downward pressure on rates for all customers and continuing to foster the tremendous economic growth of our state.

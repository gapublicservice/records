INTRODUCTION
The matters presently pending before the Georgia Public Service Commission (the “Commission”) are Georgia Power Company’s (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) Application for the Certification of Capacity from the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP (“All-Source Certification Application”) and its Application for the Certification of Capacity Supplemental Resources (“Supplemental Resources Certification Application”).
For the reasons set forth herein, the Commission should approve Georgia Power’s All-Source and Supplemental Resources certification request as modified by the Stipulation agreed to by Georgia Power and the Commission’s Public Interest Advocacy (“PIA”) Staff (collectively, the “Stipulating Parties”) (the “Stipulation”). The Stipulation resolves all the issues in this proceeding and is a reasonable compromise that provides benefits to all customers while taking into account recommendations and concerns raised by PIA Staff and Intervenors throughout the proceeding. Specifically, the Stipulation recommends approval of the All-Source and Supplemental Resources proposed for certification at their certified amounts. It further ensures that the economic development supported by these resources will provide significant downward pressure on rates. In fact, and as discussed further below, the Company has agreed to file its next base rate case proceeding in a manner that ensures the incremental revenue from the large load customers has downward pressure of at least $8.50 per month for a typical residential customer for the years 2029, 2030, and 2031. This reflects a balanced and cost-effective approach that benefits all customers by preserving and protecting reliability and supporting the continued economic development in the state of Georgia. 
Moreover, the Stipulation provides the Commission and PIA Staff with important oversight over Company-Owned Projects (“COP”), as the successfully implemented construction monitoring process and procedures used for Plant Yates Units 8-10 and for the 765 MW BESS Resources will be used for the COP thermal projects and the COP BESS/BESS + Solar projects, respectively. Even if any individual project exceeds its individual certified amount, Georgia Power agrees that it will not revise the certified amount or cost estimate any earlier than six months before a project’s commercial operation date. The Stipulation provides for an additional sum of $2.65/kW-year for the power purchase agreements included in the Company’s request. Finally, the Stipulation ensures continued collaboration between Georgia Power and PIA Staff, as the Company and PIA Staff have agreed to investigate the potential benefits of load flexibility, including potential load flexibility program offerings, through engagement with current and potential large load customers.
The Commission should approve the Stipulation and the Company’s All-Source and Supplemental Resources Certification Applications because they reflect a reasonable and responsible path to ensuring that Georgia Power can continue serving its existing and growing customer base with the reliability that customers value and expect, while continuing to support Georgia’s growing and thriving economy. In addition to securing necessary capacity resources, the Stipulation offers substantial protections and numerous benefits to all customers, reinforcing Georgia Power’s commitment to providing safe, reliable, and affordable electric service.
BACKGROUND
Georgia Power issued the 2029-2031 All-Source Request for Proposals (“RFP”) to meet capacity needs in the 2029-2031 timeframe. The Commission authorized Georgia Power to initiate the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP in its 2022 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) Final Order, given the long lead time to build new capacity and the resource need identified in the 2029-2031 timeframe. At the time, the RFP primarily targeted capacity needed due to expiring capacity Power Purchase Agreements (“PPAs”) and anticipated generation requirements.
The 2023 IRP Update introduced unprecedented load growth and increased capacity needs through 2031, thereby increasing the size and importance of the All-Source RFP. Georgia’s reputation as one of the nation’s top states for business has fueled record-breaking economic development, with over $24 billion in new capital investment and more than 38,000 jobs added to communities between the 2022 IRP and 2023 IRP Update. These remarkable growth opportunities have also created new and unprecedented customer demand for energy. As a result, Georgia Power adapted its load forecasting methodology to better capture and anticipate the large load customer growth it needed to serve through the Load Realization Model (“LRM”). As supported and justified by each iteration of the Load Forecast that followed the 2022 IRP (2023 IRP Update, 2025 IRP, and Budget 2026 (“B2026”)), the capacity needs through 2031 significantly increased and the importance of procuring sufficient resources to meet the need increased as well.
Consistent with the Commission’s RFP rules and the 2022 IRP Final Order, Georgia Power issued the All-Source RFP on June 20, 2024. The All-Source RFP sought to procure 8,500 megawatts (“MW”) of dispatchable capacity resources consisting of (i) Standalone Energy Storage Systems (“ESS”) with grid charging capability, (ii) ESS with Renewable Resource and grid charging capability, or (iii) thermal generation (e.g., natural gas or oil-fired). Throughout the RFP process, Staff and an Independent Evaluator (“IE”) actively and contemporaneously monitored all aspects of the All-Source RFP process in compliance with Commission Rule 515-3-4-.04(3).
After the completion of the Winter 2027-2028 Battery Energy Storage System (“BESS”) RFP and the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP, the Company anticipated an outstanding capacity need beyond the resources likely to be procured from those RFPs. Because of the urgency of the identified near-term capacity needs, conducting another RFP would not provide sufficient time for the Company to develop, procure, or purchase the necessary energy and capacity to meet those needs. Accordingly, the Company proactively developed the portfolio of supplemental resources that leverage existing relationships, infrastructure, and interconnections to ensure customer demand within the near-term capacity need timeframe can be met, ensuring all Georgia Power customers receive safe, reliable, and affordable electric service.

STANDARD OF REVIEW
Prior to beginning construction of an electric plant or entering into a long-term purchase of electric power, Georgia Power must first apply for and obtain from the Commission a certificate of public convenience and necessity. The Commission shall approve an application for certification upon finding 
that there is or will be a need for the proposed capacity resource at the time that [it] is proposed to be utilized to assure an economical and reliable supply of electric power and energy for the Georgia retail customers of a utility, that the certificate is required by the public convenience and necessity, and that the certificate complies with the provisions of this chapter and the rules of the commission.

“The Commission shall determine which combinations of resource options . . . best serve the public interest considering economics, safety, reliability, flexibility, risk, equity among ratepayers and classes, customer bills, externalities, and other factors the Commission deems appropriate.” “Based upon the evidence of record presented at the hearing on the [certification] application, the Commission shall render a decision either approving the application, approving it subject to stated conditions, approving it in part and rejecting it in part, rejecting it as filed, or providing an alternate capacity resource certification . . . .” “A utility’s application shall be approved if found to be in the public interest and to substantially comply with these regulations.” 
ARGUMENT
The Commission should approve the Stipulation. 
The Commission should adopt the Stipulation as a reasonable and beneficial resolution to all issues in this proceeding.
On December 10, 2025, PIA Staff filed the Stipulation with the Commission. The Stipulation consists of the following eight items:
The All-Source Certification shall be approved as filed unless amended by the Stipulation.
The Supplemental Resources and the corresponding changes to existing PPAs are approved through the RFP exceptions in Commission Rules 515-3-4-.04(3)(f)(3) and 515-3-4-.04(3)(f)(6).
Each resource certified pursuant to this Stipulation is certified at its individual project cost (“Certified Amount”) as identified in Table 1.5 of the Application for the Certification of Capacity from the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP and page 15 of the Application for the Certification of Supplemental Resources for 2028-2031 Capacity.
The Company agrees to file its next base rate case in a manner that will ensure the incremental revenue from large load customers has downward pressure of at least $8.50 per month to the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month for the years 2029, 2030, and 2031. The incremental revenue forecast as shown in Attachment A to this Stipulation will be set as a minimum estimate for the years 2029, 2030, and 2031.
Construction monitoring shall occur for all Company-Owned Projects (“COP”) and will be implemented according to the process and procedures used for Plant Yates Units 8-10 (for the COP thermal projects) and the process and procedures used for the 765 MW BESS Resources approved in Docket No. 55478 (for the BESS/BESS + Solar projects). The Company shall determine its means and methods of managing these projects and it may file its periodic monitoring reports on a portfolio basis (one for the COP thermal projects and one for BESS/BESS + Solar) as long as the monitoring reports also include project-level information similar to the reporting done for Plant Yates 8-10 and the 765 MW BESS Resources. Staff monitoring shall be conducted by project, by unit, including common and balance of plant, and information shall be provided at the level of detail in order for Staff to perform its due diligence. Nothing in this Stipulation shall be construed to limit or constrain Staff's ability to select Staffs means and methods for monitoring these projects. Construction monitoring reports shall be filed for each portfolio from the date construction begins through the date of commercial operation.
In the event any individual project is forecasted to exceed the Certified Amount, Georgia Power agrees that it shall not file for a revision to the Certified Amount or cost estimate earlier than six months prior to the scheduled commercial operation date of the project. For purposes of evaluation of potential disallowances, each project will be examined individually.
The Company will collect an additional sum of $2.65/kW-year for the term of the power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) certified in this Stipulation. Georgia Power will be allowed to continue to collect the additional sum on existing solar PPAs amended herein at the currently approved level, through the extended term of the PPA as approved in this proceeding.
The Company and PIA Staff shall work collaboratively to investigate the potential benefits of load flexibility. Prior to the collaboration, the Company shall engage current and potential large load customers to solicit input on large load flexibility options, including contract terms and cost saving opportunities resulting from flexibility, and shall report its findings to Staff. The Company and Staff shall report back to the Commission within 120 days of the Final Order in this proceeding regarding the potential for an additional load flexibility program and whether additional steps should be taken to create another program offering, including recommendations for the timing for consideration and approval of such offering, if determined to be beneficial.

The Stipulation provides significant protections and benefits to Georgia Power’s existing, non-large load customers. 
The Stipulation is a well-reasoned and balanced resolution of the Company’s All-Source and Supplemental Resources Certification Applications that will not only secure the resources needed to serve Georgia Power’s growing customer base reliably but also makes certain that the growing economic development will benefit all of Georgia Power’s customers. The Company recognizes the magnitude and significance of this request and the opportunities it provides for our customers. To proactively demonstrate Georgia Power’s commitment to affordability, the Company has agreed to lock in a threshold level of that benefit to ensure that this certification results in downward pressure in the Company’s next base rate case.

This protective framework put in place by the Commission already provides significant benefits to customers by ensuring that the revenues collected from large load customers cover the incremental cost to serve those customers. Moreover, being able to serve these large load economic development customers has already provided significant value to existing customers by allowing the Company to keep base rates stable by extending the current Alternate Rate Plan (“ARP”) for an additional three years.

(Tr. 1302–03.) This downward pressure equates to $8.50 in savings per month (or approximately $102 per year) to the typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kilowatt-hours per month, for the years 2029, 2030, and 2031. (Tr. 1303.) All of Georgia Power’s existing customers will see additional downward pressure on rates. The forecasted revenues that Georgia Power is locking in for calendar years 2029, 2030, and 2031 will more than cover the estimated cost of the resources requested in this proceeding. In fact, the estimated $8.50 per month for the typical residential customer is the minimum downward pressure that customers should expect to see. If costs are lower than expected, this benefit could be even higher. (Tr. 1373.) Timing wise, this commitment to downward pressure on rates is most helpful between 2029-2031 since the revenue requirement (amount needed to be recovered in rates to cover the costs) will be highest in these years when the proposed resources achieve commercial operation and are placed in rates. And as Company Witness Looney testified at the Staff/Intervenor/Rebuttal Hearing, “then by the nature of declining revenue requirements, the revenue requirement would actually start to decline, but we expect the revenues to maintain strength. And you actually create sort of this virtuous cycle where the costs are decreasing, the revenues are likely maintaining or increasing, and the benefits continue to accrue.” (Tr. 1369).
The Stipulation provides material and quantifiable savings for customers and follows prior examples of the Company’s proactive approach to addressing affordability, such as the 2025 stipulation to extend the ARP that froze base rates for three years, and the 2023 IRP Update, which first quantified the economic benefits of the Company’s approach to serving large load customers. Georgia Power is leveraging the opportunities afforded by serving large load customers to benefit its existing 2.8 million customers through enhanced customer protections in large load contracts and the assurance of downward pressure on customer rates, all while continuing to maintain the resiliency and reliability of the Company’s electric system. Therefore, the Stipulation should be approved by this Commission, without modifications.

The Commission should certify the requested resources as they are necessary to support the customer demand reflected in the Company’s reasonably prepared load forecast. 
The resources requested in both the All-Source and Supplemental Resources Applications are needed to meet the Company’s capacity needs for 2028-2031. The B2026 Load Forecast, which was filed in September, estimates that there is more than 8,400 MW of customer load growth to be met by 2031. As demonstrated throughout the evidentiary record in this proceeding, Georgia Power employed a reasonable methodology in developing the load forecast, including use of the LRM to capture the impact of the large load customer growth experienced in the past several years. Georgia Power developed its load forecast (organic and large load) using the most current and accurate data available to the Company, consistent with prior Commission-approved load forecasts. With each iteration, the Company continues refining its LRM assumptions based on its experience with large load customers moving through the technical review process, coming online, and ramping to their full demand. The Company’s executed contracts recently filed with the Commission demonstrate that the large loads the Company projected are coming to Georgia and actualizing against what was previously forecasted. To be able to serve customers today and in the future, Georgia Power must continue forecasting necessary customer demand using tested, proven, and well-established methods so that it may appropriately plan for its future resource needs. To that end, it is unreasonable to acquire resources based only on executed contracts with large load customers as suggested by several parties in this case. The Company’s approach is rooted in experience, proactive, and rightfully tailored to address capacity needs from customers with real, significant commitments. 
The load forecast is therefore a reasonable estimation of the load that the Company will be required to serve through the 2030/2031 timeframe. That load is coming, and the Company must be prepared to serve it. 
The forecast methodology is reasonable and consistent with the approaches approved in 2023 IRP Update and 2025 IRP.
Georgia Power employs a well-established, consistent, and Commission-approved process for developing its load forecast. Both the long- and short-term organic forecasts used in this case were developed using well-established econometric techniques previously approved by the Commission. (Tr. 190.) For the B2026 Load Forecast, the Company divided the commercial class specifically into two segments: an organic data center segment and all other categories (such as offices, schools, etc.). (Id.) The organic load forecasting methodology includes careful examination of key demographic and economic variables that are significant drivers of energy consumption. (Id.) Further, the Company uses external adjustments to account for electric vehicles, customer generation, demand side management programs, and new industries and trends not reflected in historical data. (Id.)
As this Commission knows, one of those new industries and trends not reflected in historical data involves large loads. Recognizing the magnitude and uncertainty inherent with the onset of large load customer growth, Georgia Power developed the LRM to risk-adjust its organic forecast. (Tr. 190.) The LRM evaluates thousands of possible combinations of existing and potential economic development loads, which are then sorted and ranked to create a probability distribution. (Tr. 191.) This probability distribution helps Georgia Power assess the likelihood of the loads it will need to serve. (Id.) The LRM output is the basis for the large load external adjustment applied to the Company’s organic commercial and industrial load forecasts. (Id.)
The LRM is probabilistic and does not apply a one-size-fits-all approach—it does not assume that all large load projects within the large load economic development pipeline will materialize and instead accounts for a variety of uncertainties associated with new large load projects. (Tr. 190.) The LRM’s risk adjustment methodology is directly informed by conversations the Company has with potential and actual large load customers, ensuring that the realities of customer operations and the details of specific electric service and project timing needs are all accounted for with the model. (Tr. 191.) Since the LRM’s introduction in the 2023 IRP Update, the Company has continued to revise and refine its assumptions using the most recent data and information to improve the accuracy of the forecast. In fact, the B2026 Load Forecast is the first forecast against which the Company has actual data to compare. (Tr. 1272.) The month-by-month realization for the B2026 Load Forecast compared to previous forecasts demonstrates that the accuracy of the Company’s forecast is improving over time. (Id.)
By separately accounting for large load customers in the LRM, the Company avoids the potential for double counting a load in both the organic forecast and the large load adjustment. By excluding customers above certain thresholds from the organic load forecast, the organic load forecast does not extrapolate the impact of growth of existing facilities already in the forecast. (Tr. 201.) Rather, the Company’s organic load forecast and LRM adjustment for large loads are both the result of detailed analysis, are supported by a variety of factors and considerations, and address concerns regarding the potential for double counting. Accordingly, the Commission should find that the methodology used to develop the Company’s load forecast was reasonable.
Recent contract executions demonstrate how the large loads in the forecast are coming to fruition. 
The Company continues making demonstrable progress on executing large load contracts. For example, Georgia Power is in active negotiations with multiple large load customers, which represents several gigawatts of additional load. (Tr. 202.) This load is in addition to the 7.9 gigawatts (“GW”) already under an executed contract for electric service (“CES”) under both the previous and current Company Rules and Regulations, including the 1.4 GW filed for Commission review on November 26, 2025. (Tr. 1264.) Further, as discussed at the Rebuttal Hearing, the Company just signed a request for service (“RFS”) for another 1.4 GW. (Tr. 1311.) Based on current negotiations, the Company anticipates that additional execution-ready contracts for multiple GW of additional large load will be filed with the Commission in early 2026. 
Altogether, the Company has secured approximately 90% of the projected B2026 Winter Peak Demand growth by 2031 through signed contracts and will likely contract even more load from customers who have signed an RFS. (Tr. 1267.) And, to the extent a given customer does not subsequently sign a CES, there are other customers in the pipeline queue who can fill that gap. (Tr. 1385.) The Company is confident that, through additional large load contracts projected to be signed between now and March 16, 2026, the amount of load forecasted for 2031 will exceed the B2026 projected peak demand for 2031. (Tr. 1268.) By design, PIA Staff and the Commission have been made well aware of these developments through the Commission’s established contract review process and will continue to be apprised of developments. (See Tr. 820.) These customer commitments confirm the large load customer projections in the load forecast, further demonstrate the reasonability of the Company’s forecasting process, and justify the need for every single resource presented for certification in this proceeding to serve anticipated load. 
It is impractical to only plan for large load customers that have executed contracts.
Planning only for executed contracts ignores the customer commitments made for viable projects prior to signing a contract for electric service. 
Due to skepticism in the Company’s large load forecast, more than one party recommended that the Commission certify only resources sufficient to meet large load under contract. This position is untenable. It ignores the very real fact that customers are making significant and valid commitments to the Company prior to signing a CES. A signed RFS is not a mere expression of interest—the RFS represents binding and enforceable obligations. (Tr. 1267.) In addition, under the revised Rules and Regulations, customers are required to post collateral, which not only represents a financial assurance by the customer but also ensures that a potential customer is serious about taking service and lowers risk to the Company and its existing customers. (Tr. 1268.) Should a large load customer subsequently decline to sign a CES after signing an RFS, the Company can still recover this collateral to adequately insulate and protect other Georgia Power customers. (Id.) When combined with a rigorous pre-RFS review process, RFS commitments reflect projects that are not only well positioned to proceed to construction but helps ensure that potential customers take an RFS seriously. (Id.) Any recommendation to allow certification only for resources backed by executed contracts ignores the realities and responsibility associated with signing an RFS. The Commission should instead find that the Company’s load forecast reasonably estimates customer demand to be served through 2031, thereby establishing the base upon which the Company’s capacity needs and requests for certification should be granted.
Waiting until contracts are executed to certify or procure resources undermines the Company’s and this Commission’s approach to resource planning under the IRP and RFP Frameworks.
Further, planning only to executed contracts, as suggested by Sierra Club Witness Metz, is unreasonable and would significantly inhibit Georgia Power’s ability to contract with new large load customers. If Georgia Power neglected to forecast and plan for customers beyond those with executed contracts, it would stifle economic development growth in Georgia and reduce the opportunity to place downward pressure on rates for all customers. (Tr. 1261.) Large load customers are interested in locating in Georgia for the reliability of the electric system and the availability of generating capacity within their desired timeframe. (Tr. 1265.) Accepting Sierra Club’s proposal to plan capacity resource additions only after the execution of new contracts would eliminate the availability of the electric service that large load customers seek. (Id.) To further support the economic development of the state of Georgia, Georgia Power must be able to develop and procure generation resources in anticipation of the large load customers planning to invest and operate within Georgia Power’s service area. (Tr. 1265–66.)
This approach also assumes that resources are procured to serve individual customers—they are not. Georgia Power does not procure resources dedicated to serving one or more individual customer—it designs system generation to serve all customers. (Tr. 1266.) Georgia’s resource planning and regulatory framework is designed sequentially such that the IRP, RFP, and certification processes forecast customer demand, identify the Company’s capacity needs, approve the resource procurement process to fill the need, and then certify the resources needed based on updated projections, respectively. (Id.) Any approach that delays procuring or certifying generation resources until contract execution, such as that proposed by the Sierra Club, undermines the statutory IRP framework and Commission-approved RFP process. (Id.) Furthermore, this approach creates undue delay in Georgia Power’s ability to serve customers. (Id.) If the Company were required to wait until large load contracts are executed before requesting approval for the necessary generation and transmission, then Georgia Power would have to contract now for in-service dates roughly five to seven years in the future. (Id.) Such a delay will make it virtually impossible to meet customer timelines and would place Georgia at a severe competitive disadvantage to other states. (Id.) 
As Georgia Power has demonstrated, using the Company’s LRM methodology to forecast load, rather than capping available resources to match only contracted large loads, provides Georgia Power with the necessary contracting flexibility and lead time to meet contracted in-service dates. (Tr. 1266.) This approach also supports and continues to support the existing load forecasting and resource planning processes, which are the bedrock of the IRP and RFP processes overseen by the Commission. (Id.) As such, the Commission should approve the Company’s load forecast.
Large load customer growth is not the only driver of the capacity needs the requested resources will meet. 
The certification requests in this proceeding are designed to meet the capacity needs based on the customer demand established in the Company’s load forecast. While large load growth is certainly a significant part of the Company’s current capacity needs, it is not the only driver of the capacity needs or resource requests in this case. (Tr. 1270.) The Company’s capacity needs also reflect organic load growth, resources needed to meet the Commission-approved Target Reserve Margin (“TRM”), and the anticipated decreases in available purchased capacity due to expiring PPAs. (Tr. 1270.) The Company has a net 2,206 MW of PPA expirations and decrease in capacity purchases between 2028 and 2031, which must be extended or replaced independent of new industrial or data center load additions. (Id.) Prior procurements, approved unit upgrades, and resources certified by this Commission since the 2023 IRP Update are already helping meet a portion of this capacity need. (Id.) However, notwithstanding the resources approved to date, additional resources are still needed to meet the Company’s aggregate capacity needs. The Company has taken a disciplined, data-driven, and refined approach to resource procurement—beginning with a reasonable load forecast of the demand to be served and grounded in the Commission-approved planning and procurement processes—to ensure reliability, support economic development, and responsibly meet current and projected customer demand. (Tr. 1272.) 
The Company conducted a fair, robust, and transparent RFP process overseen by Staff and the IE to procure the All-Source capacity resources.
The Company conducted the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP in accordance with the Commission-approved RFP Documents, Commission Rules, and Commission Orders. 
Pursuant to Commission Rule 515-3-4-.04(3) (the “RFP Rule”), the Company is required to issue a competitive solicitation for supply side resources identified through an IRP. As approved by the Commission in the 2022 IRP Final Order, the Company prepared the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP, pro forma contracts, and supporting documents in accordance with the RFP Rule in consultation with Staff and the IE. (Tr. 159–60.) In accordance with the RFP Rule’s requirement to select an IE, the Commission approved Accion Group, LLC to serve as the IE for the All-Source RFP on November 7, 2023. (Id.) Per the RFP Rule, the Company then drafted the All-Source RFP, a pro forma Combined Cycle (“CC”) PPA, a pro forma Combustion Turbine (“CT”) PPA, a pro forma ESS with Renewable Resource PPA, a pro forma Standalone ESS PPA, a pro forma Asset Purchase Agreement (“APA”), and a pro forma Build-Transfer Agreement (“BTA”) (collectively, the “RFP Documents”) with input from potential RFP participants, Staff, and the IE over a period of several months. (Id.) The draft RFP and the four PPAs were posted publicly on the IE Website, which served as the RFP Platform. (Tr. 160.) As provided for in the RFP Rule, Georgia Power held a Bidders’ Conference webinar to allow potential bidders and interested parties the opportunity to receive more information about the RFP from Georgia Power, Staff, and the IE and to ask additional questions and further comment upon the RFP Documents. (Id.) The Company further provided a Comment Period for RFP Participants to provide input on the draft RFP Documents. (Id.) The Company, Staff, and the IE reviewed each of the comments. Georgia Power accepted and incorporated into the RFP Documents all appropriate revisions suggested by the market. (Id.)
To avoid bias and ensure all RFP participants are afforded the same access to information as everyone else, the RFP Rule prohibits bidders and potential bidders from communicating with the IE, Staff, or the soliciting entity about any aspect of the RFP or its evaluation, except for communications expressly allowed by the approved RFP/IE structure or publicly via the IE’s website. COP Team and the Evaluation Team members must be clearly identified and physically kept separate for all RFP Process activities. COP and Evaluation Team Lists were maintained and regularly updated on the Commission’s website in the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP docket and IE Website. COP Team and Evaluation Team members executed the required acknowledgements agreeing to abide by the rules restricting communications. Additionally, Georgia Power voluntarily holds itself to the same standards of conduct and separation protocols for the COP Team as it does for the Affiliate Bid Team. (Tr. 1286.)
In accordance with the RFP and the RFP Rule, Georgia Power conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the All-Source RFP proposal submissions, which included the following: (1) Initial Price Rankings (initial price-only rankings before the IE released full bid data to Georgia Power); (2) Conforming List Screening (screening to establish a Conforming List of RFP submissions); (3) Competitive Tier selection; (4) Refreshed Submission (price refresh opportunity for active RFP submissions); (5) Short List Selection (detailed evaluation for Short List determinations); and (6) Winning Bid Selection (final selection of winning projects). (Ex. GPC-1 at 18.) Throughout the evaluation process, Staff and the IE independently verified the Company’s evaluation methodology, determinations, and steps taken by the Company to select the Competitive Tier and Short List. (Tr. 163.) Consistent with the RFP Rule, Staff and the IE actively and contemporaneously monitored all aspects of the RFP process. (Tr. 1288.) Therefore, all RFP evaluations were performed in full transparency and in compliance with Commission rules.
The RFP was overseen from start to finish by Staff and the IE.
Consistent with the Commission’s RFP Rules, the All-Source RFP was conducted with the full participation of and oversight by Staff and the IE. As the IE highlighted in the Final IE Report, “[t]hroughout the RFP process, Staff was diligently engaged and worked closely with the IE to ensure the [Commission] rules were followed, and that fairness was extended to every Bidder.” (Tr. 968.) The IE further emphasized Staff’s involvement by noting that “[p]articipation of the Staff was helpful as it provided perspective that was unique from that of the Company, Bidders, and the IE and throughout the RFP process the Staff was actively involved alongside the IE.” (Id.)
Further, the Company’s evaluation methodology was reviewed, locked down, and agreed upon with Staff and the IE before bid data was ever released to the Company. (Tr. 989, 128485.) PIA Staff and others are free to criticize the Company’s evaluation process and methodology in hindsight, but consistent with every Georgia Power RFP, the Company, Staff, and the IE discussed and agreed to the evaluation methodology and locked it down before any bid data was released by the IE to the Company. (Tr. 1284.) Thus, the evaluation methodology was vetted and pre-approved in advance of bid evaluation. (Tr. 992.) Staff and the IE had the opportunity to object to the evaluation methodology at any time before the Company received bid data but made no such objection or concern known. Therefore, any allegation that the RFP process was not fair or transparent is simply unsupported by evidence.
All RFP participants and interested parties had the opportunity to comment on the RFP Documents and process, and the Company addressed and implemented acceptable changes in response.
Consistent with prior RFPs, potential RFP participants and interested parties were encouraged to provide feedback and propose changes to each provision of the RFP and pro forma contracts. (Tr. 1285.) The Company received hundreds of comments on its RFP Documents. (Tr. 160.) Additionally, Georgia Power held a Bidders’ Conference webinar to allow potential bidders and interested parties the opportunity to receive more information about the RFP from Georgia Power, Staff, and the IE, and to ask additional questions and provide further comment on the RFP Documents. (Id.) Based on the feedback received, the Company implemented appropriate changes to the RFP Documents. For example, Georgia Power extended the timeframe for posting bid security by two weeks. (Tr. 1202.)
The Company always seeks to continuously improve the RFP process and its RFP documents to ensure that they are attractive to market participants yet still protective of customers (Tr. 1399–1400.) Moreover, Georgia Power actively seeks feedback on its RFPs prior to issuance to the market and retrospectively following the conclusion of an RFP. (Tr. 1289.) A critical part of Georgia Power’s RFP development process, as well as its history, is implementing lessons learned and feedback from the market. (Id.) This approach to continuous improvement ensures that Georgia Power’s RFPs remain transparent, fair, and aligned with market expectations to encourage strong market participation. (Id.) To that end, the Company has repeatedly demonstrated its openness to further enhancing the RFP Documents and process, both voluntarily and as required by the Commission’s RFP Rules.
The terms and conditions of the Commission-Approved All-Source RFP struck the appropriate balance of cost and risk between sellers and Georgia Power’s customers.
In proposing changes to the RFP Documents, bidders are incentivized to propose changes that, if implemented, would improve the profitability and financeability of their projects. Bidders are market participants without an obligation to serve customers and are reasonably motivated to optimize the economics of their proposals. Consistent with other RFPs, most of the changes requested by bidders to the RFP Documents were largely motivated by a desire to lower their cost to participate, reduce performance and operational parameters, and shift as much risk to the Company and its customers as possible. It is imperative that the Company strike the right balance of structuring an RFP that seeks the desired resources, attracts sufficient market participation, and secures competitive proposals that are viable and still protective of customers. (Tr. 1286.) The Company shares this objective with Staff and the IE. (Id.) This is further guided by the Commission’s oversight and final approval of RFP Documents. (Id.) 
Regarding recommendations from SREA Witness Makhyoun for future RFPs, (Tr. 1217.) Georgia Power is already implementing revisions to several of the provisions discussed, as demonstrated by the revised terms and conditions included in the Company’s CARES 2025 RFP, Energy Storage System (“ESS”) 500 RFP, and 2032-2033 All-Source Capacity RFP. (Tr. 1289.) However, many of SREA’s recommendations would inappropriately shift risk and costs back to Georgia Power’s customers. (Tr. 1240–41.) SREA Witness Makhyoun unsurprisingly took greatest issue with RFP terms that would create a risk or cost borne by the developer or seller rather than Georgia Power customers. (Id.) Specifically, she took issue with RFP terms related to bid security, performance security, and change of law. (Id.) Witness Makhyoun concedes that if these risks were not borne by the seller, Georgia Power customers would bear the cost or risks. (Id.) Witness Makhyoun further admits that if the Company and the Commission have decided that the RFP provisions strike the appropriate balance of costs and risks between sellers and customers, then even a slightly higher PPA price would still be in the best interest of customers, as it is still protective of customers. (Tr. 1240–42.)
The RFP was not biased towards or against any RFP Participants.
SREA Witness Makhyoun alleges that the All-Source RFP was biased in favor of COPs. (Tr. 1197.) This allegation is completely false and should be disregarded. With the rigorous RFP framework; physical and virtual separation of the Evaluation Team, COP Team, and Affiliate Bid Team; and the active oversight from Staff and the IE; it is disappointing and insulting to assert that the RFP was designed to favor COPs. The framework set forth in the Commission’s Rules was designed to prevent bias towards affiliate bids and, by extension, COPs. (Tr. 1286.) Georgia Power voluntarily holds itself to the same standards of conduct and separation protocols for the COP team as it does for the Affiliate Bid Team. (Id.) A COP has no advantage over any other bid when submitted in a Georgia Power RFP. (Id.) Further, the terms and conditions of the RFP and pro forma agreements were reviewed by Staff and the IE, made available for public feedback and comment, and ultimately approved by the Commission. (Id.) All RFP evaluations were performed in full transparency and under the oversight of Staff and the IE, with all resource decisions made agreeably between all three parties and therefore, no bias towards COPs existed. (Tr. 1286–87.) This is evident for three reasons. 
First, the IE maintained oversight throughout the entire RFP process and specifically noted in his Final Report that the RFP was designed to be fair and compliant with Commission rules. (Tr. 968.) Georgia Power steadfastly maintains that the All-Source RFP was conducted in a fair and transparent manner.
In addition, the Company received hundreds of comments on its RFP Documents but never received any feedback from RFP participants or interested parties that participated in the comment period that the proposed terms and conditions were biased towards COPs. (Tr. 1287.) Again, in his report, the IE expressly highlighted that “[n]o Bidder contacted the IE claiming the RFP process, Bid process, or any aspect of the RFP was unfair, discriminatory, or in any way was biased for or against any Bidder or type of Bid.” (Tr. 968.) Therefore, SREA Witness Makhyoun’s allegation of bias is wholly unsupported by evidence.
Finally, a lack of market bids in the Competitive Tier or Short List is not an indication of an RFP design flaw or bias. Third-party producers participated in the All-Source RFP and submitted bids for BESS and thermal resources. Ultimately, the Company received bids totaling approximately 14,902 MW of nominal capacity, of which 6,594 MW were submitted by market (i.e., non-COP) participants. (Tr. 1288.) Unfortunately, a large number of third-party bids that were submitted into the All-Source RFP, which included payment of a $20,000 bid fee, were withdrawn by the bidders prior to posting bid security; thus, eliminating them from consideration for the Competitive Tier. (Tr. 1288.) To be clear—Georgia Power did not reject any third-party bid based on price. Rather, the lack of market bids moving forward in the evaluation was due to the voluntary withdrawal of market bids prior to the posting of bid security or a single case of non-conforming PPA revisions. Georgia Power is unable to confirm the specific reasons these RFP participants withdrew their bids; however, given the dynamic and volatile nature of the market at the time (November 2024), it is reasonable to infer that market forces—not the structure of the RFP—were the primary drivers behind their decision to withdraw. (Tr. 1288.) Despite the volatility of the market, Georgia Power conducted a successful market-based competitive solicitation and the resources chosen from the All-Source RFP were procured pursuant to a fair, open, and transparent competitive procurement process overseen by Staff and the IE.
Contrary to intervenor assertions, it is impractical to re-evaluate proposed CC units against an alternative, theoretical portfolio.
Sierra Club/SACE Witness Metz recommends that the Company re-evaluate the proposed CCs against an alternate, theoretical portfolio of resources. (Tr. 1014.) Witness Metz’s recommendation to evaluate a non-existent portfolio should be rejected. The Evaluation Team’s responsibility is to evaluate the specific resources that are submitted in the RFP to support actionable resource decisions, which Witness Metz acknowledges. (Tr. 1180–81.) The Evaluation Team is not responsible for determining or influencing what resources are submitted by market participants or the COP Team. (Tr. 1181.) Rather, the Evaluation Team can only evaluate the resources that are actually submitted by bidders. (Id.) It is not just unnecessary but also impossible to compare RFP resources with theoretical resource options that were not made available for the Company to select through the RFP process. It is, however, in the Company’s interest to ensure that the RFP process results in a portfolio of resources that economically serve customers. This goal is accomplished through a well-designed RFP evaluation that considers the costs and benefits of the different resource types that were submitted through the RFP.
Additionally, the Company itself provided a diverse offering of resources to be considered in the RFP. Despite the Company providing proposals for thermal resources, BESS resources, and BESS + renewable resources, Sierra Club/SACE Witness Metz criticized the evaluation as lacking evidence that the resource selection was based on robust analysis under current market conditions. (Tr. 1012.) Witness Metz specifically cites the lack of Combustion Turbine (“CT”) + Solar resources in the RFP evaluation. (Id.) Even assuming CT + Solar resources were bid into the RFP, these theoretical resources are still not more cost effective than the actual proposed CCs evaluated in the RFP. While not offered by any RFP participants, the Company provided an illustration of the relative cost effectiveness of hypothetical CT + Solar resources to the CC resources selected through the RFP across five different scenarios. (Tr. 1283, Fig. 4.) Only under the single scenario that includes a significant cost on carbon emissions does the hypothetical CT + Solar resource perform more economically than the CC resources. Therefore, Witness Metz’s recommendation to re-evaluate a hypothetical portfolio should be rejected.
The Company reasonably pursued and secured the Supplemental Resources necessary to meet Winter 2027/2028–2030/2031 customer capacity needs.
As demonstrated in the record, Georgia Power sought up to 8,500 MW of capacity resources in the All-Source RFP to meet capacity needs, but the resulting 7,999 MW of diverse BESS, BESS + Solar, and thermal resources was not sufficient to meet all projected capacity needs through 2031. (Tr. 168–69.) Once the Company determined that it would not be able to procure necessary energy and capacity through existing capacity RFPs, the Company sought to identify supplemental resources to meet the remaining needs. (Tr. 169.) The Company first sought to leverage additional System length, uncovered existing resources in the market, and siting BESS resources at existing solar facilities with the goal of minimizing transmission interconnection and upgrade costs. (Tr. 169.) As a result, the Company also requested certification of each of the following resources as Supplemental Resources:
Five BESS PPAs with NextEra Energy Resources (“NEER”) (the “NEER BESS PPAs”), providing a total, nominal capacity of approximately 646 MW of four-hour BESS paired with existing solar facilities with current Georgia Power PPAs;
The 260 MW four-hour Wadley BESS Project paired with an existing solar facility with a current Georgia Power PPA;
A PPA with Tenaska Georgia Partners, L.P. (the “Tenaska Heard County PPA”) for six dual-fuel CTs at the Heard County facility for 930 MW summer capacity and 945 MW winter capacity; and
An amendment to the Company’s current PPA with Mississippi Power Company (“MPC”) for 50 MW of nominal capacity and associated energy to add an additional year to the Company’s current PPA ending on December 31, 2018.
(Tr. 169–70.) The Stipulation recommends approval of the Supplemental Resources pursuant to the RFP exceptions in Commission Rules 515-3-4-.04(3)(f)(3) and 515-3-4-.04(30(f)(6). These Supplemental Resources are cost-effective and necessary resources that represent an extraordinary advantage to customers by providing resources with accelerated commercial operation dates with minimal interconnection and transmission risk. (Tr. 175–77.) Certification of the Supplemental Resources will allow the Company to continue reliably serving capacity needs and forecasted demand through 2031 that is otherwise unmet following the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP and Winter 2027/2028 BESS RFP. 
The Supplemental Resources are an extraordinary advantage for customers.
Commission Rule 515-3-4-.04(3)(f)(3) provides an exception to the requirement to procure resources through an RFP for “[s]upply-side capacity resources of extraordinary advantage that require immediate action.” First, the Supplemental Resources represent such an extraordinary advantage. Despite working in a tight capacity market with limited existing resources, the Company leveraged its established relationships to secure additional capacity at competitive pricing. (Tr. 1262, 1304.) The Supplemental Resources represent a varied combination of additional system length for an extra year, existing thermal resources, and the option to build and purchase power from new BESS resources paired with existing solar resources. (Ex. GPC-3 at 21.) They utilize existing and established interconnections and require minimal transmission upgrades, which enables earlier access to capacity with limited to no transmission costs. (Tr. 1262.).
Second, these resources require immediate action. Simply put, another RFP cannot be conducted fast enough for the Company to develop, procure, or purchase energy and capacity to meet the near-term capacity needs in the 2028-2031 timeframe. (Ex. GPC-3 at 21.) Although Georgia Power conducted two successful RFPs for capacity, both procured fewer resources than were needed to meet the targeted capacity needs. (Tr. 169; Ex. GPC-3 at 37.) With need dates inching closer in the near term, Georgia Power sought to prioritize the resource options that could support speed to market with minimal incremental cost. Each of the Supplemental Resources meets this objective. Further, construction of new resources or additional transmission assets require long lead times, and the timeline to procure such resources through another RFP would extend well past the Company’s resource needs from winter of 2027/2028 through 2030/2031. (Ex. GPC-3 at 21.) Given the urgency of resource needs, the necessary timeframes to otherwise conduct a competitive procurement for alternative resources, and the long lead times for equipment, the Supplemental Resources identified and requested here offer significant value to customers and must be procured now.
Third, the Supplemental Resources are cost effective. The Company evaluated the Supplemental Resources using the same assumptions used in the evaluation of the proposals submitted in the All-Source RFP. (Ex. GPC-3 at 18, 37.) The Supplemental Resources evaluated comparably to the economics of the competitively procured resources. (Tr. 172.) Further, with fewer new construction projects, the Supplemental Resources arguably provide additional value given their ability to minimize interconnection and transmission risk and meet the earlier years of capacity need. (Tr. 171.)
Each of the Supplemental Resources will contribute to the large load customer growth that will benefit customers, provide downward pressure on rates for all customers, continue to support economic development in the state, and allow Georgia to contribute to the nation’s focus on the global importance of artificial intelligence and the digital economy by building the infrastructure to support those efforts. (Tr. 1263.)
Contrary to Intervenor recommendations, the Wadley BESS project offers an extraordinary advantage and is a cost-effective resource that supports near-term capacity needs. 
As proposed, the Wadley BESS project will be built by the Company at a site adjacent to an existing solar facility owned by NEER. (Tr. 175, 567.) By collocating and sizing the BESS resource to match the existing renewable facility, the Company can use the existing interconnection and transmission rights to deliver power from the BESS without adding any interconnection or transmission cost or risk. (Tr. 569.) In addition, the Wadley BESS is the project with the earliest commercial operation date (November 2027) in the Company’s entire certification request, which will help fill the capacity needs unfilled from the Winter 2027/2028 BESS RFP. (Tr. 569, 1247.) No other resource proposed in the All-Source RFP or secured as a Supplemental Resource offers this unique combination of customer value. 
The only justification provided by Intervenors in opposition to the certification of the Wadley project is that it will be owned by Georgia Power. SREA Witness Makhyoun recommended the Wadley project be rejected and the 260 MW be added to the ESS 500 RFP so that the MW could be filled with a competitively bid resource. (Tr. 1216.) Witness Makhyoun misapplies the Commission’s Final Order in the 2001 IRP to support her argument that Company-owned resources should be capped at 70%. Rather than limiting Company ownership, the Commission’s 2001 IRP Order sought to limit the amount of supply side capacity provided through purchased power contracts to 30%, a fact that Witness Makhyoun acknowledged. (Tr. 1236.) Once that limit was reached, the Commission directed the Company to meet its next capacity need through a traditional self-build project to be placed in rate base. (Id.) Contrary to Witness Makhyoun’s assertion, the Company’s ownership of the project should be supported by Commission precedent rather than rejected by it.
Moreover, the Wadley project is a cost-effective resource. On redirect, Witness Makhyoun admitted that the resource was evaluated as being “about average” in terms of cost-effectiveness in the portfolio of resources—its cost to complete was not an outlier or sufficient justification for non-certification. (Tr. 1251). As compared against the other supplemental and All-Source RFP resources, the economics for the Wadley project remained favorable and competitive amongst the alternative resources considered. The Commission should reject Intervenor recommendations to deny certification of the Wadley BESS as unsupported by the evidence and contrary to the public interest.  
Georgia Power’s decision not to rebut every assertion or allegation does not mean that Georgia Power concedes or agrees with those allegations.
It would not be an effective use of the PIA Staff, Intervenors, or this Commission’s time for Georgia Power to rebut every single allegation asserted or implied in the nine testimony filings filed in this case. In the interest of expediency and efficiency in these proceedings, Georgia Power focused its rebuttal and its discussion in this brief on the core issues in dispute in this proceeding rather than devoting unnecessary time and resources to refute positions that are clearly wrong or simply irrelevant to the issues presented in this docket. Moreover, even if Georgia Power had not expressly reserved this position, the Commission, PIA Staff, and Intervenors could not reasonably conclude that something was agreed to by the Company merely because it was not specifically addressed during rebuttal.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons set forth above, and based on the record in this proceeding, the Commission should approve Georgia Power Company’s All-Source and Supplemental Resources Certification Applications, as modified by the Stipulation.

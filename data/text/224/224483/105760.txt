Q.Please summarize the purpose of your testimony.
A.This testimony provides Staff’s evaluation of the Budget 2026 load forecast (“B2026”) produced by the Georgia Power Company (“Georgia Power” or “Company”) for the 2029-2031 All-Source Request for Proposals (“RFP”) Certification Proceeding. Summary of Findings, Conclusions, and Recommendations
Q.please summarize your Findings and conclusions.
A.To date, the large load forecast and the underlying Load Realization Model (“LRM”) have not materialized as expected. In the near term, large load projects continue to materialize lower than forecasted by the Company. This lower materialization is driven primarily by the Data Center segment via diminished load materialization and project cancellations. Absent further adjustments to the underlying assumptions in the LRM to account for these factors, the Company is likely to overestimate its load and over-procure capacity resources, which would result in unnecessary higher costs to existing ratepayers.
Given these conclusions, Staff recommends that the Commission adopt Staff’s Scenario 1 load forecast described below. This scenario minimizes the amount of risk that existing ratepayers bear by limiting the large load forecast to only projects with executed contracts. While there is still risk associated with this scenario, as nearly XX% of the load in this scenario comes from projects that are not subject to the Company’s new, modified rules and regulations approved by the Commission, it offers a reasonable mitigation of this risk relative to the Company’s filing.
If the Commission determines that Scenario 1 is not its preferred option, Staff identified an alternative scenario. Scenario 2 provides a higher level of large load (data center) growth, but moderates this growth based on a number of modifications to the underlying assumptions in the Company’s LRM. This scenario provides a higher level of risk relative to Scenario 1, but offers a significantly lower level of risk than that presented in the Company’s B2026 base case.

Q. WHAT PORTION OF THE COMPANY’S CERTIFICATION REQUEST IS BACKED BY EXECUTED CONTRACTS? 
A. The majority of the new generation Georgia Power seeks to certify—approximately X %—is not backed by executed contracts under the new large load framework. Only about 1,900 MW is supported by such contracts. The rest is speculative and exposes customers to the risk of stranded costs if the anticipated load does not materialize.
Q. DOES THE FRAMEWORK FOR NEW LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS APPLY TO ALL LARGE LOAD FOR WHICH GEORGIA POWER IS REQUESTING TO SERVE? 
A. Unfortunately, no. The framework and customer protections in it apply to only a small fraction of the proposed capacity. The majority of the new large load contracts that the Company is planning for were signed in 2023 and 2024, before the new rules and regulations went into effect. We refer to these as “legacy contracts.”  In addition to the contracts under the new rules and regulations and the legacy contracts, the Company is also planning to serve speculative new large load customers that have not yet, and may never, actually sign contracts and receive electric service.
Q. WHY ARE THESE DISTINCTIONS IMPORTANT FOR CUSTOMER PROTECTION?
A. Legacy large load contracts do not include the same termination penalties or minimum bill provisions as the new contracts. These are also shorter-term contracts and allow customers to leave the system without penalty. If these customers reduce or terminate their load, this could result in underutilized assets or stranded assets. Georgia Power’s revenues could fall short, leaving a potentially significant amount of costs to be covered by remaining ratepayers. 
Building capacity for potential large load customers that have not yet, and may never, sign a contract or receive electric service from Georgia Power is even more speculative and more problematic. There is no way to collect costs from a potential customer if it never signed a contract and never materialized.
Q. WHAT HAPPENS IF THE COMMISSION CERTIFIES THESE PROJECTS WITHOUT RESTRICTIONS?
A. If the Commission grants certification as requested, and load does not materialize as projected by the Company, the ability to disallow future cost recovery will be limited. While we are not attorneys, our understanding from Staff attorneys is that resources that have been certified cannot be disallowed on the basis of reasonableness unless the project exceeds the certified cost. While some prudence disallowances are still available, such disallowances would be limited to whether a project was executed competently, even if it turns out the project was never needed.
Q. CAN YOU GIVE AN EXAMPLE OF HOW THIS COULD HARM CUSTOMERS?
A. Yes. Suppose the Commission certifies the projects based on the Company’s forecast, and Georgia Power begins construction. If the market shifts or the Company’s forecasted large load does not materialize, the Company will still seek to recover those costs. Without executed contracts under the new large load framework, there is no guarantee those costs will not be passed on to existing customers. The Commission’s ability to prevent this impact to existing ratepayers is severely constrained once certification is granted.
Q. HAS GEORGIA POWER COMMITTED TO SHIELDING EXISTING CUSTOMERS FROM THIS RISK?
A. No. It is Staff’s understanding that Georgia Power has not guaranteed that costs associated with unmaterialized load would not be passed on to existing customers. Though asked multiple times in hearing, the Company has either not explicitly made this commitment, or objected to a response.
In hearing, the Company noted that the new rules and regulations approved by the Commission, which include the option to assess minimum monthly bills against large load customers, provide a “higher level of protection” for non-large load customers by ensuring a certain level of cost recovery. However, load growth not covered under these new rules and regulations, whether under an older contract structure or lacking a contract at all, would provide lesser protections.
Q. WHAT IS STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE COMPANY’S CERTIFICATION REQUESTS?
A. Staff recommends that the Commission only certify generation, and authorize associated transmission investments, which are backed by executed contracts, whether under the new large load rules or those executed prior to the implementation of these rules (“legacy contracts”). This ensures that customers are protected from speculative investment risk.
Q. ARE THERE ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES IF THE COMMISSION WANTS TO PRESERVE FLEXIBILITY?
A. Yes. If the Commission wishes to allow certification beyond contracted load, it should do so only if Georgia Power agrees to two conditions: 
First, Georgia Power will not seek cost recovery until the associated load materializes. 
Second, the Company will not recover financing costs incurred during any delay in construction or incremental capital cost due to the delay from a lack of signed contracts. 
This approach allows the Company to pursue opportunities while ensuring it bears the risk and protects Georgia Power’s other customers if those opportunities do not materialize.

Summary of Company Load Forecast 
Q.Please summarize the company’s filed load forecast.
A.From 2025 through 2031, the Company’s peak load forecast is projected to grow at 7.7% compounded annually, reaching 26,979 MW in the Summer and 25,065 MW in the Winter, as shown in Figure 1 below. This growth represents a 56% increase relative to the forecasted peak in 2025.
In 2024, the Company reported an actual summer peak of 16,555 MW and winter peak of 16,458 MW. The forecasted summer peak for 2025 is 17,288 MW, representing a 4.4% increase relative to 2024 actuals. The forecasted winter peak for 2025 is 16,063 MW, representing a 2.4% decrease relative to 2024 actuals. 
The forecast period extends through 2045 and the compound annual growth rate moderates down to approximately 3% for the entire forecast period, 2025 through 2045. In 2045, projected growth represents more than an 80% increase relative to 2025 forecasted peak.
Figure 1: B2026 Peak Forecast Summary

Q.Please summarize the methodology underpinning the company’s Load forecast.
A.The Company’s load forecast consists of two core pieces: the historically informed “organic” load forecast and the probabilistic large load forecast. The organic load forecast uses multiple techniques to produce a customer class-based energy forecast which is then converted to a peak load forecast. The large load forecast uses a probabilistic model, the LRM, to produce external adjustments to both the energy and peak load forecasts to account for Commercial projects greater than 115 megawatts (“MW”) and Industrial projects greater than 45 MW. Additionally, the Company makes several other adjustments to its energy and peak load forecasts to account for growth in electric vehicles, Behind-The-Meter (“BTM”) solar, Demand-Side Management (“DSM”), and industrial cogeneration. 
Q.Has the company’s load forecast changed since the 2025 irp proceeding?
A.Yes. While the structure of the load forecast is generally similar to the forecast submitted in the 2025 IRP proceeding, the Company has updated both the Organic and Large Load forecasts with the most recent available information. The direction and magnitude of these changes vary depending on the specific customer class. Further details are provided in Section IV below.

The Company explains that the large load forecast is growing “slower than the previous version due to more stringent risk adjustment assumptions.” The Company is referring to the rubric introduced in the Company’s Rebuttal testimony during the 2025 IRP Proceeding, which provides a framework to assign probabilities to the likelihood of a project signing a contract with the Company (“P2”) within the LRM. Prior to this rubric, the Company did not have a clear framework to assign these P2 values. This modification resulted in a reduction to the large load forecast overall.
Reductions to the Large Load forecast were also driven by a new classification system for Data Center projects specifically, which differentiated the likelihood of load materialization based on the type of customer. This system, also introduced in the Company’s Rebuttal testimony during the prior IRP proceeding, assumes that prospective customers with demonstrated experience developing Data Centers (“Hyperscalers”) should be expected to materialize at a higher percentage of their announced load compared to those who have less experience or do not have secured tenants for their Data Centers (“Developers”). These changes to the Large Load forecast specifically reduced the Large Commercial forecast. 
The Organic Industrial forecast continues to decrease, growing at 1.21% CAGR in the B2026 forecast, compared to 1.7% in the February 2025 Sensitivity and 2.2% in the B2025 Forecast. Reductions in Organic Industrial sales are driven primarily by changes to the Industrial Production Index, a key independent variable in the econometric model.
The Organic Commercial forecast decreased in the near-term through 2028 while increasing slightly in the years beyond. Long-term growth is driven by an increase in the energy intensity of customers, particularly driven by growth in the size of data center customers.
The organic B2026 Residential forecast has shifted downward, growing at 1.12% CAGR, compared to the 1.28% projects in the B2025 and February 2025 forecasts. The Company explains that “[a]most all the forecasted change in residential sales is due to slower growth in the Average Use forecast…[The Customer forecast is]  projected to have the same average growth rate…” This suggests that customers are growing as expected, but these customers are using less electricity per capita. It is not immediately clear whether this is driven by increased energy efficiency across residential appliances, customer reactions to changes in power prices, or other changes in customer behavior.

Q.Please summarize your conclusions regarding the near-term reductions IN the company’s load forecast.
A.The Company’s near-term forecast shows a consistent decrease in each latest forecast vintage. If the Company continues to reduce its near-term forecast with every iteration, the Company would be regularly over-estimating its load forecast, resulting in an over procurement of resources. This over-estimation is driven primarily by the large load forecast, particularly the Large Data Center class. 
If this trend continues in the future, the likelihood of the Company over-procuring resources increases. If minimum billing terms and other protections from new rules and regulations that are placed in the contracts of large load customers do not sufficiently protect against this risk, existing customers are likely to bear the cost of over-procurement. As mentioned above, only a fraction of the generation requested in the Certification filing is backed by contracts under the Company’s new rules and regulations.
The LRM continues to materialize significantly lower than forecasted by the Company.

A.The Data Center segment is primarily underperforming expectations due to a mixture of lower materialization rates, project cancellations, and delays. Lower materialization is the primary driver of this underperformance, referring to operating projects using lower levels of peak demand (MW) than forecasted in a given year along their submitted load ramp. 
As demonstrated in Table 7, XX% of the Data Center load that was expected in the B2026 model, accounting for project cancellations and delays as of August 2025, failed to materialize. This suggests that, while Data Center projects are providing load ramps for every forecast year, projects in operation are failing to materialize at the level expected. This does not represent a delay in the in-service date, but specifically a lower level of load materialization. 
Of the 53 MW of cancelled/delayed projects for 2025, all of them are Data Centers. As such, in addition to the diminished materialization of operating Data Centers, this segment is also demonstrating a unique tendency to be cancelled or delayed. This behavior illustrates the speculative nature of the data center industry.

As such, 24% of Data Center projects that entered the LRM have been removed with an average of 5 Data Center projects exiting the pipeline each quarter. Load flexibility and contract revisions may further reduce the Company forecast
Q.Please explain how load flexibility may further impact the Company’s large load forecast.
A.Load flexibility and curtailments during peak hours may significantly mitigate the peak impact of large load customers, particularly for Data Center projects. A recent study by Tyler H. Norris, Tim Profeta, Dalia Patino-Echeverri, Adam Cowie-Haskell at Duke University suggests that a 1% curtailment could enable load addition equivalent to 20% of peak load. For the Southern Company Balancing Authority, the report estimated 6,400 MW of load growth enabled with 0.25% curtailment. 
The application of large load flexibility is beginning to deploy on the market. A 96 MW data center in Virginia, scheduled to open in 2026, is committed to providing flexible operation via the Electric Power Research Institute’s (“EPRI”) “DC Flex” initiative. Acknowledging that the Company also participates in the EPRI DC Flex initiative, via Southern Company, the Company may have further understanding of how load flexibility could enable large load growth in Georgia that is not currently available on the record.
A load flexibility program sponsored by the Company could enable significant growth of large load customers while mitigating the peak impacts of this growth, providing a more cost-efficient means of facilitating economic development in Georgia. Such a program could be administered through existing demand response provisions, new terms specifically for large load customers via the new commission approved rules and regulations, or some other pathways.
Q.Has the Company considered load flexibility in its large load forecast?
A.Staff’s understanding is that the Company has not considered the impact of load flexibility on its load forecast. Without considering load flexibility programs that may be available to large loads in the future, the Company may be overestimating large load growth and the amount of capacity needed to serve these large load customers. The Company explains that it has “held discussions on the … potential impact load flexibility could have with prospective large load customers.” However, the Company has not clearly demonstrated that this has been considered directly in its load forecast.
Q.Please explain how LOAD REDUCTIONS may impact the Company’s large load forecast.
A.The opportunity for load reductions could significantly reduce the demand from large load customers, resulting in diminished materialization of the large load forecast. If multiple large load projects exercise this option, the Company’s large load forecast may materialize lower than forecasted.
The Company allows customers who are 100 MW or greater, following the execution of a contract, a one-time option to reduce their peak load during “a specified period,” modifying the projects load forecast into the future. When asked how the Company accounts for this load reduction risk in the LRM, the Company did not directly respond to the question beyond explaining that load forecasts would be modified as load ramps are modified.
Multiple factors justify an adjustment to the B2026 load forecast
Q.Do you have any concerns that warrant adjustments to the Company’s load forecast?
A.Yes. Based on our discussion above, the following concerns are clear:
Changes in the B2026 forecast, relative to the 2025 IRP proceeding forecast, are primarily driven by the LRM,
The LRM continues to be revised downward in the near-term, 
The LRM is materializing lower than forecasted by the Company,
The LRM is increasingly inaccurate at longer time horizons, tending to overestimate load in greater magnitude,
This overestimation is primarily driven by Data Center projects,  
Uncertainty in the Data Center pipeline is driven primarily by projects materializing lower than expected and project removals, and
Adjustments to the LRM are necessary to better reflect the actual performance of the large load pipeline, as evidenced by the quarterly large load economic development reports and the actual operation of large load projects on the system.
Furthermore, the Company’s LRM exhibits clear errors in the load ramps of certain projects. Considering these concerns holistically, the B2026 exhibits an overestimation bias driven by the LRM, requiring an adjustment of the model to account for both identified errors, as well as the factors that might be leading to the overestimation of large load in the LRM. 
As such, the LRM represents a large source of uncertainty for the Company’s load forecast. This uncertainty is primarily driven by Data Center customers which are not materializing as projected. The LRM requires proactive adjustment to reflect the actual materialization of these projects.

Q.Which scenario is staff’s preferred scenario?
A.We recommend Staff Scenario 1. The biggest risk to non-large load customers in this proceeding is the Company spending money on capacity for speculative load. If the Data Center load does not materialize on time or does not materialize at all, non-large load customers would become the backstop and burdened by the costs of the unnecessary capacity. 
Scenario 1 restricts the LRM to projects with signed Contracts for Electric Service, which makes it the option with the least risk to existing customers. Under this scenario, the Company would not start spending money on new capacity until it had a contract to back that capacity. Scenario 1 would reduce the capacity cost of the Company’s proposal by approximately $17.5 billion. Given the restriction to projects with contracts only, Staff does not propose any adjustments to the underlying assumptions in the LRM in this scenario.
Q.IS scenario 1 rISK fREE FOR EXISTING CUSTOMERS?
A.No. Even Scenario 1 is not risk free to existing customers. Scenario 1 includes both contracts under the new rules and regulations as well as legacy large load contracts which were signed before 2025. While the large load contracts under the new rules are designed to protect existing customers from bearing any of the costs of adding new large customers, the majority of the load under contract are legacy contracts, which offer far fewer protections. 
As shown in Figure 10, only ~21% of the requested load increase is covered under the new rules and regulations approved by the Commission, while the remaining 79% offer far fewer protections to existing ratepayers. Many of the legacy contracts contributing to load in 2031 are already covered by prior procurements. The remaining uncovered legacy contracts have shorter terms and lack the minimum bill, security, and termination payment provisions of the new contracts, showing a higher level of risk to existing ratepayers. Most of the Company’s capacity expansion request consists of entirely speculative load growth that is not covered by any contracts, representing nearly 70% of the request.

Q.Why would the Commission consider adopting scenario 2?
A.The Company views the new Data Center market as an opportunity for economic development. However, it is an opportunity that comes with risk. The Commission’s decision to select Scenario 2 depends on its tolerance for more uncertainty in the materialization of the LRM (and more risk to existing customers) by including more speculative or otherwise uncertain projects. 
Q.How does increasing the amount of capacity the company is certified to build increase the risk to existing customers?
A.When the Commission certifies new generation, the Company begins incurring costs to build or otherwise acquire it. As more speculative new generation is built and more costs are incurred, there is a risk that the large load customers needed to cover those costs will not materialize. If the large load customers do not materialize, the Company may still seek recovery of the costs incurred for the excess capacity (or for the abandoned or delayed projects) from other customers, as they have done in this proceeding, with a request to recover development costs of projects that are not approved by the Commission from ratepayers. Depending on the circumstances, the Commission’s ability to deny such a request may be limited. 
Q.DO PROTECTIONS BUILT INTO NEW LARGE LOAD CONTRACTS FULLY PROTECT OTHER CUSTOMER CLASSES FROM RISK ASSOCIATED WITH LARGE LOAD NOT MATERIALIZING?
A.No. It could turn out that the Company is not able to fully mitigate costs associated with load materializing below their forecast, with risk still present beyond the protections offered by the new rules and regulations. Many potential mitigation measures will present associated costs, especially if the Company intends to cancel or delay a project for which they have already incurred significant costs. 
Staff understands the mitigation strategies available to the Company to include the following:
Electing not to replace or renew PPAs after expiration,
Cancelling capital spending on Company-owned proposals, and
Adding new large load contracts to replace any early terminations or diminished materialization. 
Each of these strategies may present associated costs. The PPA expiration strategy requires the Company to continue any charges and fees associated with the contract for its duration. Cancellation of capital spending will present costs associated with any construction completed up to the point of cancellation, referring to the recovery of CWIP and any financing costs that were prudently incurred. Finally, the addition of new contracts would require ratepayers to pay for any excess capacity costs until the point of load actually materializing.
As such, the Company is planning to employ mitigation measures for both contracts that do not materialize, as well as those that terminate early. At least in regard to contracts that do not materialize, the Commission’s ability to disallow any of these costs is limited to situations of imprudence, fraud, concealment, or failure to disclose a material fact. Even for contracts that terminate early, the Company still retains the ability to seek recovery from existing customers. In other words, the Company’s mitigation is limited and may not be sufficient to protect ratepayers if multiple large load customers terminate early. Potentially, every single forecasted Data Center MW could need to be mitigated.

Conclusions & Recommendations
Q.Please summarize your conclusions.
A.The Company’s LRM is based on speculative assumptions, creating unreasonable risk to existing ratepayers if forecasted load from data centers does not materialize. To date, the large load forecast and the underlying LRM have not materialized as expected, underscoring the speculative nature of the model’s underlying assumptions. In the near term, large load projects continue to materialize lower than forecasted by the Company. This underperformance is driven primarily by the Data Center segment via diminished load materialization and project cancellations. Absent further adjustments to the underlying assumptions in the LRM to account for these factors, the Company is likely to overestimate its load and over-procure capacity resources, resulting in greater risk and potentially higher costs to existing ratepayers. The most reasonable approach to forecasting large load is to limit predictions to those customers who have executed contracts for electric service with the Company.
In addition to modifying the Company’s load forecast, there are a number of uncertainties that may further mitigate the peak impact of large load projects. The most significant of these uncertainties is the impact of load flexibility. The Company has not demonstrated how load flexibility may impact its large load forecast. Further exploration of how load flexibility could impact peak demand at various curtailments level and the pathways through which such a program could be administered is necessary to promote the cost efficient integration of large load customers.
Q.please summarize your recommendations.
A.Given the discussion and conclusions above, Staff provides the following recommendations:
Staff recommends the Commission require the Company to adopt the adjustments and model modification provided in Staff Scenario 1. This scenario minimizes the amount of risk that existing ratepayers bear by limiting the large load forecast to only projects with executed contracts. While there is still risk associated with this scenario, as most (nearly 80%) of the load in this scenario comes from projects that are not subject to the new rules and regulations approved by the Commission, it offers a reasonable mitigation of this risk relative to the Company’s filing.
If the Commission determines that Scenario 1 is not its preferred option, Staff has provided Scenario 2 to the Commission as a lower-risk alternative to Georgia Power’s request. Scenario 2 provides a higher level of large load growth, but moderates this growth based on a number of modifications to the underlying assumptions in the LRM. This scenario provides a higher level of risk relative to Scenario 1, but offers a significantly lower level of risk than that presented in the Company’s B2026 base case.
Staff recommends the Commission to require the Company to provide a report by February 15, 2026, proposing a structure, potential administrative pathway, and estimated system impact of a load flexibility program in this docket by February 15, 2026. The report should include the following:
Program Structure: A description of the load flexibility program including the types of customers eligible for the offering, the extent of curtailments that could be applied to these customers, and how prospective customers would be incentivized to participate in this offering. Incentives could include a variety of mechanisms including priority interconnection.
Administrative Pathway: The mechanism by which the Company will offer the load flexibility offering to large load customers. Staff recognizes that the new rules and regulations approved by the Commission allow the Company to require a wide variety of terms and conditions upon large load customers. This pathway offers the Company a means to develop a load flexibility offering rapidly. However, Staff recognizes that the optimal pathway may vary. As such, the Company should explain the various administrative pathways available for a large load flexibility offering, and any costs or benefits associated with these pathways.
Estimated System Impact: The capacity headroom enabled by a load flexibility program and any resulting cost savings. The system impact analysis should consider the level of curtailment, the number of curtailable hours per year, and the types of customers requested to curtail, producing an analysis of how many additional large load projects could be enabled without procuring additional capacity resources.

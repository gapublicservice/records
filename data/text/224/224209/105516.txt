STF-PIA-9-12

Question: 

Referring to the Direct Testimony of Francisco Valle, p. 5, line 4, the Company is requesting 9,900 MW of capacity resources to meet increasing customer needs. Please explain and differentiate between portion of this additional capacity need is driven by unit retirements, organic load growth, or large load growth. For the portion of capacity need driven by large load growth, please also differentiate between load that is under a executed contract with new terms pursuant to new Commission rules and regulations, load that is under a executed contract without new terms, and load that is not under an executed contract.

Response:

The referenced 9,900 MW capacity resource request is driven mainly by load growth and the expiration of existing PPAs. There are no unit retirements supporting the 9,900 MW request. There are 3,347 MW of PPAs set to expire by 2030. Of the portion driven by load growth, large loads are a driving force, accounting for approximately 90% of the projected load growth from 2026-2031. The Company currently has 4,600 MW of contracted loads included in the Budget 2026 (“B2026”) Load and Energy Forecast that were executed prior to the revisions to the Company’s rules and regulations. For additional information on contracted loads included in the Company’s B2026 Load and Energy Forecast, please refer to the Company’s Q2 Large Load Economic Development Report. Since filing the B2026 Load and Energy Forecast on September 17, 2025, the Company has filed for Commission review three large load contracts containing terms consistent with the new rules and regulations. Collectively, these three contracts total almost 1,900 MW. The additional resources cannot be directly differentiated to the items referenced in the question as there are other impacts to capacity needs such as prior approved resources, capacity equivalence values, planning reserve margin levels leading into the time period in question, and changes in resource ratings.

Please explain how Georgia Power determines when to classify a prospective customer as “on-hold”. 
Please provide examples of the events that would lead Georgia Power to classify a prospective customer as “on-hold”.

Response:
The Company classifies a customer as “on-hold” either when the customer indicates they have encountered difficulties advancing through the project planning phases or the customer ceases to provide Georgia Power with sufficient and timely project information necessary to evaluate its viability.
Examples of events that would lead to a prospective customer being classified as “on-hold” include, but are not limited to, a customer encountering an issue finalizing funding for a project, having persistent problems securing permits, or failing to provide requested information in a timely manner.

If an updated analysis has been performed, please provide all supporting workbooks, with formulas and links intact, used to develop that analysis.

Response:
Although the Company has not changed the value modeled for the P3 probability, the Company has updated its analysis supporting the value modeled for the P3 probability in the Load Realization Model since its introduction in the 2023 IRP Update. The Company now uses a historical default rate analysis provided by Moody’s Ratings, which investigates the historical default rate of Baa3 rated companies over time. Such analysis indicates that of Baa3 rate companies, 2.3% have defaulted within five years. The Company has conservatively chosen to maintain the original assumption for P3. Because the P3 probability measures projects’ ability to remain financially viable such that commercial operations can be achieved, this analysis strongly supports the continued use of the Company’s P3 assumption.

===== STF-PIA-9 PUBLIC DISCLOSURE/STF-PIA-9-24.docx =====
STF-PIA-9-24

Question: 
Referring to the B2026 Load and Energy Forecast, Section 1.5.1.5, please explain how Georgia Power developed the probabilities assigned to each of the following criteria and milestones:
Projects on hold
Projects that have not completed a Transmission Study and are not on hold
Projects that have completed a Transmission Study but have yet to complete Timeline Review
Projects that have completed Timeline Review but have yet to submit an RFS
If there are supporting analyses or calculations Georgia Power used to develop the probabilities, please provide the analyses.

Response:
The Company’s P2 probability within the Load Realization Model is meant to measure the probability of prospective customers signing a Contract for Electric Service (“CES”), with 0% probability defined as the customer being removed from the pipeline and 100% defined as the customer having signed a CES. The Company uses its internal review process as a means of characterizing the P2 probability into the following buckets: very low likelihood, low likelihood, medium likelihood, high likelihood, and very high likelihood. 
The percentages behind the low, medium, and high likelihood buckets are standard thresholds used in risk classification, determined by measuring the proportionate points between 0% and 100%. From there, the percentage behind the very low bucket was determined by identifying an extreme point between 0% and low likelihood, reflective of the speculative nature of on-hold projects signing a CES. Similarly, the very high bucket was determined by identifying a point between high likelihood and 100%, reflective of the promising nature of projects having signed a Request for Electric Service (“RFS”) converting into signing a CES.

Question: 
Referring to the Company’s Budget 2026 Load and Energy Forecast report, pp. 6-7, Figures 1.1-1 & 1.1-2, the Company shows that the Peak forecasts for years 2026 and 2027 have decreased from the B2025 forecast to the B2026 Forecast. Please respond to the following questions:
To what extent are the reductions in forecasted peak load driven by reductions in the organic load forecast as opposed to the large load forecast? 
For reductions driven by the large load forecast, please explain if the primary drivers for load reductions are project cancellations, project delays, or diminished load materialization? 

Response:
For the summer peak forecast (Figure 1.1-1), the large load forecast reductions account for 81% of the total reductions for 2026 and 78% for 2027, while the organic load forecast reductions account for the rest. For the winter peak forecast (Figure 1.1-2), the large load forecast reductions account for all the reductions; the organic forecast stays the same (less than 0.01% increase) and therefore did not drive any reduction in forecasted peak load. 

Reductions driven by the large load forecast in 2026 and 2027 are mainly related to the Company’s changes to the Load Realization Model (“LRM”) methodology introduced in the Company’s 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) Rebuttal Testimony in Docket No. 56002. These changes result in a more disciplined, stringent risk adjustment, thus driving decreases to the LRM output and subsequent adjustment to peak demand. See pages 9-10 of Francisco Valle’s Direct Testimony, filed September 17, 2025, where these adjustments are described in more detail. The MW in the Company’s Budget 2026 (“B2026”) Large Load Pipeline in years 2026 and 2027 are greater than or equal to those supporting the Budget 2025 (“B2025”) Forecast. As such, decreases in the forecasted peak demand in those years are driven neither by project cancellations nor project delays. After 2030, the Company’s B2026 Large Load Forecast surpasses B2025, as the magnitude of Large Load Pipeline increases more than offset the effects of the changes the Company implemented to the LRM.

Question:
Referring to the Company’s Budget 2026 Load and Energy Forecast report, p. 18, please explain why the Company uses the period from 1980-2024 to define “normal” weather. Explain why this is appropriate compared to a more recent period of time.

Response:
The Company supports a weather-normal definition derived from historical data spanning 1980 through 2024. This provides a more stable and consistent weather-normal definition across budget and planning cycles.

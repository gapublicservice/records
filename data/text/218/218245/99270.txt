                                       INTRODUCTION

       The testimony and evidence presented to the Georgia Public Service Commission

(“Commission”) over the course of these proceedings demonstrate that Georgia Power

Company’s 2023 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) Update should not be approved as filed. The

Commission should also not adopt the stipulation that Georgia Power Company and the Public

Interest Advocacy (PIA) Staff submitted on March 27, 2024 (the “Stipulation”). Instead, the

Commission should:

       [1] Deny approval of the power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) with Mississippi Power

Company (“MPC”) and Santa Rosa Energy Center (“Santa Rosa”) before the winter of

2025/2026. If the Commission decides to approve the PPAs, it should disallow inclusion in retail

rates of all PPA costs in the months prior to when Georgia Power Company needs the capacity to

serve customers.

       [2] Deny Georgia Power Company’s proposal to build three combustion turbines (“CTs”)

at the Yates site. If the Commission decides to approve the CTs, it should implement sufficient

guardrails to prevent fuel costs from being entirely passed on to ratepayers.

          [3] Direct Georgia Power Company to stay committed to retiring Plant Bowen Units 1-2

and Scherer Unit 3 because it saves ratepayer money.

          [4] Deny Georgia Power Company’s proposed FlexCapacity framework or, alternatively,

allow it only to deploy battery energy storage systems (“BESS”), which are an economic

available option according to Georgia Power’s own Resource Mix Study.


                                                ARGUMENT

     I.   Legal Standard

          The Rules of the Commission require that an IRP be “in the public interest,” and, if it is

not, that the Commission reject the utility’s resource plan or provide an alternate plan. See

Commission Rule 515-3-4-.01(2); 515-3-4-.06. An IRP that fails to be in the public interest

should therefore not be approved by the Commission.

PPAs start well before there is an actual need in Georgia Power’s system2, and if the Commission

       Like the MPC PPA, the Santa Rosa PPA also starts well before there is a need in Georgia

transmission capacity for the PPA from 2024 to May 2026. Later, in its rebuttal testimony,

Georgia Power indicated that it was able to “secure firm transmission on the Southern

remainder of term of the agreement,” but has not secured it. GPC Rebuttal Testimony of Grubb

       As Sierra Club’s witnesses Glick and Metz explained in their testimony, “there is no

customers.


III.   The proposed CTs are expensive, polluting, and not in the best interest of customers

       Contrary to the IRP Update request and the proposed Stipulation, the Commission should

follow PIA Staff’s original recommendation and deny approval of the buildout of three CTs at

the Plant Yates site (“Staff recommends that the Commission deny the Company’s request for

certification of the Yates [CTs]. The Yates capacity is not required until 2029 and should be bid

into the 2029 – 2031 capacity RFP to determine whether this capacity is least-cost and the most


                                                7

reliable compared to other options.” PIA Staff Direct Testimony of Newsome, Hayet and

Wellborn at 8). As explained below and demonstrated over the course of these proceedings, the

proposed Yates CTs are not the most economic option for ratepayers, do not have firm gas supply

available, and are therefore not in the best interest of Georgia Power customers.

   1. It is more economic for GPC to fill its firm capacity need with BESS than with three CTs

       Instead of building three CTs, it is more economic for GPC to fill its firm capacity needs

with BESS, both paired with solar PV and standalone. As Sierra Club witnesses Glick and Metz

explained in their direct testimony, the Commission should not approve the Yates CTs because

“it would be more economic to replace the firm capacity of one or more of the CTs with 4-hour

BESS, which could be charged at low cost with energy from solar PV as well as the grid”. Sierra

Club Direct Testimony of Glick and Metz at 26. Comparing just fixed and capital costs

(including financing, taxes, insurance, and tax credits), witnesses Glick and Metz found that

BESS costs less than the CTs. See id. at 33. Therefore, instead of approving Georgia Power’s

proposal, the Commission should “direct GPC to test the market and procure as much battery

storage (paired with solar PV and standalone) as it can.” Id. at 7.

       The advantages of BESS over the proposed CTs are numerous. For instance, “BESS

would provide the same dispatchable firm capacity value as a gas CT, and 4-hour BESS are

readily available and cost-competitive today”. Id. at 29. Additionally, “GPC’s system has ample

capability to add 4-hour storage before its capacity contribution will begin to decline (based on

GPC’s own effective load carrying capability (ELCC) assumptions).” Id. at 30. In fact, GPC’s

plans to build three CTs does not follow its own modeling, which shows that “it would be

economic to procure” 3.4 GW of BESS by 2030—over three times more BESS than GPC’s 1

GW proposal—“in the moderate fuel price scenario[,] and between 3.1 and 4.4 GW in the other


                                                  8

scenarios”. Id. at 38. Similarly, the “moderate fuel scenario in the Resource Mix Study shows

that it would be economic for GPC to procure 1.5 GW of new solar by 2030, which is over seven

times the capacity in the 2023 IRP Update”. Id. at 39.

       As GPC acknowledged in direct testimony, “[t]he time to construct BESS is shorter than

other types of generation and, therefore, can be more quickly deployed to help meet the earlier

capacity needs identified”. GPC Direct Testimony of Grubb et al. at 37. GPC again recognized

that it is capable of procuring BESS faster than building the CTs in its January supplemental

filing. (“GPC itself thinks that it can procure battery storage in time for the winter of 2026/2027,

and therefore should be more ambitious about constructing storage and solar instead of building

all three gas CTs.” Sierra Club Direct Testimony of Glick and Metz at 33). Building BESS

instead of CTs brings benefits to Georgia Power’s system and ratepayers and, during cross

       2. The Company and ratepayers would benefit from including Section 111 Rules in GPC’s

           modeling

           As with the ELG rule, the greenhouse gas regulations proposed under Section 111 of the

Clean Air Act (“Section 111 Rules”) will increase costs to continue operating the Company’s

coal plants beyond 2031: these costs “could be substantially higher, and the plants’ operation

limited, relative to what GPC projected in its 2023 Resource Mix Study.” Sierra Club Direct

Testimony of Glick and Metz at 6. Section 111 Rules will apply to many of Georgia Power’s new

and existing resources. Compliance pathways differ based on (1) whether the unit is coal or gas;

(2) whether the unit is existing or new; (3) how much the unit runs; and (4) when the unit is

scheduled to retire. Section 111 Rules would reduce operations of new gas and existing coal

plants and/or require major investments in or carbon capture and sequestration (“CCS”)

conversion and hydrogen co-firing capability.

           Because Bowen and Scherer 1 and 2 coal plants are currently scheduled to retire in

2035,13 these units “will need to be retrofitted to co-fire with natural gas starting in 2030”. Sierra

Club Direct Testimony of Glick and Metz at 52. But, as witnesses Glick and Metz explain, “if the

retirement dates were shifted earlier, to 2032, they do not need to do anything to comply with the

proposed Section 111 Rules. This would allow them to avoid costly investments for conversion

to co-fire on gas.” Id. Further, Scherer Unit 3 and Gaston Units 1-4 “will not be required to take

 V.    The PSC should not approve the proposed FlexCapacity framework or, if the PSC

       approves it, it should only be used to procure BESS.

       Sierra Club agrees with paragraph 18 of the proposed Stipulation between PIA Staff and

the Company, which states that “[t]he Flex Capacity framework filed in the 2023 IRP Update is

not approved”. Stipulation at 4. However, if the Commission decides to approve the Flex

Capacity framework, it should only be used to procure BESS. This is in alignment both with

Sierra Club’s witnesses recommendations (“The Commission should only allow GPC to use the

Flex Capacity framework to deploy BESS given that the Company’s resource plan that it was

economic to deploy substantially more BESS than the Company current plans to bring online,”

Sierra Club Direct Testimony of Glick and Metz at 6-7), and with what the Company is already

planning to use the Flex Capacity framework for:

              Q What types of assets are you thinking about acquiring to fill this
              emergent need under the flex capacity framework; like a PPA, for
              example?
              A (Witness Grubb) It would most likely be energy storage systems
              because we [] could build those a little quicker.

Tr. 0518:11-0519:12. Additionally, as witnesses Glick and Metz noted in their testimony, it is

“would be reasonable for GPC to use this framework to have its build-plans most closely match

with the results of its resource planning analysis.” Sierra Club Direct Testimony of Glick and

Metz at 37. Therefore, should the Commission approve the Flex Capacity framework, it should

direct Georgia Power to implement it only to deploy BESS, an economically available option

according to the Company’s Resource Mix Study.

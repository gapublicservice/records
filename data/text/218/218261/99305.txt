POST-HEARING BRIEF OF GEORGIA POWER COMPANY 
INTRODUCTION
This matter pending before the Georgia Public Service Commission (the “Commission”) is Georgia Power Company’s (“Georgia Power” or the “Company”) 2023 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”) Update (“IRP Update”). The Company filed its application seeking approval of the IRP Update on October 27, 2023, pursuant to the planning process established by the Integrated Resource Planning Act of 1991 (the “IRP Act”) as well as Commission Rule 515-3-4-.06(5). That Commission rule requires the Company to submit an amendment to its IRP if the Company anticipates submitting an application for a certificate to construct or purchase a supply-side or demand-side capacity resource that was not previously approved as part of the IRP, or if it finds that other conditions, such as an increase in its projected load forecast, warrant an amendment. Included within the Company’s IRP Update are the Application for Certification of Yates 8-10 (“Yates Certification Application”), the Company’s requests for certification of the Mississippi Power Company (“MPC”) and Santa Rosa Energy Center (“Santa Rosa”) power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) (collectively, the “Certification Applications”), and the Company’s request to amend the certificate for the Residential Thermostat Demand Response Demand Side Management (“DSM”) Program. 
On March 27, 2024, Georgia Power and the Commission’s Public Interest Advocacy Staff (“PIA Staff”) entered into a stipulation resolving all issues in this proceeding (the “Stipulation”). Since then, several Intervenors, specifically, the Georgia Association of Manufacturers (“GAM”), Advanced Power Alliance (“APA”), Clean Energy Buyers Association (“CEBA”), Georgia Watch, Metropolitan Atlanta Rapid Transit Authority (“MARTA”), Southern Renewable Energy Association (“SREA”), and Walmart Inc. (collectively, the “Stipulating Parties”) have also signed on to the Stipulation. For the reasons set forth herein, Georgia Power’s request, as modified by the Stipulation, benefits all customers by preserving and protecting reliability and supporting the continued economic development of our state, all while placing downward pressure on rates for all customers. Accordingly, the Commission should approve the Company’s 2023 IRP Update and the Certification Applications, as modified by the Stipulation. 
SUMMARY OF THE STIPULATION
The Commission should approve the Company’s 2023 IRP Update, as modified by the Stipulation, because it represents a reasonable path to ensuring that Georgia Power can continue to serve its existing and growing customer base with the reliability that customers value and expect, while continuing to support Georgia’s growing and thriving economy. The constructive Stipulation accounts for many of the recommendations raised by PIA Staff and multiple Intervenors in this proceeding and resolves most of the contested issues in this case. In addition to providing necessary capacity resources to support the Company’s growing customer load and the state’s growing economy, the Stipulation provides substantial protections and numerous benefits to all customers.
All parties, including PIA Staff, agree that the historic and unprecedented load forecasted by the Company is in fact coming to the state. Accordingly, the Stipulation approves the Company’s load forecast as it was filed. The Stipulating Parties agree that this will not constitute approval of the methodologies used in creating the forecast and shall not be precedent in future proceedings. The Stipulating Parties also agree that the Company shall file quarterly reports that will include information identified in Attachment A of the Stipulation, as well as any other pertinent information about large load economic development activity and any updates, beginning on May 15, 2024, and continuing through the Commission’s decision in the Company’s 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (the “2025 IRP”). 
The Stipulation supports the incremental revenue forecast for large load customers presented in the IRP update, as shown in Attachment B to the Stipulation. This incremental revenue forecast will be set as a minimum estimate for the years 2026, 2027, and 2028, and the Stipulating Parties agree that the Company will file its next base rate case in a manner similar to previous rate adjustment requests. These minimum estimates will ensure that the incremental revenue from new large load customers will have downward pressure of at least $2.89 per month for a typical residential customer using an average of 1,000 kWh per month in those years. The Company will file a Cost-of-Service Study as part of its next base rate case. Additional Cost-of Service data will include detail sufficient to show how forecasted costs relating to the new capacity and revenues from the new large load customers at issue in this case will be allocated to customer rate groups.
The Stipulating Parties agree to the certification of the MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs, with several modifications. The Stipulating Parties agree to the exemption of these PPAs from the Commission’s Request for Proposal (“RFP”) requirements. The Stipulating Parties also agree to eliminate the regulatory asset deferral treatment for both PPAs originally requested by the Company and that neither PPA will earn an additional sum in 2024 and 2025. Instead, the Company will collect an additional sum at the requested $3/kW-year amount beginning in 2026 and continuing for the remainder of the terms of the PPAs. 
Through the Stipulation, Georgia Power agrees to modify its 1,000 MW request related to battery energy storage systems (“BESS”). First, the Stipulating Parties agree that the Company should be authorized to develop, own, and operate up to 500 MW of BESS resources at various sites designed to meet the needs of economic development customer loads through the Winter of 2026/2027. These 500 MW of BESS are exempted from the Commission’s RFP process, and the Company shall file for expedited certification of projects. Should the projects not be certified, the Commission will consider whether any incurred project costs not useful or transferable to other future projects shall be deferred to a regulatory asset for future recovery. The Commission will monitor load growth and the Company or PIA Staff may request an adjustment to the amount of capacity needed to ensure that customers’ needs can be met in the winter of 2026/2027. Second, as part of the approved 500 MW of Company-owned BESS, the Stipulation approves the BESS projects at Robins and Moody Air Force Bases and exempts them from the Commission’s RFP process. The Company agrees to file an expedited certification for these two projects, and the Company further agrees to file semi-annual construction monitoring reports for these projects from the date construction begins through the date of commercial operation. Third, the Company agrees to issue an expedited and narrowly tailored RFP for the remaining 500 MW of BESS sought by the Company, which will have commercial operation dates no later than December 1, 2027, and will follow the procedures laid out in Attachment C of the Stipulation. The Stipulation provides for relevant waivers of Commission rules to carry out this narrowly tailored RFP. Finally, the Stipulation provides that the Company’s request for a 200 MW BESS plus 200 MW solar project should not be approved, and the Company may bid the project in a future RFP.
As provided in the Stipulation, the Plant Yates combustion turbines (“CTs”) Units 8-10 (“Yates 8-10”) should be approved as part of the 2023 IRP Update. Accordingly, the Commission would proceed with a hearing on the Company’s Application for Certification of Yates 8-10 (“Yates Certification Application”). The Stipulating Parties agree that, if the Application is granted, Georgia Power will not seek recovery of any project construction costs that exceed the proposed amount in the Company’s Yates Certification Application, absent a showing that such costs are the result of circumstances beyond the Company’s reasonable control. However, if the Company seeks recovery of additional project construction costs due to such circumstances, the Company has the burden of proving such costs were the result of circumstances beyond its control and that such costs were reasonable and prudent. If the Yates Certification Application is granted, the Company agrees to file semi-annual construction monitoring reports from the date construction begins through the date of commercial operation.
The Company’s proposed Distributed Energy Resource (“DER”) Colocation Program, DER Customer Owned Program, and Curtailable Load Program should all be approved as modified by the Stipulation. Further, the Company’s request for an amended certificate for its Residential Thermostat Demand Response (“DR”) program should be approved, and the Company agrees to evaluate the potential of this program for the upcoming 2025 IRP to determine the optimal participation level to ensure cost-effective demand reductions.
The Stipulation promotes the continued development of renewable energy by ensuring that the Company will evaluate and develop a residential and small commercial solar and battery pilot program. This pilot program will provide grid reliability and capacity benefits and will be submitted as part of the 2025 IRP. The Stipulating Parties agree that the Company, PIA Staff, and other interested parties will meet twice to collaborate prior to finalizing the pilot program proposal.
The Stipulating Parties agree that the Company’s proposed Flex Capacity framework should not be approved. Accordingly, the Company’s request to record regulatory assets related to the framework should also not be approved.
The Stipulating Parties agree to approve the transmission projects necessary to accommodate the loads and resource portfolio that are approved by the Commission, including those projects or equivalent solutions identified in the Transmission Supplemental filing. The Company agrees to continue investigating opportunities to use DERs and grid-enhancing technologies (“GETs”) to reduce or defer the need for transmission upgrades needed for future load growth or proposed generation additions.
Finally, the Stipulating Parties agree that the Company will investigate additional behind the meter opportunities for DSM, DERs, energy storage systems, and related federal funding opportunities.

STANDARD OF REVIEW
“The Commission shall determine which combinations of resource options. . . best serve the public interest considering economics, safety, reliability, flexibility, risk, equity among ratepayers and classes, customer bills, externalities, and other factors the Commission deems appropriate.” Commission Rule 515-3-4-.05(4).
ARGUMENT
The Stipulation should be adopted as a resolution to all issues in this proceeding. 
The Stipulation considers nearly every issue raised in this proceeding. As with any settlement, no single party to the Stipulation received everything it advocated for or requested. Nevertheless, the Stipulating Parties worked hard to look beyond single issues and reach an agreement that is focused on the overall benefit for Georgia Power’s customers that also supports continued economic growth for our state. The Stipulation is a fair resolution of the Company’s 2023 IRP Update that strikes a reasonable balance among the various complex, technical issues in this proceeding. The Company’s 2023 IRP Update, as amended by the Stipulation, should be approved by the Commission. 
Resolution of contested Commission proceedings through a stipulated agreement is a well-established practice. In fact, the PSO specifically contemplates that PIA Staff “may negotiate settlements with other parties, in the public interest.” (PSO at 2.) The Company and PIA Staff have been available to all Intervenors to discuss potential settlement throughout the proceeding, and the Company engaged with many of the Intervenors concerning potential settlement positions. In addition, all parties had an opportunity to cross examine Company witnesses during each phase of the hearings, and specifically about the Stipulation during the Company’s rebuttal hearing. Further, each party is entitled to file briefs and proposed orders setting forth their respective positions. Accordingly, the record in this case supports the Stipulation, as executed by the Stipulating Parties. Importantly, the Stipulation is a well-reasoned and balanced resolution of the Company’s 2023 IRP Update that will provide significant benefits to Georgia Power’s customers. These benefits include customer protections, the assurance of downward pressure on customer rates, and the continued resiliency and reliability of the Company’s electric system (“System”), all while supporting the economic growth of Georgia. Therefore, the Stipulation should be approved by this Commission, as adopted by the Stipulating Parties.
The Commission should approve the Company’s load forecast as filed and agreed to in the Stipulation.
The Commission should have confidence in the Company’s load forecast and should approve the forecast as filed and agreed to in the Stipulation. The Company’s forecast is based on a statistically sound methodology, is informed by the latest economic trends, and uses proven methods to evaluate the probability, level, and timing in which the projected load will materialize. Substantial empirical evidence shows that the load projected by the Company is indeed coming to Georgia. There is a large economic development pipeline made up of businesses seeking to locate in Georgia, and the Company has continued to see progress from large load customers included in its forecast, as well as accelerated customer load ramps and other tangible evidence of growth. The Company provided robust and sufficient data to support the accuracy of its load forecast, allowing PIA Staff and the Commission to determine the adequacy of the load forecast in this case. Moreover, no party in this proceeding challenged the underlying methodology supporting the Company’s base forecast or the methodology underlying the Company’s Load Realization Model. Certain Intervenors propose using different load values to plan generation resources and criticize the Company’s load forecast, but as the Company has demonstrated, these critiques present an unacceptable planning risk or are already accounted for and should be denied or rejected. Next, any concerns regarding the Company’s load forecast can be mitigated by the various levers at the Company’s and the Commission’s disposal. Finally, the Company has agreed to file quarterly reports on large load economic development activity, which will keep this Commission apprised of any progress or changes in economic development activity.
Substantial empirical evidence proves that unprecedented load growth is coming to Georgia.
There are at least three factors that demonstrate the increasing number of large load customers and corresponding load growth that are coming to Georgia. First, the number of committed Georgia Power customers continues to increase. As of the 2023 IRP Update filing, the Company had already been chosen to serve over 3,600 MW of load from the approximately 17,000 MW pipeline of economic development—nearly 3,000 MW of which is already under construction. (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2031.) Since the 2023 IRP Update filing in October of 2023, the economic development pipeline has grown from 17,000 MW to 21,000 MW, and Georgia Power has been selected to serve an additional 2,602 MW. (Id.) The customers represented by this 2,602 MW were already included in the load forecast. By signing a request for electric service with Georgia Power, these customers have affirmed their commitments to take electric service from the Company. The risk around this potential load materializing continues to decrease, as it is now committed load that Georgia Power must plan to serve. (See id. at 2033.)
Second, the large load customers included in the Company’s forecast are moving forward and making progress without material delay. (Id. at 2032.) Of the 51 large load customer projects included in the external adjustment to the Company’s 2023 IRP Update Load Forecast, 32 projects, accounting for almost 10,500 MW, have already chosen to do business in Georgia and 14 of those projects have signed requests for electric service and selected Georgia Power as their electric service provider. These customers have taken concrete actions evidencing their commitment to do business in Georgia, such as purchasing or leasing property. Of the original 14 committed customers at the time of filing, nine have broken ground, four are pending construction, and only one 66 MW project has been delayed. (Id.) Since the initial filing, an additional 11 projects have signed requests for electric service and selected Georgia Power as their electric service provider.
Third, three committed customers representing 2,173 MW have accelerated their load ramps to incorporate their entire load five years earlier than previously requested. (Id. at 2024.) In certain years, such as 2028 and 2029, this acceleration would result in approximately 800 MW of more load than was assumed for the 2023 IRP Update Load Forecast. This demonstrates the potential for substantial load to materialize at a faster pace than projected. 
As evidenced by the Company’s current 6,214 MW load from committed customers, Georgia Power anticipates that these and other projects will continue to advance through the various stages of economic development and ultimately materialize. (Id. at 2033.) As such, the Company’s forecast provides the most realistic depiction of large load growth in Georgia. Given the continued increase, progress, and pace of committed customer load, the Commission should have an extremely high degree of confidence in the forecast and should approve the Company’s load forecast as filed and agreed to in the Stipulation. 
The Company provided robust and sufficient data to support the accuracy of its load forecast.
The Company complied with the Commission’s Rules regarding the contents and validity of a load forecast. As acknowledged by PIA Staff (see Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 244) and the Commission’s PSO in this docket, the Company’s IRP Update is of a limited scope. Commission Rule 515-3-4-.06(6) provides that the Commission may limit the scope of issues it will consider in the review of subsequent plans (e.g., the IRP Update) to those issues directly related to material changes. In accordance with this rule and the material change necessitating the filing of the 2023 IRP Update—extraordinary economic development and large load customer growth—the Company appropriately focused on the Load Realization Model and forecast adjustments supporting the substantial new large loads coming to the state.
Although the Company would typically provide a high and low load growth sensitivity to the load forecast supporting the triennial IRP, the Load Realization Model, which was used to adjust the forecast in this case for the new large load customer growth, appropriately evaluated a range of outcomes and possible futures within the context of the changed circumstances the IRP Update was intended to address. The Company ran multiple load forecast sensitivities in the months leading up to the IRP Update filing, each of which was provided to PIA Staff in response to data request STF-DEA-1-4. The Company analyzed different sensitivities, chose the most reasonable load forecast to plan to, and proposed a portfolio of resource solutions to meet the need. The presentation of a singular forecast as part of an IRP amendment process is not a failure to meet Commission Rules, which are directed at the requirements for a triennial IRP filing. To the contrary, the singular forecast as a part of this IRP amendment process was needed to identify a specific and deployable plan for resources to meet capacity needs and certainly did not prevent any party from proposing an alternative forecast. 
The Company filed the load forecast and supporting information that is relevant and directly related to the material change in assumed load over the Company’s planning horizon. As part of its initial filing, the Company provided short-term, long-term, peak, and load realization models; workpapers including weather data, forecast inputs, short-term model outputs, and Peak Demand aggregate and Territorial files that contain final output information on peak and energy forecasts; and data supporting all charts and tables included in the filing. The models shared with PIA Staff provided more information than would normally be included in a static IRP technical appendix; these models can also be tested and used to develop PIA Staff’s own comparable models. 
Further, the underlying base forecast, which captures organic growth on the System, was compiled using the same methodologies, models, and techniques as used in prior IRPs and as accurately described in the Load Forecast Technical Appendix to the 2022 IRP. In relying on the same methodology used previously, it was not necessary to re-submit Sections 4 through 6 of the Technical Appendix of the 2022 IRP. The Company also provided alternate levels of expected growth as part of its resource mix study. A description of all load forecast determinants was provided in response to data request STF-JKA-2-10. The impact of self-generation and cogeneration is included in the Load Forecast Technical Appendix of the 2022 IRP, and the transparency of the Company’s forecasting tools are consistent with each load forecast filed and approved in every IRP since 2001. The Company provided more than sufficient supporting information for the Commission to have confidence in the data, information, methodology, and rigor of analysis used in preparing the Company’s 2023 Load Forecast. Accordingly, the forecast should be approved as a just and reasonable estimation of anticipated load growth. 
No party reasonably challenged the methodology supporting the Company’s base forecast or the methodology underlying the Load Realization Model.
PIA Staff and the Intervenors submitting testimony on this issue accepted the validity of the Company’s organic load forecast and the statistically sound methodology used to account for the growth in new large load customers. (See, e.g., Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 89, 1266.) Regarding the Load Realization Model, PIA Staff agrees that the Company’s methodology is appropriate and GIPL agrees that a new methodology was needed to account for this growth. (Id.) The Company’s forecasting methodology for large loads accounts for numerous types of uncertainty, including the potential that load will not materialize, will be delayed, or will materialize at a lower level than expected. Moreover, the Company’s forecast for large loads is based on the most recent economic trends, includes projects that have already selected Georgia Power as their electric service provider, and incorporates direct input from customers about growth in our state. In sum, the Company’s forecast provides the most realistic depiction of large load growth in Georgia, is appropriately risk-adjusted, and is based on statistically sound methodology.
Microsoft requested in its comments that the Company “provide greater transparency to stakeholders regarding its large load forecasting methodology and the underlying data used to support it.” (Microsoft Comments 6.) However, as described in Section (1)(b) herein, the Company made the Load Realization Model and the data used to support it and the base forecast available for all parties who expressed an interest in it. Numerous other parties in this docket, including PIA Staff, were able to review and analyze the Company’s Load Realization Model and underlying data, and this same data was available for Microsoft’s review. Unfortunately, prior to filing its comments, Microsoft did not engage with the Company to better understand its forecasting methodologies. For the reasons set forth above, the Company’s 2023 IRP Update Load Forecast, Load Realization Model, and supporting data is sufficiently transparent, was made available to interested parties, and adequately supports the Company’s request in this case.
GIPL’s proposal to use a P50 load value would jeopardize reliability in Georgia and should be rejected.
Georgia Power’s load forecast appropriately plans to a P95 load value. Contrary to GIPL’s recommendations, planning generation resources to the P50 load value presents unacceptable reliability risk in serving both existing customer loads and the significant large customer loads coming to the state. Under different circumstances, planning to P50 could help support slow and steady load growth. But the rapid and extraordinary load growth being experienced in Georgia is not slow and steady and cannot be addressed with a P50 load value. Planning generation resources to P50 necessarily means that the Company would not have sufficient resources to meet customer needs in half of the potential planning scenarios for new large load customers and would thus not be able to support the projected economic development load coming to Georgia. 
Notably, GIPL used the Company’s large load forecast methodology and assumptions but proposed using a P50 level load value instead of the P95 load value used by the Company. (Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1265.) GIPL asserted that the P50 value is, statistically speaking, the most likely outcome and should, therefore, be used in lieu of the P95 value. (Id. at 1271.) The Company agrees that P50 represents the median outcome, meaning that it equally splits the possible outcomes in half. However, as stated by Witness Valle at the rebuttal hearing, planning to a P50 load value is equivalent to a flip of a coin—where there is a fifty/fifty chance that loads will materialize higher or lower than assumed. (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2178.) Simply put, if Georgia Power were to plan its System using the P50 value, that would mean there is a 50% chance that it would have too few resources to meet large load customer needs and would not be able to reliably support new economic load growth. The difference in load at the P50 and P95 load levels is approximately 1,700 MW, representing a substantial difference in load that Georgia Power must be able to serve. Georgia Power’s customers expect the Company to be able to serve them, and gambling with whether the Company can meet customers’ energy needs is not prudent resource planning. Planning to the P95 load level recommended by the Company, agreed to by PIA Staff in its testimony, and as agreed to in the Stipulation, provides greater confidence that the Company will have the resources needed to serve Georgia as it continues to grow.
Further, the load forecast is a static snapshot in time that does not capture the fact that additional load growth is taking place. (Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1312.) For each new large load customer that comes onto the System, the load level previously planned for becomes less representative of the higher needs the Company must serve. (Id. at 1312–1313.) Thus, as customer load continues to grow, it is even riskier to plan according to the P50 load level than the P95 load level. As previously explained, the large load customer volume Georgia Power must serve only continues to increase. Planning to the P95 load level is necessary to ensure the Company has sufficient resources to reliably serve the growing customer demand for energy. Accordingly, GIPL’s recommendation to plan to the P50 load level should be rejected.
The Commission should afford no weight to Microsoft’s criticisms of the Company’s forecasting methodology.
Microsoft makes several uninformed recommendations regarding the Company’s load forecast, and these recommendations should be afforded no weight by the Commission. First, Microsoft suggests that “GPC should analyze the different commitment levels” from known and potential large load customers and assign standardized and decreasing discount levels to each stage of commitment, reflecting the likelihood that such large loads will in fact appear. (Microsoft Comments 5–6.) However, the Company already considers various levels of customer commitment in its analysis. The Company is well aware of the natural progression of projects in the economic development pipeline, from the state selection phase up until contracts for electric service are finalized. The Load Realization Model provides a risk adjustment for each project in the pipeline specifically to account for the level of individual project commitments, delays, and materialization. (See Direct Hearing Tr. 118–19.) Failure to account for each project in the pipeline would underestimate the generation needed to serve customers, resulting in inadequate resource planning and a lack of sufficient resources. In summary, Microsoft’s recommendation related to commitment levels is already accounted for in detail as part of the Company’s methodology.
Next, Microsoft suggests that near-term resource requirements should only be based on known, mature projects “that have made firm commitments to GPC versus load that has yet to select GPC or the State of Georgia” to avoid the risk of over procurement. (Microsoft Comments 6.) This approach is untenable not just for the Company or the state of Georgia, but for Microsoft itself. If Microsoft’s load forecast criteria were adopted, it would exclude Microsoft’s own projects under development from inclusion in the Company’s forecast and would result in insufficient resources to serve those projects. In short, this suggestion would be detrimental to Microsoft’s own interests. The Company has appropriately considered project uncertainties and varying stages of customer commitment within its forecast. (See Direct Hearing Tr. 118–19.) As such, Microsoft’s proposed load forecast limitations should be rejected.
The Company and this Commission have numerous methods available to mitigate any potential risks associated with adopting the Company’s proposed load forecast. 
Approving the Company’s load forecasts and corresponding requests in this case will enable the Company to continue to reliably serve its growing customer load and support Georgia’s economic growth. Intervenors have cautioned that if the projected load materializes at a level lower than forecasted, the Company will more likely than not have overbuilt its System and left customers to cover the cost of resources the Company does not need. (See, e.g., Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1276, 1313.) As noted previously, the Company is confident that load will develop as it has projected. For example, the Company can sell capacity on the wholesale market, especially given the extraordinary need for additional dispatchable capacity resources across the country, particularly on neighboring systems. (See Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 1947.) The Company can also slow down, reduce, or halt generation development by modifying the development timeline of approved resources or the target procurement amount from approved RFPs. (Id.) (See id.) Thus, the Company and Commission have several options at their disposal to appropriately manage and mitigate the risk of load materializing at a level less than projected. Accordingly, any risk associated with the Company’s load forecast should not prevent the Commission from approving the forecast, and the forecast should be adopted to ensure there are sufficient resources to serve the load growth projected in Georgia.

The Company’s 26% target reserve margin should be maintained, and Intervenor recommendations to reduce it should be rejected. 
As part of the 2022 IRP, the Commission approved, and the Company adopted a 26% winter target reserve margin (“TRM”) for planning purposes. (See Order Adopting Stipulation 20, para. 16, Docket Nos. 44160 and 44161 (Jul. 29, 2022).) The TRM adopted by the Company is a “balance of economics and reliability.” (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 1991.) A 26% TRM is vital to ensuring System reliability during extreme weather events (Id. at 2078.) and results in an expected loss of load event (“LOLE”) once every 18 years. (Id. at 2168.) This 26% winter TRM is appropriate and should be maintained by this Commission.
Some Intervenors suggested lowering the TRM to 20%, which equates to a LOLE occurring once every 10 years. (See Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1286–87.) While some Intervenors argue that this 1:10 LOLE level is an “industry standard,” Georgia Power appropriately views this as a minimum level of reliability. (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2165.) Georgia Power customers do not expect Georgia Power to provide the minimum level of reliability. They expect and deserve the exceptional reliability we have in Georgia. Georgia Power strongly opposes the proposal to adopt a winter TRM that would reduce reliability and result in substandard service for its customers. Lowering the winter TRM to the levels proposed by some Intervenors would lead to a substantial decline in reliability (roughly 80%) while increasing costs and risks for customers. (See id. at 2079, 2050, 2168, 2234.) Notably, utilities across the Southeast have either increased or established winter TRMs in the last few years. (See id. at 2048.) Use of a 26% TRM aligns with recent industry trends. The adoption of a lower TRM would run counter to industry reliability trends and, more importantly, reduce System reliability. Therefore, recommendations to lower the winter TRM should be rejected.
Some Intervenors also argue that the Company should have updated its Reserve Margin Study for the 2023 IRP Update. (Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1286, 1767, 1814.) However, adjusting or re-running the Reserve Margin Study for a single variable, as suggested by these Intervenors, is inappropriate. All relevant inputs and variables must be considered when updating the Reserve Margin Study. (See Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2049.) Put simply, the approach recommended by certain Intervenors is inappropriate and fails to account for the highly complex nature of Reserve Margin Studies. Consistent with its prior practice, the Company will file an updated Reserve Margin Study in the 2025 IRP. (See id.) At that point, the Company, Intervenors, and this Commission can properly evaluate the winter TRM in the context of all other changes in the Reserve Margin Study. The 26% winter TRM, as approved in the 2022 IRP for planning purposes and used in this 2023 IRP Update, is appropriate. Accordingly, the Commission should maintain its use for planning purposes in this case and revisit the winter TRM in the 2025 IRP.

It would not be an effective use of the PIA Staff, Intervenors, or this Commission’s time for Georgia Power to rebut every single allegation asserted or implied in the 21 testimony filings and one position paper filed in this case. As expressly stated in the Company’s rebuttal testimony:
Due to the number of Parties filing testimony in this case and the limited time available to file rebuttal, our testimony focuses on several key issues without attempting to address every issue raised in PIA Staff or intervenor testimony. The fact that the Company may not respond to a particular position of PIA Staff or an intervenor should not be construed as acceptance of such position. 

(Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2022.)
In the interest of expediency and efficiency in these proceedings, Georgia Power focused its rebuttal and its discussion in this brief on the core issues in dispute in this proceeding, rather than devoting unnecessary time and resources to refute positions that are clearly wrong or simply irrelevant to the issues presented in this docket. Moreover, even if Georgia Power had not expressly reserved this position, the Commission, PIA Staff, and Intervenors could not reasonably conclude that something was agreed to by the Company merely because it was not specifically addressed during rebuttal.

Georgia Power’s 2023 IRP Update, as modified by the Stipulation, benefits all customers by preserving and protecting reliability and supporting the continued economic development of our state, all while placing downward pressure on rates for all customers. For the reasons set forth above, and based on the record in this docket, the Commission should approve the Company’s 2023 IRP Update as modified by the Stipulation.

        B.     The Company’s 26% target reserve margin should be maintained, and
               Intervenor recommendations to reduce it should be rejected.

        As part of the 2022 IRP, the Commission approved, and the Company adopted a 26%

winter target reserve margin (“TRM”) for planning purposes. (See Order Adopting Stipulation 20,

para. 16, Docket Nos. 44160 and 44161 (Jul. 29, 2022).) The TRM adopted by the Company is a

                               Post-Hearing Brief of Georgia Power Company
                                         GPSC Docket No. 55378
                                               Page 33 of 38
170331249v1

“balance of economics and reliability.” (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 1991.) A 26% TRM is vital to

ensuring System reliability during extreme weather events (Id. at 2078.) and results in an expected

loss of load event (“LOLE”) once every 18 years. (Id. at 2168.) This 26% winter TRM is

appropriate and should be maintained by this Commission.

        Some Intervenors suggested lowering the TRM to 20%, which equates to a LOLE

occurring once every 10 years. (See Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1286–87.) While some

Intervenors argue that this 1:10 LOLE level is an “industry standard,” Georgia Power appropriately

views this as a minimum level of reliability. (Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2165.) Georgia Power

customers do not expect Georgia Power to provide the minimum level of reliability. They expect

and deserve the exceptional reliability we have in Georgia. Georgia Power strongly opposes the

proposal to adopt a winter TRM that would reduce reliability and result in substandard service for

its customers. Lowering the winter TRM to the levels proposed by some Intervenors would lead

to a substantial decline in reliability (roughly 80%) while increasing costs and risks for customers.

(See id. at 2079, 2050, 2168, 2234.) Notably, utilities across the Southeast have either increased

or established winter TRMs in the last few years. (See id. at 2048.) Use of a 26% TRM aligns with

recent industry trends. The adoption of a lower TRM would run counter to industry reliability

trends and, more importantly, reduce System reliability. Therefore, recommendations to lower the

winter TRM should be rejected.

        Some Intervenors also argue that the Company should have updated its Reserve Margin

Study for the 2023 IRP Update. (Staff/Intervenor Hearing Tr. 1286, 1767, 1814.) However,

adjusting or re-running the Reserve Margin Study for a single variable, as suggested by these

Intervenors, is inappropriate. All relevant inputs and variables must be considered when updating

the Reserve Margin Study. (See Rebuttal Hearing Tr. 2049.) Put simply, the approach

                               Post-Hearing Brief of Georgia Power Company
                                         GPSC Docket No. 55378
                                               Page 34 of 38
170331249v1

recommended by certain Intervenors is inappropriate and fails to account for the highly complex

nature of Reserve Margin Studies. Consistent with its prior practice, the Company will file an

updated Reserve Margin Study in the 2025 IRP. (See id.) At that point, the Company, Intervenors,

and this Commission can properly evaluate the winter TRM in the context of all other changes in

the Reserve Margin Study. The 26% winter TRM, as approved in the 2022 IRP for planning

purposes and used in this 2023 IRP Update, is appropriate. Accordingly, the Commission should

maintain its use for planning purposes in this case and revisit the winter TRM in the 2025 IRP.

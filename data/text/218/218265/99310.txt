                     INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

       The Department of Defense (“DOD”), on behalf of itself and all other Federal Executive

Agencies (collectively, “DOD/FEA”), hereby submits its post-hearing brief in opposition to the

settlement agreement (“Stipulation”) entered into on March 26, 2024 between the Georgia Public

Service Commission Public Interest Advocacy Staff (“Commission Staff”) and Georgia Power

GPC (“GPC” or “Georgia Power”), as amended to include the Georgia Association of

Manufacturers.

       Through its 2023 Integrated Resource Plan Update (“2023 IRP”), Georgia Power

proposed, and Commission Staff have since approved, to add generation assets to Georgia

Power’s portfolio in an amount and type that is all but assured to create a staggering amount of

carbon dioxide emissions. Future load growth projected in the 2023 IRP presents an opportunity

for Georgia Power to move this state towards a clean, resilient, and affordable energy future. But

the Stipulation fails to live up to this potential by omitting the inclusion of even a single carbon-

pollution free electricity (CFE) generation asset. Moreover, conspicuously absent from the

Stipulation is any means by which GPC customers can procure CFE to meet their energy

requirements. For these reasons and the others described herein, DOD/FEA strongly opposes this

settlement.

       I.      THE MILITARY AND GEORGIA POWER’S OTHER CUSTOMERS ARE
               DEPENDENT ON GEORGIA POWER WHEN PROCURING
               ELECTRICITY TO MEET THEIR RESPECTIVE NEEDS

       Over roughly the next six years, DOD/FEA must transform how it buys energy in the

state of Georgia. By 2030, it must procure 100 percent of its electricity demand in the form of

CFE. (Presidential Executive Order 14057.) As GPC’s largest customer by consumption, the

enormity of this task cannot be overstated. The days for business as usual and incremental

change are behind us.

       Notably, DOD/FEA is not an outlier in these goals. An urgent desire to move quickly

towards clean energy sources is shared by a significant number of other large Georgia Power

customers, including members of the Georgia Association for Manufacturing, the Clean Energy

Buyers Association (which represents hundreds of large employers), Microsoft, Walmart, and

local governments across the state of Georgia. Yet the energy future sought by these and other

parties is now considerably further out of reach because of this Stipulation.

       With few exceptions, Georgia Power’s customers have little to no choice with respect to

the sources from which they procure their electricity. GPC’s status as a regulated utility means

that the multiple large military bases that provide tens of thousands of jobs and the other federal

agencies that provide services to millions of Georgians are dependent on Georgia Power to

provide pathways for the procurement of clean, resilient power. For that reason, DOD/FEA has

engaged thoroughly in this 2023 IRP process. It has presented detailed expert testimony that

explains our requirements and how Georgia Power can make choices that align with these

objectives. DOD/FEA is not asking Georgia Power to make choices that will come at the


                                                 2

expense of GPC’s customer base on the issues of reliability or price. Rather, DOD/FEA and

other intervenors have presented realistic changes and strategies available to GPC to move

towards a clean energy future while affordably and reliably serving current and future customers.

But, through this Stipulation, Georgia Power has rejected these opportunities.

                                           ARGUMENT

       A.      DOD/FEA’S CLEAN ENERGY POLICY AND 2023 AGREEMENT WITH
               SOUTHERN COMPANY

       On December 28, 2021, President Biden issued Executive Order No. 14057, Catalyzing

Clean Energy Industries and Jobs Through Federal Sustainability. E.O. 14057 states that the

Federal Government is the single largest landowner, energy consumer, and employer in the

Nation, and can catalyze private sector investment and expand the economy and American

industry by transforming how we build, buy, and manage electricity. E.O. No. 14057 calls on

                                                  8

the Federal Government to lead by example to achieve a carbon pollution-free electricity sector

by 2035 and net-zero emissions economy-wide by no later than 2050, using a whole-of-

government approach. Section 203 of E.O. 14057 calls for each FEA to (1) increase its

percentage use of CFE so that it constitutes 100 percent of facility electrical energy use on an

annual basis, and (2) seek to match use on an hourly basis to achieve 50 percent 24/7 CFE, by

fiscal year 2030.

       The Federal government seeks to implement E.O. 14057 Section 203 through programs

such as Georgia Power’s CARES program, which will help enable the Federal government to

scale CFE procurement to 100 percent of its load by 2030 and 50 percent CFE by 2030 on a 24/7

hourly-matched basis. This objective can be achieved by a combination of: grid-supplied CFE

(i.e. the CFE resources all jurisdictional customers are already paying for via standard rates):

purchased CFE such as via green tariffs like the CARES program; on-site CFE generation, and

purchased Energy Attribute Certificates (EACs).

       The success of FEA’s efforts to implement E.O. 14057 Section 203 depends on the

willingness of electricity suppliers to partner with the federal government and both maximize

grid-supplied CFE and institute robust purchased CFE options. To the extent those options exist

in a service area, DOD and all other federal agencies will pursue “bundled CFE” rather than

purchase EACs decoupled from the electricity they represent.

       In recognition of the important role our utility partners play in retail regulated markets the

Federal government has signed Memoranda of Understanding (“MOUs”) with various utilities.

These MOUs state that DOD/FEA will work with utilities to execute on existing purchased CFE

options as well as establish new programs to enable FEA to achieve clean energy objectives.

Southern Company - parent of GPC - has signed one of these MOUs with the U.S. General

                                                 9

Services Administration (“GSA”) on behalf of all federal agencies, indicating our mutual

agreement on the following four core objectives:


      ●    Developing a roadmap for the Federal Government’s electricity needs served by
           Southern Company to meet the targets set out in the CFE requirements of EO 14057

      ●    Designing a CFE tariff or identifying an applicable regulatory pathway that would
           provide the appropriate pricing or other terms needed to achieve the requirements of
           EO 14057

      ●    Subject to Southern Company’s respective resource planning and operation
           obligations, the construction or procurement of CFE resources needed to generate
           additional electricity and associated EACs, which in conjunction with Southern
           Company’s existing resources, would result in Federal government operations using
           electricity that is 100 percent attributable to CFE resources by 2030

      ●    In support of EO 14057 requirements, certification and reporting of EACs and
           Scope 2 emissions accounting to satisfy Federal requirements, including hourly time
           matching of load and generation and certification of CFE legacy asset output that can
           be properly attributable to Federal customers

       Per the above, the IRPs of Southern Company’s subsidiary operating utilities are an

essential piece of achieving our joint objectives. GPC currently has green tariff programs in

which agencies are exploring enrollment; how much CFE agencies ultimately need to procure is

a function of the share of CFE that GPC delivers to customers broadly, as well as how much

CFE is available to be procured through existing or newly developed programs. Therefore, IRPs

that propose to add substantial new emitting generation, and include relatively low participation

caps on clean energy programs, that are likely to be easily outstripped by demand, create serious

challenges to our ability to meet the requirements of E.O. 14057. In retail regulated markets

customers are largely dependent on utilities and their regulators to ensure that customers have

sufficient options, while not taking more than customers’ pro rata share of these resources.

       C.      GEORGIA POWER’S IRP AND THE STIPULATION DO NOT RESULT
               IN A CLEAN, AFFORDABLE, OR RESILIENT PLAN THAT SERVES
               THE BEST INTERESTS OF ALL CUSTOMERS


       As noted above, the DOD-FEA has significant concerns with the direction GPC and Staff

have taken in the stipulated settlement and ultimately FEA does not believe the plan reflected in

the settlement is in the best interest of GPC’s customers or the state of Georgia.


       In this IRP, GPC has asserted that they are facing unprecedented load growth over the

next 5-10 years, primarily as a result of substantial manufacturing onshoring and new data

centers. Between confirmed and anticipated future load growth, and the Company’s preferred

Planning Reserve Margin (“PRM”), GPC anticipated that it would need an additional 6,600 MW

of resources by the early 2030s. This projection is based on a new load forecasting approach, to

understand large, high load factor customers’ likelihood of materializing and when; whereas it is

industry standard to forecast probabilistic load into the future and utilize a 50th percentile value

(or P50) forecast on which to plan, the Company here proposes to utilize a P95 forecast.

                                                  12

       The Company is forecasting planned load per existing agreements, as well as speculative

incremental load, and then proposing resources to meet those needs assuming a 95th percentile

probability that this load materializes. On top of this P95 forecast the Company applies a high

winter planning reserve margin of 26 percent to arrive at its obligation forecast and inform the

magnitude and type of resources it proposes in this IRP. While GPC currently has a long position

on capacity in all evaluated seasons, this high forecast combined with high winter PRM results in

an asserted “need” for incremental accredited capacity specifically in late 2020s winter seasons.

       There is compelling testimony on the record, however, that the most reasonable load

growth forecast to utilize, as identified by GPC’s own modeling, is the P50 level. According to

testimony submitted by Chelsea Hotaling on behalf of Georgia Interfaith Power & Light

(“GIPL”) and supported by other parties, using a more reasonable and likely P50 load forecast

enables deferral of long-range capital investments, such as the Plant Yates CTs, until there is

more information to consider in the 2025 IRP, and suggests that competitively procured battery

energy storage solutions (BESS) are sufficient to meet GPC’s capacity needs through 2028. (see

GIPL witness Hotaling’s Testimony at 3.) Ms. Hotaling’s testimony on behalf of GIPL

underscores the facility in GPC’s use of the P95 load forecast: in adopting P95, GPC is planning

for larger load growth than its own simulation says it needs 95% of the time. Put another way,

“GPC is planning for more load growth than its model says will be needed in 96,000 out of the

100,000 simulations run.” Further scrutinizing GPC’s model, Ms. Hotaling finds that even if the

Commission were to adopt the P95 load forecast, the Yates Plant is still not necessary to meet

GPC’s capacity needs, and that a combination of BESS resources and PPAs in 2027 and 2028

could meet the need.


                                                13

       GPC argues in its rebuttal testimony that a P95 forecasting approach and incremental

near term resource plan is necessary to ensure that all current customer agreements and a

substantial amount of speculative new load can be served. DOD/FEA notes that the Commission

would inherently be approving resources in this IRP to serve load that is not guaranteed to

materialize and - in fact - in most simulations does not materialize to the level projected per

Hotaling’s explanation of what a P95 forecast represents. Further, the resources the settlement

agreement contemplates approving are not competitively procured nor the most future-proof

resources of which the Company can avail itself.

       GPC reported in its rebuttal testimony that only one project included in this incremental

load forecast had been completely canceled and a handful postponed thus far; but changing

financial positions and priorities of these prospective customers can alter these plans without

substantial advanced notice. Data centers and cryptocurrency customers can change plans on

short notice.

       Certainly, neither the Company nor the Commission can control what these customers

decide is in their best financial interest and no party in this proceeding is challenging the

Company’s general assertion that there is significant new load anticipated in the region. It is,

however, the Commission’s responsibility to ensure that it does not allow GPC to over-procure

resources - especially highly emitting, aging and inefficient resources, or resources that were

subject to no competition - based on inflated or speculative future load projections. It is existing

customers that will be on the hook for these expenses should this new speculative load not

materialize. And while the Company provided some information in its rebuttal testimony on the

overall expected cost impacts to customers – noting that the increased load would result in lower

rates, even with the planned expenditures on new capacity – this analysis was submitted late in


                                                  14

the docket with insufficient time for parties to review its validity or stress test the assumptions.

As we will discuss later in this brief, a lack of transparent and well supported scenario analysis is

a hallmark of the instant IRP and the Commission should require more rigorous analysis in the

future.

          Moreover, and more problematic, a few large new customer failures or even increased

operating costs for thermal resources could easily flip those benefits to costs. In sum, the type

and magnitude of new procurement that could be approved in this IRP looks cost beneficial if

one assumes the Company’s P95 forecasts and generation operational cost inputs are the most

likely outcome; however, there is significant risk that these forecasts are too optimistic on load

growth and existing customers will be left financially responsible for at least some magnitude of

highly emitting, uncompetitive resources that are ultimately not needed.

       G.      THE PLAN IS NOT RESILIENT TO FUTURE RISKS OF FUEL PRICE
               VOLATILITY AND ENVIRONMENTAL REGULATIONS, AND IT
               OVERESTIMATES THERMAL RESOURCE CONTRIBUTION TO
               RESOURCE ADEQUACY.

       DOD/FEA notes that a resource plan with more fossil generation does not automatically

result in a more resilient and reliable system. Fuel diversity resulting in resilience to future fuel

cost volatility and environmental compliance costs is an important component of resilience as

well. Unfortunately the Company presents analysis that is easy to misinterpret, does not follow

its own modeling, and does not adequately consider the impact of future regulation on balance,

and therefore does not propose the most resilient plan possible. Further, the Company

overestimates the resource adequacy value of thermal generation, assigning it very high

accredited capacity values (often 100 percent) that do not reflect even normal amounts of forced

outages, much less appropriately accounting for non-firm fuel contracts.

       As a threshold matter, it is important to recognize that the idea that additional thermal

generation results in a more resilient and better system for customers is based on outmoded

planning concepts and does not position the Company well for the future. GPC’s system does

need a set of resource attributes, including capacity and energy, spinning reserve, ancillary

services and others to ensure reliability, but these needs can often be met by different

combinations of resources that offer different benefits to the system.

       Further GPC’s modeling does not appropriately test the resilience of each particular

modeled capacity expansion plan to future environmental costs or fuel price volatility. In GPC’s

supplemental filing they did provide several expansion plans and net present value system cost

results under varying assumptions, but these are easy to misinterpret without additional context.


                                                  24

To appropriately test a particular plan’s resilience to volatile fuel costs, for example, it would

have had to test different fuel prices on the same capacity expansion plan and then examine the

range of results to understand how sensitive that plan is to changes in fuel price. The scenarios

presented in GPC’s supplement did not follow this guideline and thus comparing them directly

provides stakeholders no meaningful takeaways.

       Further, the Company’s refusal to appropriately consider the risk of future environmental

regulation and how that would impact the current proposed resource plan is short sighted. When

asked to discuss how the company evaluated the potential impacts of pending Environmental

Protection Agency Rule 111 changes - which would govern emissions reduction requirements

from both existing and new coal and gas facilities - Company witness Grubb asserted that it was

premature to consider these impacts because the rule had not yet been finalized. While it is true

that the specific rule language is not yet final and may change, that is not an adequate response

when proposing customers pay for a 1,400 MW, 40-plus year emitting asset. Importantly, in the

capacity expansion plans where the Company does consider future potential carbon regulation

costs or constraints, its own modeling selects far fewer emitting resource MW up to 2030 and far

more BESS. This shows that a future system that incorporates more dispatchable batteries is

more resilient to future environmental costs and ensures that customer resources are being spent

on the most future-proof assets possible.

       Finally, DOE/FEA notes that it is essential that the Company correctly estimate thermal

resource resilience and contribution to load, especially during extreme weather events. It is

incorrect to utilize 100 percent resource accreditation values for thermal resources, ignoring even

basic standard forced outage rates much less the potential impact of correlated outages during

critical events. Standard forced outage rates for conventional generators can average somewhere


                                                  25

between 5-10 percent, 19 and should be factored into Unforced Capacity (UCAP) values utilized

in resource planning. Further, Southern Renewable Energy Association expert witness Olson

highlights emerging best practices in considering ELCC as an appropriate measure for

dispatchable resources in addition to variable renewable and duration limited resources. In his

Direct Testimony, he states “...an emerging best practice is to use ELCC to estimate the capacity

contribution of “firm” resources such as natural gas. The ELCC of a gas unit may differ from its

Unforced Capacity or UCAP value because loss-of-load events tend to occur during periods with

an unusually high number of resource outages; that is, the combined outage rate of the fleet

during loss of load hours tends to be higher than the expected loss-of-load for any individual

resource, or the combined outage rate for the fleet during normal hours.” 20 If the Company does

not begin accounting for these factors in its IRPs, it may propose plans that over-rely on thermal

resources that experience outages at higher rates during extreme weather events and that will not

adequately account for resiliency benefits of a diverse resource mix that includes clean resources.

       I.      THE COMMISSION SHOULD AUTHORIZE AN INCREMENTAL 1,000
               MW OF SOLAR PROCUREMENT THROUGH THE ONGOING CARES
               RFP AND REJECT ADDITIONAL SUMS ON THE MPC AND SANTA
               ROSA PPAS

       First, the Commission should authorize GPC to procure at least 1,000 MW of incremental

solar or solar-plus-storage capacity through its existing RFP, consistent with the motion

DOD/FEA submits alongside this brief. Doing so would have multiple benefits. It would allow

customers who want to choose clean energy to receive more of their energy from CFE resources;

in particular with a substantial amount of new load coming on the system from companies with

stated CFE and decarbonization goals as well as existing customers like DOD/FEA, local

governments, and many CEBA members, the Company’s proposed IRP will set all customers

back on their decarbonization goals with respect to system resources. As such, CARES and any

other CFE opportunities the Company makes available are likely to be oversubscribed in its first

tranche if this amount is not increased. Further, procuring incremental solar resources would be

consistent with the Company’s own Base Case modeling, which calls for over 700 MW of

incremental solar resources as a matter of course, even before considering potential impacts of

higher gas prices or future environmental regulation. Further, CARES has the benefit of ensuring

all costs incurred for new resources are paid for by participating customers, and thus other

customers without clean energy goals will not be subject to increased costs as a result.

Authorizing incremental solar procurement now - via a program that will be fully paid for by

participating customers and that will not affect the remaining customer base - is a least-regrets

move the Commission can take to at least partially offset the impacts of the Plan to customers’

carbon footprint.

                                                27

       Second, if the Commission is to approve the MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs, it should reject

additional sums in all years of the PPAs as unnecessary and inappropriate incremental costs to

existing customers. As noted above, the Company has made much of the essential need for these

PPAs to serve new anticipated system load and that the urgency of these needs made a

competitive acquisition impossible. If this is the case, then the Company needs no additional

incentive to procure these resources. Further, it is inappropriate for GPC customers to pay an

additional sum on assets that other Southern Company customers are already paying for in base

rates in another state. Rejecting the additional sum will help ensure that the acquisition of these

resources is not financially harmful to GPC customers even if the magnitude of load the

Company projects does not materialize in the timeframe expected.

       J.       THE COMMISSION SHOULD REQUIRE MODELING REFORMS AND
                ADDITIONAL REPORTING IN THE COMPANY’S NEXT IRP

       In alignment with the recommendations made by other parties to this docket, DOD/FEA

believe that GPC can do better to improve alignment between its modeling and the ultimately

proposed Plan, as the most prudent path forward in light of all evaluated opportunities and risks.

These recommendations span modeling inputs, scenario analysis and outputs, and reporting

requirements.

       As noted in the testimony of several intervenors, the Company inappropriately relied on

outdated studies for key inputs to its modeling and proposed plan - in particular the Planning

Reserve Margin. Especially given the magnitude of new, high load factor customers upon which

the Company has based this interim IRP request, the PRM study should have been refreshed to

incorporate new load shapes and updated capacity costs and used to either revise or re-validate

the Company’s proposed PRM (which, it should be noted, is not a settled matter in this


                                                 28

proceeding). A PRM as high as 26 percent in the winter has significant impacts on the types and

magnitude of resources proposed and this core assumption should be more thoroughly vetted.

Further, if these inputs will not be refreshed, the Company should provide well-reasoned

justifications for why that will not result in negative outcomes for customers. Lack of time is not

an appropriate justification when the outcome of an IRP will be thousands of new, emitting

resources that will be recovered in customer rates.

       Record testimony indicates that Georgia Power did not appropriately capture the

increased capacity value of solar and battery storage when added in combination, relative to their

standalone values, and thus undervalued the solar and storage resources modeled relative to other

types of generation. As Mr. Olson notes, GPC’s capacity limits on renewable energy and

energy storage are arbitrary and unsupported by technical or economic feasibility studies. Mr.

Olson recommends that GPC assign solar resources a calculated ELCC value consistently across

its modeling practices that is informed by its ELCC study. With respect to the proposed

stipulation’s approval of the MPC PPA and the Santa Rosa Energy Center PPA, Mr. Olsen notes

that the results of GPC’s RFI indicated that GPC could potentially have met its near-term needs

with energy storage or solar in the 2025-2028 period, but that the lack of a standard, regular,

competitive bid solicitation process disallowed any opportunity for GPC to explore the optimal

solution to meet their needs by evaluating bids alongside each other or in relation to the MPC

and Santa Rosa PPAs. DOD-FEA respectfully requests that the Commission include in any final

order in this docket a directive to GPC to clearly demonstrate in its next IRP filing that it

appropriately accredits the capacity value of CFE and considers CFE solutions along with other

resource options on a fair and equal footing.


                                                 29

       The Commission should further clarify how ELCC for variable renewables and duration

limited resources is to be used to ensure the Company appropriately applies it to its planning.

Finally, as discussed above, the Company should more appropriately account for thermal asset

forced outage rates in its resource accreditation values. If these are not incorporated, the

Company risks overestimating its thermal resources’ contribution toward meeting load

requirements, especially during winter weather events.

       K.      THE COMPANY SHOULD PRESENT A CLEAR AND DOCUMENTED
               COMPARATIVE SCENARIO ANALYSIS IN ITS NEXT IRP,
               INCLUDING ANY PROPOSED COAL RETIREMENT CHANGES

       In addition to input analysis improvements, the Commission should require GPC to

provide additional, publicly available data on various scenario analyses and both the capacity

expansion and cost differences between these scenarios, how to interpret the different results,

and how they relate to the plan the Company ultimately puts forward. This is essential if the

Company proposes to postpone its previously approved coal retirements.

       The Company did not present clear and coherent scenario analysis. The Company needs

to provide additional modeling scenarios and sensitivities that isolate impacts of variable changes

so parties and the Commission can understand cost and risk deltas between the Company’s

proposed Base Case scenario and alternate cases. DOD/FEA makes the following

recommendations:

                   ● Require GPC to model different expansion plans when underlying load or
                     resources are varied (i.e. different load sensitivities and current plant
                     retirement dates, PRMs or ELCC inputs, etc.)
                   ● Require GPC to model different operational cost inputs - such as fuel
                     prices or carbon regulation compliance costs - on the same capacity
                     expansion plan, to isolate the impacts of potential future uncertainty on
                     each modeled capacity expansion plan


                                                 30

                   ● Provide a thorough, publicly available accounting of all input assumptions
                     and net present value system costs, resulting capacity and energy dispatch
                     mix, explanations of how each scenario and sensitivity varies from the
                     Base Case, and additional context regarding how to interpret differences in
                     system costs or operational outputs
                   ● In any case where the Company is proposing to postpone previously
                     approved coal retirement dates, require that the company perform a
                     specific alternatives analysis that allows parties to assess the cost and
                     performance of an alternate future in which the coal facility’s resource
                     attributes are replaced with non-emitting generation resources and/or
                     transmission investments.

       If the Commission requires this enhanced analysis and information, Staff and parties will

be better able to vet the results and trust that GPC is putting forward the best possible plan for

the future of its system and customers.

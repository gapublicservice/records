Q.WHAT IS THE PURPOSE OF YOUR REBUTTAL TESTIMONY?
A.My testimony (i) explains why Georgia Power projects the 2023 IRP Update to place downward pressure on rates for customers, and (ii) addresses specific accounting aspects of the Company’s request.   
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE YOUR REBUTTAL TESTIMONY.
A.As outlined in the direct testimony of Jeffrey R. Grubb, Francisco Valle, Lee Evans, and Michael A. Bush (the “Company Direct Panel”), the Company is requesting, among other things, 3,300 MW of capacity resources in the form of Power Purchase Agreements (“PPAs”), three simple cycle combustion turbine (“CT”) resources at Plant Yates, and battery energy storage system (“BESS”) resources. These capacity resources are necessary to meet the substantial energy needs being driven by the extraordinary pace and magnitude of economic development in Georgia. Importantly, the Public Interest Advocacy (“PIA”) Staff and other parties to this case acknowledge that this growth is very real. As further explained by the Company Direct Panel, based on our forecast and in-depth understanding of our customers’ energy needs and plans, we are confident that this growth will materialize in the 2026-2028 timeframe, and the capacity resources requested in the Company’s 2023 IRP Update will ensure Georgia Power can continue to reliably serve our customers and support Georgia’s growing economy.  
The 2023 IRP Update will provide substantial benefits to customers, and we expect that the revenues associated with energy sales from incremental large load customers will put downward pressure on rates for all customers, including residential and small business customers. If approved, the 2023 IRP Update is projected to provide approximately $168 million of levelized net benefit annually for customers for 2026 through 2028, or more than $500 million of net benefit on a cumulative basis for the three years. This benefit would put estimated downward pressure on total retail rates of approximately 1.6% as a key variable in our next base rate case filing. The Company expects that the projected revenues associated with energy sales from large load customers will exceed the revenue requirement associated with the incremental resources requested to serve the forecasted increase in peak demand materializing in 2026-2028. Downward pressure on rates would be achieved for all retail customers when, as a part of our next base rate case, Georgia Power would use the net excess revenues resulting from the 2023 IRP Update to decrease the impact of other revenue requirements when calculating the requested rate adjustments. 
The regulatory asset deferrals and additional sum requested by the Company in this case are appropriate and should be approved. The deferrals will allow the costs and benefits from the proposed resources to be concurrently recognized in the year the resources are needed. Consistent with existing regulatory assets and regulatory liabilities previously approved by this Commission, the costs related to the 2023 IRP Update stem from unforeseen circumstances that could not have been accounted for during our last base rate case. These costs could not have been, nor were they, contemplated when the Commission established the Company’s current base rates. The additional sum requested by the Company in this case is appropriate and consistent with the amount per kW recently approved by the Commission.   
As acknowledged by Staff, the Company considered a wide array of federal incentives made available by the Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”) as part of its request. (Tr. 519-521.) This includes considering the IRA’s Domestic Content Bonus Credit and Energy Community Bonus Credit as part of the selection of resources and identifying specific BESS projects that incorporate the benefits of the IRA. The Company will continue to pursue additional opportunities to leverage these and other federal incentives to lower costs for customers.
If the Commission approves the Company’s request and load growth materializes as the Company expects, Georgia Power will be able to serve all of its customers—both current customers and anticipated future customers—while supporting Georgia’s growing economy and related energy needs. If load growth does not materialize as expected, both the Company and the Commission have multiple levers available to manage the timing and extent of generation resources and related costs to customers. 
In this request, Georgia Power has sought to balance the need created by projected growing peak energy demands with the timing of the generation resources necessary to support Georgia’s extraordinary growth and economic development. This Commission’s long-standing history of forward-thinking and constructive regulation is highly correlated to Georgia's continued ability to attract and retain businesses – ranging from technology and manufacturing to small business – and without approval from the Commission to acquire these necessary capacity resources, Georgia Power would be unable to serve its existing and growing customer base with the reliability customers value and expect. 
At Georgia Power, our customers are at the center of everything we do, and we are unwavering in our commitment to provide them with clean, safe, reliable, and affordable energy. Approval of our 2023 IRP Update will preserve and protect the reliability and quality of service our customers expect and support the continued economic development of our state, while placing downward pressure on rates for the benefit of all customers.

II.DOWNWARD PRESSURE ON RATES FOR ALL CUSTOMERS
Q.PLEASE EXPLAIN HOW, IF APPROVED, THE 2023 IRP UPDATE WILL PUT DOWNWARD PRESSURE ON RATES.
A.The incremental forecasted load projected in the 2023 IRP Update would result in additional revenues that will benefit all retail customers in future rate adjustments. When those revenues exceed the revenue requirement, or costs, this creates excess revenues that helps to offset other costs in the computation of rate adjustments. 
Georgia Power filed its 2023 IRP Update to serve the extraordinary energy demand of Georgia’s economic growth. Although there is a cost to serve this increased and accelerated demand, there is also a benefit that materializes through increased energy sales and revenues. In this case, with this request, the incremental revenue benefits outweigh the costs. Downward pressure on retail rates occurs when the costs associated with the resources requested in the 2023 IRP Update are more than offset by the projected energy sales’ revenues associated with the large load customers. 
Q.HOW DOES THE 2023 IRP UPDATE IMPACT THE COMPANY’S REVENUE REQUIREMENT?
A.As shown in Figure 1 below, in the context of Georgia Power’s upcoming 2025 base rate case, and assuming a three-year Alternate Rate Plan for the years 2026, 2027, and 2028, the revenues from the incremental large customer loads projected by the Company are expected to exceed the revenue requirement associated with the capacity resources requested in the 2023 IRP Update. Consistent with the Company’s typical practice, Georgia Power would include both the projected revenues and the costs associated with these resources when calculating the rate adjustments in our next base rate case and would use the net excess revenues related to these resources to reduce the impact of other revenue requirements, placing downward pressure on rates for all retail customers.

Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE THE REVENUE REQUIREMENT COMPONENTS IN FIGURE 1.
A.The amounts presented in Figure 1 represent projected net revenue requirements for 2026 through 2028 on a levelized basis. The projected retail base revenues for incremental large load customers in Figure 1 are consistent with the revenues presented in the Company’s response to STF-DEA-3-6. These projected revenues include estimated energy sales growth of nearly 9% over the next three to five years, with data centers representing more than 80% of that growth based on our most recent data. The revenue requirement blocks represent the projected revenue requirements of the capacity resources proposed in the 2023 IRP Update. As depicted in Figure 1, the projected result of the incremental revenues and the revenue requirement associated with the capacity resources is a levelized net benefit to customers of approximately $168 million, which would equate to a cumulative three-year net benefit of more than $500 million attributable to the Company’s request in the 2023 IRP Update. This $500 million net benefit would help to offset other costs and mitigate, or lessen, rate adjustments for customers.  Figure 1 clearly demonstrates the basis for the Company’s expected downward pressure on customer rates.
Q.WHAT GIVES YOU CONFIDENCE THAT THE LOAD FROM THESE LARGE LOAD CUSTOMERS IS GOING TO MATERIALIZE IN THE 2026-2028 TIMEFRAME? 
A.As Mr. Valle will explain further, Georgia Power’s confidence is founded on robust forecasting methodologies, strong economic development activity, customer activity data, and customer commitments. The 2023 IRP Update is informed by current economic trends and utilizes established forecasting methods to predict the likelihood, scale, and timing of the projected load increases. Notably, PIA Staff and the Intervenors submitting testimony on the issue have not challenged the Company’s organic load forecast and the statistical approach for anticipating growth from new large load customers. (Tr. 89, lines 10-11; Tr. 1190, lines 8-9.)
Georgia Power’s forecast is realistic and appropriately risk adjusted. With a long-term economic development pipeline capacity of 21,000 MW in committed and potential projects by the mid-2030’s, our near-term winter 2028/2029 economic development pipeline capacity is now estimated at nearly 14,200 MW, up from the 11,600 MW at the time of our initial filing in October 2023. Consistent with our past practices, we are estimating that Georgia Power will ultimately serve just a portion of all anticipated economic development projects, and the 2023 IRP Update forecasts Georgia Power’s winter of 2028/2029 peak demand will be approximately 5,260 MW higher than the winter peak of 2023/2024.
At the time of our initial filing, Georgia Power was chosen by fourteen large load customers, securing agreements accounting for 3,612 MW of load, with 2,810 MW anticipated by winter 2028/2029. Nine of these projects have initiated construction, four are pending construction, and one 66 MW project is delayed. The trend of robust economic activity has continued, and since our initial filing, Georgia Power has secured an additional 2,602 MW from committed customers contributing 1,647 MW by winter 2028/2029. This information clearly indicates that these large load customers are materializing and will need to be served sooner rather than later. 
All signs, including direct input from customers, support Georgia Power’s expectation for continued robust economic growth in Georgia and the timing of new large customers, and further my confidence that the load from these large load customers will indeed materialize in the 2026-2028 timeframe.

A.With the 2023 IRP Update, the Company expects continued downward pressure on rates for all retail customers beyond 2028. If approved, the cost of the proposed resources requested in this filing would be more than offset by the revenues from the forecasted incremental load beyond 2028. Since the revenue requirement associated with the Company-owned resources is expected to decline over time, the forecasted revenues from the incremental large customer loads included in this request are expected to continue to exceed the total revenue requirement of the resources included in the 2023 IRP Update.
Q.WHAT LEVERS ARE AVAILABLE TO THE COMPANY AND THE COMMISSION TO MITIGATE THE RISK OF RATE PRESSURE IF THE LOAD GROWTH DOES NOT MATERIALIZE AS PROJECTED?
A.Let me first reiterate that we are confident the load will materialize as projected and that the requested resources will be critical to ensuring Georgia Power is able to serve its existing and growing customer base with the reliability customers value and expect. However, even if the forecasted load does not materialize as we expect, there are multiple levers in place to mitigate the risk of upward pressure on rates. 
The IRP framework provides ample opportunities to adjust our resource plan in subsequent IRP cycles by reducing future investments in generation resources. First, the framework allows us to adjust procurement plans for the resources requested in this proceeding based on the most recent load forecast at the time resources are submitted for certification. For example, the Company could reduce the capacity procured for 2029 through 2031 through the All-Source Capacity RFP, which was approved in the 2022 IRP. Second, the Company can make sales in the wholesale market for any capacity above its target reserve margin to reduce the cost obligation for customers, just as we did to secure off-System sale related to the MPC PPA. Third, the Company could slow, reduce, or halt the development of BESS resources if load growth is slower than expected or fails to materialize. .
III.ACCOUNTING MATTERS
A.Deferrals
Q.WHY IS THE COMPANY’S REQUEST TO DEFER CERTAIN COSTS TO REGULATORY ASSETS APPROPRIATE?
A.The Company is seeking regulatory asset treatment for the following: (i) capacity and non-fuel energy payments made under the MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs in 2024 and 2025 (including additional sum and net of amounts remarketed to third parties); (ii) non-useful and non-transferable development costs associated with the requested BESS systems and Yates Units 8-10 CTs if the Commission denies these requests; and (iii) the development costs associated with the Flex Capacity framework that would otherwise be expensed for recovery in the next base rate case. The deferral of these costs to a regulatory asset is appropriate because both the costs and benefits from these capacity resources should be concurrently recognized in the year the resources are needed. Like existing regulatory assets and regulatory liabilities that were previously approved by this Commission, the costs related to the 2023 IRP Update stem from unforeseen and extraordinary circumstances. These costs could not have been, nor were they, contemplated when the Commission established the Company’s current base rates. 
Q.HAS THE COMMISSION ROUTINELY GRANTED DEFERRAL REQUESTS OUTSIDE OF A BASE RATE CASE?
A.Yes. The Commission has approved deferrals to regulatory assets numerous times in an IRP. For example, in the 2019 IRP, the Commission approved deferrals for Plant Hammond Units 1-4, Plant McIntosh Unit 1, Plant Estatoah Unit 1, Plant Langdale Units 5-6, and Plant Riverview Units 1-2. In the 2016 IRP, the Commission approved a deferral associated with Plant Mitchell Unit 3. In the 2013 IRP, the Commission approved a deferral for Plant Branch Units 3-4 and Plant Boulevard Units 2-3. In the 2011 IRP Update, the Commission approved deferrals for Plant Branch Units 1-2 and Plant Mitchell Unit 4C.
In addition to deferrals granted in the Company’s IRPs, the Commission approved cost deferrals in 2020 related to the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as the deferral and subsequent disbursement of hundreds of millions of dollars in customer refunds during 2018 and 2019 resulting from the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. Notably, these deferrals were approved during the three-year ARP periods for items that were not contemplated in the respective base rate cases.
Q.DO THE DEFERRALS REQUESTED BY THE COMPANY IN THIS CASE PLACE UPWARD PRESSURE ON RATES?
A.No. As previously mentioned, when the incremental revenues from the additional large customer load presented in this filing are properly considered in conjunction with the total revenue requirement of the capacity resources requested in this filing, including the cost of the deferrals, the revenues are expected to exceed the revenue requirement, putting downward pressure on rates for customers.  
These deferral requests are directly associated with the capacity resources necessary to serve our customers. Absent the efforts taken by the Company to secure these resources to serve peak demand starting in 2026, Georgia Power would be unable to serve its existing and growing customer base with the reliability customers value and expect. 
Q.CAN THE COMPANY WAIT UNTIL THE 2025 BASE RATE CASE TO MAKE A DEFERRAL REQUEST?
A.No. The costs being deferred will be incurred in 2024 and 2025, and the Company will need to account for those costs in that time period. Without Commission approval to defer the costs, the Company will be required to expense these costs in the Company’s financial statements during that period. The Company cannot wait to request deferral treatment in the 2025 Base Rate Case because, even if the Commission were to grant the deferral in that case, the Company could not retroactively reflect the deferral treatment in its financials for the periods when the costs were incurred.
B.Additional Sum
Q.ARE POWER PURCHASE AGREEMENTS REQUIRED TO BE PROCURED THROUGH AN RFP TO QUALIFY FOR AN ADDITIONAL SUM?
A.No. The IRP Act authorizes an additional sum on any long-term power purchase, and only instructs the Commission to consider lost revenues, if any, changed risks, and an equitable sharing of benefits between the utility and its retail customers when setting the additional sum. The purpose of the additional sum is to provide an incentive to the Company to encourage long-term power purchases in lieu of building or buying generation assets on which it can earn a return. Contrary to Georgia Association of Manufacturers Witness Pollock’s assertion that the statutory criteria for the Company to recover an additional sum is unmet, it is not a requirement that the Company procure the purchased power through an RFP for it to be eligible for an additional sum. More importantly, the additional sum incents the Company to act for the benefit of its customers to secure capacity, whether Company-owned or power purchases, when that capacity is needed. This is exactly what the Company has done in this case. 
Q.WHY IS IT APPROPRIATE FOR THE COMPANY TO RECOVER THE ADDITIONAL SUMS ASSOCIATED WITH THE MPC AND SANTA ROSA PPAS?
A.The MPC and Santa Rosa PPAs are needed to serve Georgia Power customers. Commission Staff acknowledges this fact. (Tr. 248, lines 11-12; 297, line 10; 298, lines 1-3.) The Company’s request for an additional sum in this case is consistent with the treatment of other Georgia Power long-term power purchases needed to serve our customers’ capacity needs. Further, the amount per kW-year requested is consistent with what was approved by the Commission for the six capacity PPAs certified in the 2022 IRP, of which, five were affiliated PPAs. Although some Intervenors have argued that the Company should not receive an additional sum for a PPA with an affiliate (Tr. 939, lines 21-23), there is no statutory restriction on the Company’s ability to receive an additional sum for a long-term power purchase with an affiliate. To the contrary, Commission practice has been to routinely allow additional sums for affiliate PPAs.   
C.Federal Incentives
Q.WHAT FEDERAL INCENTIVES DID THE COMPANY CONSIDER AS PART OF ITS REQUEST?
A.The Company considered a wide array of federal incentives as part of its request. As stated by Staff Witnesses Smith and Deitchman in their testimony, “the Company was aware of and considered many of the tax incentives that have been made available by the IRA.” (Tr. 484, lines 19-20.) Witnesses Smith and Deitchman also acknowledged this during the February 28 hearing. (Tr. 624, lines 13-15.) They note that the Company considered the IRA’s Domestic Content Bonus Credit and Energy Community Bonus Credit as part of the selection of resources and identified the BESS projects that incorporate the benefits of the IRA (Tr. 520-521.) Specifically, these projects, some of which include co-located solar projects, qualify for certain investment tax credits and production tax credits, the benefit of which were included in the Company’s economic evaluation for the 2023 IRP Update.
Q.WILL THE COMPANY PURSUE ADDITIONAL OPPORTUNITIES TO LEVERAGE FEDERAL INCENTIVES?
A.Yes. The Company is continually pursuing new and evolving opportunities to leverage federal incentives, including financing through DOE Loan Guarantees, tax incentives, and other credits to lower costs for customers. 
IV.CONCLUSION
Q.WHY SHOULD THE COMMISSION APPROVE THE COMPANY’S REQUEST AS FILED?
A.At Georgia Power, the customer is at the center of everything we do. We are unwavering in our commitment to provide our customers with clean, safe, reliable, and affordable energy. Our request in this 2023 IRP Update seeks to continue delivering on our customer commitment, as the forecasted load growth and resulting need for additional capacity presents a significant opportunity to continue to reliably serve our customers. Approval of our 2023 IRP Update will preserve and protect the valuable reliability and quality of service both our current and future customers expect and will support the continued economic development of Georgia, while placing downward pressure on rates for the benefit of all customers. 
Georgia’s continued ability to attract and retain businesses - ranging from technology and manufacturing to small business - is highly correlated to the Commission’s long-standing history of forward-thinking and constructive regulation, which facilitates Georgia Power’s ability to provide customers with clean, safe, reliable, and affordable energy. We respectfully request that the Commission approve the Company’s 2023 IRP Update without modification. 
Q.DOES THIS CONCLUDE YOUR TESTIMONY? 
A.Yes.

Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE THE REBUTTAL TESTIMONY OF THE PANEL.
A.As demonstrated by the load forecast in the 2023 IRP Update, Georgia is experiencing extraordinary economic growth that has significantly impacted the magnitude and timing of Georgia Power’s capacity needs. A substantial portion of this rapid economic growth is expected to occur over the next five years. The Company must act expeditiously to address the significant energy needs associated with this economic growth and to ensure it continues to maintain an electric System that can reliably meet the needs of customers and our thriving state. 
Between the conclusion of the 2022 IRP and the filing of the 2023 IRP Update, Georgia Power was selected to serve more than 3,350 megawatts (“MW”) of new, large load customers, representing approximately 34 years of traditional customer choice growth in just over one year. While substantial, this growth represents only a fraction of the potential load growth from Georgia’s economic development pipeline. In fact, since the 2023 IRP Update was filed in October of 2023, the economic development pipeline has continued to grow and has now expanded to more than 21,000 MW. In addition, the Company has now been selected to serve an additional 2,602 MW since its initial filing in this proceeding. What’s more, several customers representing 2,173 MW of the new large load included in the 2023 IRP Update Forecast have now accelerated their load ramps, which would incorporate their entire load by 2031. These changes since October 2023 evidence the continued economic growth we are seeing in Georgia, continued customer interest in being served by Georgia Power, and the need to act now to reliably meet our customers’ demand for energy as it continues to grow and accelerate. Moreover, it underscores the need to plan for a P95 load forecast, a load level supported by PIA Staff, to ensure the Company has sufficient resources to serve Georgia’s substantial energy needs that are being driven by economic growth.
No party has reasonably challenged that the overall increase in demand is real and coming to the state. The only point of disagreement is the magnitude of the increase and the timing in which the projected large loads will materialize. In doing so, GIPL asserted that the P50 value is, statistically speaking, the most likely outcome and should, therefore, be used in lieu of the P95 value. Using Figure 7 from GIPL Witness Hotaling’s testimony, you can see that the P50 value occurs at the peak of the bell curve on the distribution of possible outcomes. However, as shown in the modified Figure 7 graphic below, electing to use the P50 value for the large load forecast means that there is a 50% chance that loads exceed the predicted value, as seen by the red shaded area under the curve. Simply put, if Georgia Power were to plan its System using the P50 value, that would mean there is a 50% chance that it would have too few resources to meet large load customer needs, and therefore would not be able to reliably support the demands of new economic load growth.

Thus, it was not until June 2023 that the Company detected a change in the Company’s capacity need year (i.e., the year in which the Company will require additional resources moved from winter 2028/2029 to winter 2026/2027). It was this change that necessitated the development of the 2023 IRP Update to procure resources to meet an even earlier capacity need beginning in the winter of 2025/2026 based on the final load forecast from September 2023.

Other than remarketing capacity, the Company can also manage the development timeline of approved resources or the procurement amount from approved RFPs. IV. THE REQUEST FOR PROPOSAL PROCESS
Q.WHY DID THE COMPANY REQUEST AN EXCEPTION TO THE COMMISSION’S RFP PROCESS IN THE IRP UPDATE?
A.Due to the Company’s capacity need in the winters of 2025/2026, 2026/2027, and 2027/2028, there simply isn’t enough time to use an RFP, which typically takes two to two-and-a-half years to implement, evaluate, contract, and certify resources to meet the need. This is especially true in a constrained market with few existing resources available for contracting as additional time is needed to construct new resources and any necessary supporting transmission projects. Therefore, the Company requested to procure resources pursuant to one or more of the RFP exceptions to ensure it can serve the customer load identified in its forecast and continue to maintain a reliable electric System. 
Q. DOES GEORGIA POWER SUPPORT THE COMMISSION’S RFP PROCESS? 
A. Yes. Georgia Power supports the Commission’s RFP process and has conducted over 20 RFPs since 2006. The Company is currently developing and implementing numerous RFPs, including the All-Source Capacity RFP, CARES US RFP, the Biomass RFP, the DG RFP, and the 500 MW ESS RFP that were approved in the 2022 IRP. The Company fully intends to continue implementing its current RFPs and utilizing the RFP process in procuring generation resources in the future.
Q.WHY CAN’T THE COMPANY RELY ON EXISTING RFPS TO PROCURE THE RESOURCES REQUIRED IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE?
A.The Company needs resources online to meet the capacity need in the winters of 2025/2026, 2026/2027 and 2027/2028, and the RFP process typically takes two to two-and-a-half years to identify, evaluate (including associated transmission), select, and certify capacity resources. Any new-build resources selected through such an RFP would not only have to be identified on an expedited timeline but constructed and brought online as early as November 2025 through November 2027, which is simply not feasible. Moreover, there is no guarantee that the market would offer a sufficient quantity of firm, dispatchable resources in response to an RFP to provide the required capacity in the timeframe needed. Also, the existing RFPs are crucial to meeting the needs identified in the 2022 IRP for renewable energy resources, ESS resources, and capacity resources for the needs in the 2029-2031 timeframe and therefore must be conducted to best meet those specific needs.

Ultimately, this decision does not have to be made in this case. The Company plans to give the appropriate capacity credit to any storage resources that are selected in the 2023 CARES US RFP. If Staff’s prediction comes to fruition, the Company and the Commission can act at the time of certification, when the resources procured through the 2023 US RFP are known, to reduce the amount of capacity resources the Company needs to procure in a future RFP such as the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP.

V.TARGET RESERVE MARGIN
Q.WHAT TARGET RESERVE MARGIN DID THE COMPANY USE IN THE 2023 IRP UPDATE?
A.Consistent with the 2022 IRP Final Order, the Company used a 26% winter target reserve margin for planning purposes. Planning for 26% in the long term and 25.5% in the short term resulted in an 80% increase in reliability in the Company’s 2021 Reserve Margin Study as compared to a 20% reserve margin, which was the 1:10 Loss of Load Expectation (“LOLE”) threshold. The Company’s winter target reserve margin served the state well in recent extreme weather events, such as Winter Storm Elliott on Christmas Eve in 2022 and Winter Storm Heather, which occurred earlier this year on the second day of direct hearings in this case. Planning to a 26% winter target reserve margin ensures that the Company is prepared to handle reliability events such as these, even as customer behavior evolves over time. Further, maintaining a 26% target reserve margin lowers expected customer costs as compared to a 20% reserve margin per the 2021 Reserve Margin Study, which considers not only capacity costs, but also production costs and reliability costs.
Q.IS THERE A GENERAL TREND IN THE INDUSTRY TOWARD WINTER RESERVE MARGINS?
A. Yes. Several utilities are now either establishing winter reserve margins or are starting to plan for higher winter reserve margins. Duke Energy Carolinas, Duke Energy Progress, Santee Cooper, MISO, SPP, and PJM have either increased their winter target reserve margins or established winter target reserve margins in the last few years. Decreasing the Company’s reserve margin would be a step in the opposite direction compared to the industry and would reduce Georgia Power’s System reliability.
Q.WHY IS STAFF’S PROPOSED 24.5% TARGET RESERVE MARGIN INAPPROPRIATE?
A.Customers benefit from the enhanced reliability offered by planning to a winter target reserve margin of 26% for very little additional cost. Planning to a lower target reserve margin like the 24.5% recommended by PIA Staff reduces System reliability and does not provide substantial cost savings. If the Company planned to a 24.5% winter target reserve margin, the System’s performance during extreme winter weather events like Winter Storm Elliott would have been diminished. Also, Staff’s recommendation in the 2022 IRP for 24.5% sought to remove the risk of under forecasting loads from the Reserve Margin Study, while only planning for risks associated with over forecasting loads. Planning for only one direction of movement on something like a load forecast is inappropriate because it fails to sufficiently capture the reliability risk. The 2023 IRP Update is itself strong evidence that loads can be higher than forecasted.
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO INTERVENOR RECOMMENDATIONS THAT RE-RUNNING THE RESERVE MARGIN STUDY WOULD PLACE DOWNWARD PRESSURE ON THE ECONOMICS OF RESOURCES CONSIDERED BY THE COMPANY.
A.Witness Hotaling suggested that higher current capacity prices might reduce the Target Reserve Margin down to the 20% threshold for 1:10 LOLE. (Tr. 1286, lines 15–17.) While capacity prices do impact the economic evaluation in a Reserve Margin Study, it would be inappropriate to update a single variable without considering how other variables may have also changed. The assumption that a higher capacity price could result in a lower Economic Optimum Reserve Margin (“EORM”) also ignores the Value at Risk component to the Company’s Reserve Margin Study, which considers the value to customers for a reserve margin higher than the EORM. The Company will update all the relevant variables and perform a full Reserve Margin Study that will be filed as part of its 2025 IRP. At that time, the impact of capacity price changes can be properly evaluated in the context of all other changes in the study, including any updates to the level of reserve margin associated with a 1:10 LOLE.
Q.SHOULD THE COMPANY SIMPLY REDUCE ITS WINTER TARGET RESERVE MARGIN TO 20%, EQUIVALENT TO THE 1 IN 10 LOLE, AS RECOMMENDED BY PIA STAFF AND SOME INTERVENORS? 
A.No. The Company does not recommend reducing the winter target reserve margin below the 26% approved for planning purposes. Although some utilities plan to a level of reliability associated with a 1:10 LOLE, the Company considers that to be a minimum threshold, and also considers the costs and risks to customers when the target reserve margin is set. Lowering the winter target reserve margin to 20% would result in a reduced level of reliability, on par with or even below other regions that had firm load shed during recent winter events. Lowering the reserve margin to 20% would result in an 80% increase in the likelihood the Company would need to shed firm load, and the loss of firm load would be expected to be twice as severe, with a 110% higher amount of Expected Unserved Energy (“EUE”). With a 20% target reserve margin, the Company would be twice as reliant on interruptible loads and would expect higher generation production costs and purchased power costs. This lower level of reliability without consideration of costs and risks is not what the Company endeavors to provide its customers. System wide, adoption of a 20% winter planning reserve margin reduces the System capacity by approximately 1,800 MW. This 1,800 MW, embedded in the Company’s 26% winter target reserve margin, was necessary to maintain reliability during Winter Storm Elliott, and there is no evidence to the contrary.
VI. PROPOSED RESOURCE SOLUTIONS
Staff Economic Analysis
Q.Please describe Staff’s Economic Analysis and Resource Mix Analysis.
A.PIA Staff conducted a review of the resources proposed by the Company. Their review encompassed two primary analyses – a Resource Mix Study and a Ranking Analysis – utilizing the Aurora optimization model, within the defined study period of 2024 to 2040, which was used to expedite simulation times. The Resource Mix Study was conducted to determine the optimal selection of resources. This study was also intended to address the perceived shortcomings in the Company’s economic analysis. In reevaluating the proposed resource investments, PIA Staff aimed to ascertain the necessity and economic viability of procurement of the Company’s proposed resources. The analysis purported to weigh the proposed new resources against generic candidate units. Additionally, an economic ranking analysis was carried out, positioning the Company’s identified resources on a spectrum from most to least favorable. This supplementary analysis was intended to provide the Commission with a structured reference should there be a requirement to pursue additional resources beyond the PIA Staff’s endorsement. 
Q.Does the Company have concerns with PIA Staff’s Economic Analysis and Resource Mix Study used in the 2023 IRP Update? 
A.Yes. The Company has several reservations regarding PIA Staff’s Economic Analysis and Resource Mix Study in the 2023 IRP Update. The extent of the issues signals to the Company that PIA Staff’s findings are flawed and merit no consideration by the Commission. The identified discrepancies and oversights in PIA Staff’s analysis are in the following key points: 
The abbreviated study duration ending in 2040 inadvertently skews results and biases the model against all new construction proposed by the Company, primarily because it fails to account for the complete operational lifespan of the proposed new construction Company-owned assets. This short study period also precipitated fundamental mistakes producing inaccurate results. 
PIA Staff’s analysis relied on generic units that are not feasible for construction within the time frame of 2026-2028, yet they were assumed to be available regardless. 
The conditions set by PIA Staff only allowed for the selection of new Company-owned proposals at their explicit Commercial Operation Dates (“COD”), in stark contrast to the ongoing availability afforded to generic alternatives, which could be selected at any time. This discrepancy, combined with an error related to the incorrect entry of the CODs for Plant Yates Units 8-10, hindered the model’s ability to consider the Company's proposed resources appropriately, effectively excluding Yates Units 8-10 from consideration.
Finally, the approach taken did not consider variability of natural gas, ignoring the low and high gas price scenarios. 
Q.Please explain the impact of the shorter study period.
A.The truncated study period chosen by PIA Staff has considerable implications, not the least of which is the disregard for the long-term benefits that Company-owned assets with extended lifespans could offer customers. By arbitrarily setting 2040 as the study cutoff, PIA Staff’s economic analysis fails to account for unit performance and cost savings that would accrue from assets like Plant Yates Units 8-10, the proposed BESS projects, and the proposed BESS plus Solar project—all of which are anticipated to operate beyond the end date assumed by PIA Staff. 
PIA Staff’s methodological oversight in adjusting for the shortened study period also introduced significant errors, particularly in the assessment of Combined Cycle units with carbon capture and sequestration (“CC with CCS”). PIA Staff’s selection of a substantial volume of CC with CCS in the moderate gas, zero-dollar carbon scenario (“MG0”) raises questions about the legitimacy of their analysis. By limiting the final study year to 2040 in PIA Staff’s analysis, the availability of the 45Q tax credit — which is applicable only for the first 12 years of CC with CCS operation — was not correctly factored into the long-term economic model. Consequently, the stark cost increases that are expected to follow the 45Q tax credit’s expiration were disregarded in PIA Staff’s analysis. This oversight signifies broader analytical inaccuracies and undervalues the perceived benefits of the resources proposed by the Company, which in turn has contributed to PIA Staff’s recommendation to reject these resources. 
Q.Did Staff’s Resource Mix Analysis consider adding the proposed Plant Yates Units 8-10? 
A.No. PIA Staff’s analysis included a combination of errors that prevented the evaluation of Plant Yates Units 8-10 in the Resource Mix Analysis. Notably, the allowed selection dates for Plant Yates Units 8-9 and Unit 10 were incorrectly set to 1/1/2026 and 1/1/2027, respectively. In addition, there was no capacity modeled for Unit 9 in 2026, as the actual projected COD is in 2027, and only one month of capacity was modeled for Unit 8. PIA Staff artificially limited the opportunity for the model to pick Plant Yates Units 8-10 exclusively to the year of the entered selection dates and before any winter capacity contributions could be recognized by the model. Such limited parameters led to the Aurora model’s optimization algorithm dismissing the Yates Units outright without sufficient consideration. 
In response to hearing requests, PIA Staff acknowledged that allowing the Yates CTs the flexibility to be chosen in years beyond 2026 and 2027 would have resulted in the selection of Plant Yates Unit 8 in 2028, even without extending the study period. 
Q.CAN Generic Resources MEET CAPACITY NEEDS in 2026-2028? 
A.No. Asserting that generic resources could meet the Company’s capacity requirements in 2026-2028 is fundamentally inappropriate. Generic resources lack defined specifics such as location, transmission interconnection and deliverability, land and fuel availability, finalized engineering plans, and the necessary environmental permits.
The utilization of these hypothetical constructs in place of the Company’s proposed projects undermines reliability, as generic resources cannot be built swiftly enough (if at all) to meet imminent capacity needs. A more reasonable approach would bypass the use of generics from 2026-2028, focusing solely on actual, executable resources.
Q. DID THE COMPANY COMPLETE AN ANALYSIS CORRECTING FOR STAFF’S ERRORS? 
A. Yes. The Company modified PIA Staff’s modeling, which used the Company’s load forecast, to correct for the errors described above to conduct an updated analysis. Specifically, the Company’s analysis extends the study period to 2058 and corrects the limitation on Plant Yates Unit 8-10 CODs. This recalibration not only rectifies the CC with CCS error but also provides a more accurate representation of new construction resource lifespans.
The results achieved by using PIA Staff’s own model—with the Company’s corrections—led to the selection of previously excluded projects, including the Robins and Moody BESS projects and Plant Yates Units 8-10. These outcomes demonstrate that these projects included in the Company’s proposed portfolio of resources are economically favorable when evaluated without errors. The Company’s revised analysis correcting PIA Staff’s modeling casts reasonable doubt on PIA Staff’s initial rejection of the proposed Yates CTs and Robins and Moody BESS projects and underscores the importance of a more thorough and long-term evaluation provided by the Company’s adjustments to PIA Staff’s analysis.

DER
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH PIA STAFF WITNESS KADUK’S PROPOSED SIZE CAP TO THE DER CUSTOMER PROGRAMS?
A.No. The Company does not agree with the size cap PIA Staff Witness Kaduk recommended for DERs in the DER Co-location program. The Co-location program was designed to make more efficient use of resiliency resources that customers are bringing to provide backup power for their operations, and to incent cleaner resources certified and permitted for non-emergency use. Introducing a 10 MW cap would exclude a significant portion of the resiliency resources large load customers will be procuring and prevent them from being used for the benefit of all customers. 
Further, PIA Staff recommends setting the limit on the installed DER to match the customer’s annual peak load at the premises, up to, but not exceeding the program maximum DER size limit. This recommendation is inappropriate for two reasons. First, resiliency assets designed to provide full back up are typically sized with nameplate capacity exceeding peak load. This accounts for operational best practice not to run resources for extended periods at maximum output and mitigates concerns should an individual asset fail. Second, unlike resources utilized in the DRC-1 tariff Witness Kaduk references, the resources deployed through these programs will be supply side assets capable of pushing back to the grid. As a result, there is no need or basis for putting a size cap based on load. 
Q. PLEASE RESPOND TO GAM WITNESS POLLOCK’S RECOMMENDED MODIFICATIONS TO THE DER CUSTOMER PROGRAMS. 
A.One of GAM Witness Pollock’s concerns is that the proposed tariffs do not specify the amount of the required credits/payments. He believes that this gives the Company “undue discretion” and that the “customer would have no opportunity to either vet the proposed credits/payments or seek a remedy from the Commission.” (Tr. 962.) However, by not detailing the specific credit or payment within the terms of the tariff, the programs have flexibility to accommodate different types of resources, can expand to reflect additional use cases should they materialize, and are able to adjust to changing conditions. That does not mean that customers and the Commission will not have an opportunity to view or vet the values and associated calculation methodologies. In fact, each of the proposed programs and tariffs requires an additional customer agreement. The customer agreement and supporting documents will provide further details, requirements, and specifications for the individual customer and resources. The resources participating in DCL-1 and DCO-1 will be supply side resources subject to Commission certification and approval. 
Witness Pollock also expressed concern about the procurement of resources in the proposed DER Co-location Program and wants to require a competitive solicitation with engagement from the customer. The Company agrees that the participating customer must be engaged in the selection of resources but does not support a requirement that each resource must be competitively sourced. A participating customer may have existing vendor relationships or specific preferences on what technologies and or companies they would like to work with outside of a competitive procurement process. In addition, the Company has already issued an RFP and selected qualified vendors for the current DER Customer Pilot. These same vendors can be utilized for the proposed DER programs.
Q.DO THE DER CO-LOCATION AND CUSTOMER OWNED PROGRAMS EXCLUDE CARBON FREE RESOURCES?
A.No. The program is designed to be resource neutral; provided that such resources are dispatchable, have a firm fuel supply, and are interconnected to transmit the energy they produce to the electric grid. This could include microgrids, fuel cells, or other carbon free resources. Ultimately, the participating customer will be responsible for determining the set of resources that meet their needs and the Company will evaluate and reflect the appropriate System value associated with those resources.

A.No. The Company proposed expansions of existing and new distributed energy resource programs that rely on end-use or customer-sited technology. Fully leveraging additional load flexibility programs, like virtual power plants (“VPP”), will require more planning time and further operational capabilities than exist in this 2023 IRP Update. The Company is in the process of evaluating and planning demand-side programs that will be filed in the 2025 IRP, including potential VPP programs. Deriving System value from mass market load flexibility programs will require continued investment in enterprise Distributed Energy Resource Management Systems (DERMS) for visibility, predictability, and enhanced operational capabilities of these resources.
Minimum Ownership
Q.SHOULD THE COMMISSION REVISE THE MINIMUM OWNERSHIP REQUIREMENT IN ITS RULES?
A.No. The Commission’s longstanding 70/30 policy ensures System reliability and sufficient Commission oversight of the resources serving customers. Staff and Intervenors dismiss the Commission’s 70/30 rule claiming that it is outdated and does not ensure that the Company procures the least cost resources for customers. However, this ignores the fact that apart from the resources requested in this case, the vast majority of all other capacity resources since 2001 were successfully procured through an RFP process. Further, allegations that the ratio is too high and biased in favor of Company-owned resources are based on comparisons to utilities in RTOs and deregulated markets, where service reliability has been a challenge in extreme weather events. Notably, Georgia is not alone in providing a minimum number of utility-owned and controlled resources to ensure a sufficient level of reliability and oversight. For example, the 70/30 ratio is comparable to that set in Virginia, where 65% of all new renewables are to be utility owned, and N.C.G.S. § 62-110.9(2)(b) in North Carolina, which provides for all new resources to be company owned except for 45% renewables and storage.

A.Yes. The Company conducted initial screening analyses for the 2023 IRP Update filing at a base load level that includes all committed large loads. The Company then continued to refine the analyses for the supplemental filing with an updated load forecast that includes committed and potential large loads. Both versions of the analyses yielded the same results for transmission needs validating the comprehensive process conducted by the Company. Additionally, the Company conducted a Unit Designation Study for the Yates CTs that further validated the transmission needs. 
Q.DO THE TRANSMISSION LIMITATIONS ON THE PROPOSED YATES UNITS LIMIT DELIVERY FROM THE UNITS ALL THE TIME?
A.No. The transmission upgrades required to unlock the full designated capacity of the three proposed Yates CTs only impact power delivery in peak demand periods under certain transmission contingencies. For most hours throughout the year, the new Yates CTs will be available to serve load up to the full designated capacity as soon as they come online.
Q.DID THE COMPANY PROVIDE SUPPORT FOR THE 600 MW TEMPORARY DELIVERY LIMITATION FOR THE YATES CTS IN PEAK PERIODS UNTIL 2028 WHEN TRANSMISSION CONSTRAINTS ARE ALLEVIATED?
A.Yes. As shown in the supporting studies and workpapers accompanying the Transmission Supplemental Filing on January 19, 2024, the Company provided documentation of its analysis and results regarding the transmission limitations on the proposed Plant Yates CTs until required transmission improvements as listed in the Supplemental Filings are complete. 
Q.ARE THE PROPOSED YATES CTS SUBJECT TO TRANSMISSION CURTAILMENT RISK AS A RESULT OF THE COMPANY HAVING REQUESTED ENERGY RESOURCE INTERCONNECTION SERVICE RATHER THAN NETWORK RESOURCE INTERCONNECTION SERVICE?
A.No. Since the Company’s transmission planning process includes firm and future firm commitments only, there is no curtailment risk for the delivery of power from the proposed Yates CTs because of the Company’s interconnection service request. This concern from Staff Witness Goggin and SREA Witness Olson represents a fundamental misunderstanding between the requests and studies for interconnection service and delivery service on the Southern Company System. As explained in the Company’s response to STF-GS-2-1, neither Energy Resource Interconnection Service (“ERIS”) nor Network Resource Interconnection Service (“NRIS”) conveys delivery rights to a generator. Delivery service in the Southern Company System is separately requested through a transmission service request for point-to-point transmission or through the designation of the resource as a network resource for networked transmission service. The Company has already requested that Plant Yates Units 8-10 be designated as network resources and that designation study is complete. 
The fact that transmission operators in other parts of the country and in different market structures combine interconnection and delivery service as part of a NRIS request does not signal a fundamental flaw or risk in how the Company has planned for the delivery of power from the proposed Yates CTs. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY CONSIDER NON-WIRES ALTERNATIVES IN ITS TRANSMISSION PLANNING AND, IF SO, WHAT ALTERNATIVES WERE CONSIDERED IN THIS CASE?
A.Yes, the Company considers non-wires alternatives in its transmission planning and has demonstrated the integration of these solutions in past projects where they added operational flexibility and were more cost effective. However, given the time constraints in this case, the Company was unable to explore all the options typically available for a given transmission constraint or deficiency as part of the transmission analyses supporting the resource requests in this case. Nevertheless, the transmission projects identified to support the Yates CTs are consistent with the typical types of projects needed to accommodate the addition of large generation resources to the System. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF AND INTERVENOR RECOMMENDATIONS THAT THE COMPANY EMPLOY EMERGING, GRID ENHANCING TECHNOLOGIES AND NON-WIRES ALTERNATIVES LIKE DYNAMIC LINE RATING, GEOLOGICAL TYPOLOGY OPTIMIZATION, FLEXIBLE AC TRANSMISSION SYSTEMS (“FACTS”), ETC.
A.The Company already employs many of the alternative and advancing solutions that Staff and Intervenors propose where they provide value to customers. Grid Enhancing Technologies refers to a broad range of technologies, and these are considered as part of the Company’s 10-year Transmission Planning Process. For example, the Company already uses FACTS on the System through its deployment of static VAR compensators and static synchronous compensators to improve power quality and efficiency of the transmission grid. The Company has also specified the use of series reactors and series compensation as power flow control devices in addition to advanced conductors. As a standard practice and through collaboration with the Southern Company Research and Development team, the Company continues to consider new types of technologies in its evaluation criteria. However, there are limitations to using some of the suggested technologies as they are not mature enough for deployment on the System, require long lead times, or are inappropriate for long term planning. To this point, dynamic line ratings (“DLR”) have the potential to enhance operational flexibility by incorporating various factors such as wind speed, temperature, load profiles, and solar irradiance. These benefits occur in real-time or near real-time conditions, however, which make it inappropriate to use DLR capabilities as a long-term planning solution because it is impossible to know today what speeds the wind will blow at or what the solar irradiance will be many years in the future. Although not a long-term planning solution, the Company is evaluating opportunities for DLR implementation under unique circumstances to provide real-time operational benefits as a temporary solution until a long-term solution can be executed.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY USE AMBIENT-ADJUSTED RATINGS (“AAR”) IN THEIR OPERATIONS AND PLANNING PROCESSES? 
A.Yes. The Company uses AAR in its dynamic ampacity ratings program for transmission planning and operations planning processes that includes local parameters such as weather and wind. By use of this program, the Company equips its operators with current System conditions and minimizes risks associated with short-term, excess conductor loading. Within the Company’s transmission planning processes, the Company leverages AAR as a bridge to permanent solutions, where appropriate. These applications tend to occur in the early years of the planning horizon when there is not sufficient time to construct a project. 
Q.WHY DIDN’T THE COMPANY CONSIDER BATTERIES SITED CLOSE TO LOAD AS A TRANSMISSION SOLUTION IN THIS CASE?
A.Batteries sited near load are not a practical solution or replacement for the identified transmission upgrades for several reasons. First, the battery would need to charge from the grid, which causes it to act like additional load, thereby exacerbating the problem the additional resources and transmission solutions are trying to resolve. Second, a battery serving as a solution to a transmission contingency cannot be counted as both generation capacity and a transmission solution by planners on the Southern Company System. To serve as a transmission solution, a battery would need to be fully charged around the clock, available when and if called upon to address a contingency since contingencies can occur at any time and potentially for extended durations. Third, a 4-hour battery is not a practical solution to address transmission contingency scenarios. For example, a tornado could create an unexpected outage during hot weather conditions that lasts for a week. For batteries to resolve the transmission constraints identified in the transmission supplemental filing analysis, the transmission grid would need to have enough available capacity to charge the battery system(s), they would need to be of significant size and discharge time, and there would need to be multiple battery systems to cover the multiple contingencies across different locations identified in the analysis. The overall cost and land requirements to support battery projects that can cover extended duration outages would be cost prohibitive, especially when contemplating the relatively small magnitude of the total amount of transmission projects identified in the supplemental transmission filing. In addition, a total net present value of the battery systems versus wire solutions would need to be run to contemplate the total life-cycle costs of the two alternatives.
Q.SHOULD THE COMPANY PROACTIVELY INVEST IN MORE 500 KV TRANSMISSION LINES ACROSS THE STATE AS RECOMMENDED BY STAFF WITNESS GOGGIN, SACE WITNESS PATEL, AND SREA WITNESS OLSON?
A.No, not as part of its request in this case. Intervenor-proposed 500kV solutions cannot be implemented in the required timeframe and would not completely address the local area 230kV constraints identified to bring the Yates CTs online. That said, the solutions proposed in the 2023 IRP Update and supplemental filings could complement future 500kV development. 
Further, simply proposing to build more 500kV lines in the state misunderstands how transmission planning is coordinated with generation resource planning in Georgia and would result in very expensive infrastructure investment with no guarantee to alleviate known or anticipated transmission constraints. In deregulated markets, transmission owners build large transmission lines across states hoping that they have sited the infrastructure where independent power producers will seek to interconnect. In contrast, Georgia Power builds transmission as needed to deliver power from facilities that have been sited, financed, and have initiated the process of interconnecting to the System. Georgia Power’s integrated approach ensures interconnections actually occur and minimizes transmission investments based upon speculation. Further, Witness Goggin’s testimony seems to ignore the integrated nature of the transmission system in Georgia whereby Georgia Power jointly plans and operates the networked transmission system with the other Georgia ITS participants in the state: Georgia Transmission Corporation (“GTC”) and the Municipal Electric Authority of Georgia (“MEAG”).
In addition, adding or upgrading existing line voltage to 500 kV is not as simple as swapping out the line on existing structures. The Company requires additional rights of way access for larger voltage lines, the acquisition of which adds substantial time and cost to the process of rebuilding or retrofitting a line. Finally, Witness Goggin seems to gloss over the significant costs and time requirements associated with changing the voltage level of existing facilities, which would be additional costs borne by Georgia Power customers. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO THE RECOMMENDATION OF SEVERAL PARTIES THAT THE COMPANY IMPLEMENT A MORE HOLISTIC, INTEGRATED PLANNING BETWEEN TRANSMISSION AND GENERATION THAT IS PROACTIVE RATHER THAN REACTIVE.
A.The Company already does most of the planning improvements parties recommend. Unlike deregulated markets, which lack the insight and structural mechanisms to jointly plan transmission and generation, the Company’s IRP process already holistically and intentionally considers the balance between transmission investment and generation deployment. While transmission is a significant aspect of IRP processes, long-term firm commitments by third parties under a FERC-jurisdictional Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”) is also a key driver in the development of transmission expansion planning. Additionally, Southern Company and Southeastern Regional Transmission Planning (“SERTP”) Sponsors’ transmission planning processes utilizes multi-disciplined analyses that identify System needs using least-cost and reliable solutions from all reasonably available alternatives. As an added step, the Commission reviews, regulates, and approves the Company’s planning process and ensures that the assets built are reliable, economic, and in the public interest.
Q.DOES GEORGIA POWER ALREADY PARTICIPATE IN REGIONAL PLANNING WITH NEIGHBORING SYSTEMS?
A.Yes. That is the purpose of the SERTP organization. Georgia Power is also a Participant in the Georgia ITS with GTC, MEAG, and Dalton Utilities.
VIII.CONCLUSION
Q.IN CONCLUSION, PLEASE SUMMARIZE WHY THE COMMISSION SHOULD APPROVE THE COMPANY’S REQUEST WITHOUT MODIFICATION.
A.The 2023IRP Update presents a plan that will ensure Georgia Power can continue to reliably serve the state’s growing capacity needs while supporting economic growth and lowering rates for all retail customers. The Company’s plan provides substantial benefits for our customers and our state, and we request that the Commission approve the Company’s request without modification. 
Q.DOES THIS CONCLUDE YOUR TESTIMONY? 
A.Yes.

15       Q.   WHAT IS A VIRTUAL POWER PLANT?

16       A.   The U.S. Department of Energy (“DOE”) recently defined VPPs as “aggregations of DERs

17            that can balance electrical loads and provide utility-scale and utility-scale grid services like

18            a traditional power plant.” 1 The fundamental components of a VPP are 1) behind-the-

19            meter (“BTM”) distributed energy resources (“DERs”) and 2) software which enables

20            behind-the-meter DERs to be controlled and networked with other similar devices to bring

 1        aggregated value to the larger energy system. Presently, these DERs fall into three broad

 2        categories: 1) renewable energy generators, predominantly rooftop solar; 2) battery energy

 3        storage systems; and 3) intelligent, digitalized loads, such as smart thermostats, electric

 4        vehicles and their charging equipment, and grid interactive water heaters, among others.

 5        Virtual power plants can provide a wide range of grid services, including capacity, energy,

 6        frequency response, demand response, and spinning and non-spinning reserves. Virtual

 7        power plants can also support load shifting or act as load sinks, absorbing excess renewable

 8        energy that would normally be curtailed.

 9   Q.   HOW DO VIRTUAL POWER PLANTS WORK?

10   A.   For many utilities, customer-sited resources are largely unmanaged. Rooftop solar injects

11        energy into the grid when it is produced, at roughly the same time that all other rooftop,

12        community-scale, and utility-scale solar facilities are producing and injecting into the grid.

13        Customers run their air conditioners or heat pumps whenever they want to, trusting that

14        there will always be enough electricity to achieve and maintain their desired temperature.

15        In some limited cases, such as cases of customers with EVs or customers with rooftop solar

16        paired with storage, rate structures might encourage customers to charge at a certain time

17        or to store their excess generation and consume it during system peaks, but these price

18        signals are static and are not responsive to current conditions. These rate structures also do

19        nothing to dynamically utilize a pool of energy resources, responding to real-time

20        conditions within the energy system. As an example – let’s imagine that harnessed solar

21        and grid energy is immediately called on and dispatched across a thousand specific homes

22        and a hundred specific buildings, in a purposeful manner, as a synchronous grid asset,


                                   Direct Testimony of Blake F. Richetta
                                  Georgia Coalition of Local Governments
                                  Georgia PSC Docket Nos. 56002 & 56003
                                                Page 9 of 30

 1   again, as part of the VPP, due to extreme congestion within a specific load pocket. This

 2   cluster of batteries is dispatched to shed each of their local home or building loads, in a

 3   matter of a few seconds, acting to decongest that specific sub-station load pocket where

 4   they are located, while simultaneously injecting a precise “set point” of energy into the

 5   grid, balanced and optimized by VPP node, offsetting the use of peaking power plants to

 6   deliver energy to thousands of other homes and buildings co-located in the same load

 7   pocket. And of course, this action not only reduces peaking power plant usage, it acts to

 8   further decongest the upstream energy system. Such a dynamic VPP dispatch may be based

 9   on an extraordinary grid side peak period, combined with extraordinary congestion in a

10   specific load pocket. However, at a higher level, this example of VPP operation could be

11   viewed as an argument for a non-wires-alternative to that specific load pocket, providing

12   genuine infrastructure upgrade deferrals at the sub-station level and to its related

13   distribution infrastructure.

14   In summary, VPPs take these unmanaged and largely uncontrolled generation systems,

15   storage resources, and loads and orchestrate them to achieve grid harmonization and

16   balance on a dynamic basis, in response to real time conditions. Resources are grouped

17   into thoughtful, purposeful energy networks that can interact as a single firm dispatchable

18   grid asset to provide specific grid services. All of this is made possible by powerful VPP

19   software that can remotely monitor and control compatible technologies via a simple

20   internet connection.

17           •   System level resiliency, helping to prevent outages.

18           •   Reverse demand response for grid balancing – using battery network as a load sink

20               featuring excess generation and delivery potential.

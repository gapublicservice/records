 3           On-Bill Financing

 4   Q.      PLEASE RESPOND TO THE RECOMMENDATION THAT THE
 5           COMPANY OFFER ON-BILL FINANCING PROGRAMS.

 6   A.      It is not prudent to offer on-bill financing due to the lack of success of the
 7           Residential Investment Saving Energy (“RISE”) Pilot and the ultimate expense to
 8           customers. Georgia Power previously offered a program similar to on-bill financing
 9           for income-qualified customers in its RISE Pilot program. Under the RISE Pilot,
10           the Company covered the upfront cost of energy efficiency and demand side
11           management improvements and recovered those costs over time through a charge
12           applied under a customer-specific RISE Tariff. However, covering the upfront costs
13           for customers who later paid a charge over time did not increase customer adoption
14           of or participation in energy efficiency measures. It was also difficult to find
15           qualifying customers for the RISE Pilot, which resulted in only 18 qualifying
16           customers despite being extended from three to six years. In most cases, customers
17           did not qualify because it would have taken longer than the life of the installed
18           equipment to pay back savings. There is no evidence that a third-party financing
19           arrangement would have helped the RISE Pilot be more successful than the tariff-
20           based approach, which offered customers the same benefit of participating without
21           an upfront investment. Notably, after the pilot concluded, an independent program
22           evaluator determined that the pilot was unsuccessful.

 3   Q.   IS THE IRP THE PROPER FORUM TO CONSIDER SPECIFIC
 4        FEEDBACK ON THE COMPANY’S RFPS?

 5   A.   No. While the IRP is the appropriate place to consider high level decisions such as
 6        whether an RFP is needed or if fundamental changes, such as adding storage to
 7        renewable resources, should be made, it is not the appropriate forum for detailed
 8        RFP and contract specific feedback, which is better addressed within the context of
 9        each RFP. Advanced Power Alliance, the Southern Renewable Energy Association,
10        Georgia Solar Energy Industries Association, and other Intervenors now attempt to
11        use the IRP process to circumvent the existing RFP comment period and structure
12        to get outsized and inappropriate influence to modify the Company’s RFPs. To
13        allow this would undermine the process set forth in the Commission’s RFP Rules,
14        which were designed to ensure transparency and fairness of the RFP to all
15        participants. All potential bidders should have an equal opportunity to provide
16        feedback, and the impacts of any changes should be considered across all potential
17        bidders and interested parties. Circumventing the existing process could result in
18        some market participants having more influence than others, undermining the intent
19        of the Commission rules, since not all potential bidders and interested parties
20        participate or are represented in the IRP process.

Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION THAT IT BE REQUIRED TO INCLUDE AN ADDITIONAL “INTEGRATED MODELING CASE” IN THE 2028 IRP? 
A.No. Staff’s recommendation to require the Company to continue including the Supply Side Case, in which DSM is allowed to compete head-to-head with supply-side options in the IRP model, is unnecessary. The Supply Side Case, which was originally proposed by Staff and Intervenors, is an administrative burden to prepare, and has never been supported or recommended by any party in any case. 
Rather, the Company determines an appropriate portfolio of DSM measures and programs to benefit its customers through the DSM program planning process that includes: (1) utilizing standard utility cost-effectiveness screening tools; (2) incorporating input from a variety of stakeholders as well as the Company’s third-party design consultant; (3) leveraging lessons learned through program delivery, program evaluation, and historical program performance to create the proposed DSM portfolio; and (4) collaboration with Staff and the DSMWG. Further, the Company already evaluates DSM on a level playing field with supply-side resources by utilizing the same underlying system assumptions for both supply-side and demand-side resource evaluations. The production of the “Integrated Modeling” or Supply Side Case does not inform the ultimate DSM portfolio. Instead, the inclusion of this additional case as requested by Staff results in the expenditure of significant time and resources with no measurable benefit to customers.  
DSM Budget
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF’S TESTIMONY REGARDING THE COMPANY’S DSM BUDGET.
A.Staff contends that the Proposed Case budget increase does not align with current or past program spending or with Commission policy or precedent. Although Staff’s contention is true that the Proposed Case budget is higher than past program spending, the increased budget is required in light of the increased savings target included in the Commission’s Vogtle Prudence Order. Therefore, the budget reflects the increased cost to achieve the required 0.75% savings target.
Q.WHY IS IT NOT FEASIBLE TO SIMPLY CAP THE PROPOSED BUDGET AT 1% OF RETAIL REVENUES UNDER THE PROPOSED CASE, AS GIPL/SOUTHFACE WITNESS SHERWOOD RECOMMENDS? 
A.Georgia Interfaith Power & Light and Southface Energy Institute (“GIPL/Southface”) Witness Sherwood recommends that the annual budget not exceed 1% of retail revenues to achieve the Proposed Case savings target. This recommendation is unrealistic and imposes an arbitrary cap on the Company’s budget that is divorced from the actual programs and program implementation requirements, as well as what is required to achieve savings in the future. Further, by relying on the prior year’s retail sales to determine the 1% budget calculation, DSM program implementation would be unnecessarily delayed due to the lag in closing the prior year’s books and the need to identify and hire program implementers based on a known budget.
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO THE COMPARISONS MADE BY GIPL/SOUTHFACE OF THE COMPANY’S HISTORIC AND PROJECTED ENERGY SAVINGS TO THOSE OF OTHER STATES.
A.To compare energy savings with those achieved by other utilities operating in other states assumes that each state and utility have similar DSM policies and regulatory requirements. That is not the case. Each state reaches its own determination of the specific policies that guide customer-subsidized DSM implementation. Such policies are impacted by the various state-specific considerations, including program maturity, energy intensity of customers, regional climate differences, the cost of electricity, regulatory structure, capacity needs, state energy efficiency and renewable energy portfolio standards, and participation in regional greenhouse gas initiatives. For example, public utilities in North Carolina are subject to state-wide energy efficiency standards, which are not similarly required in Georgia. Further, in states where the cost of electricity is higher, kilowatt hour (“kWh”) savings from DSM programs are more economic and impactful. This same logic applies to Witness Sherwood’s recommendation that the Commission compare the Company’s DSM budget to that of other utilities. 
GIPL/Southface Witness Sherwood attempts to use the Company’s American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy (“ACEEE”) Scorecard results to bolster her position; however, as she admitted through cross examination, Georgia Power’s ACEEE scorecard results are in line with the “peer utilities” that Witness Sherwood self-selected and better than other utilities in surrounding states such as Florida Power and Light and Duke Energy Florida. 
On-Bill Financing 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO THE RECOMMENDATION THAT THE COMPANY OFFER ON-BILL FINANCING PROGRAMS.
A.It is not prudent to offer on-bill financing due to the lack of success of the Residential Investment Saving Energy (“RISE”) Pilot and the ultimate expense to customers. Georgia Power previously offered a program similar to on-bill financing for income-qualified customers in its RISE Pilot program. Under the RISE Pilot, the Company covered the upfront cost of energy efficiency and demand side management improvements and recovered those costs over time through a charge applied under a customer-specific RISE Tariff. However, covering the upfront costs for customers who later paid a charge over time did not increase customer adoption of or participation in energy efficiency measures. It was also difficult to find qualifying customers for the RISE Pilot, which resulted in only 18 qualifying customers despite being extended from three to six years. In most cases, customers did not qualify because it would have taken longer than the life of the installed equipment to pay back savings. There is no evidence that a third-party financing arrangement would have helped the RISE Pilot be more successful than the tariff-based approach, which offered customers the same benefit of participating without an upfront investment. Notably, after the pilot concluded, an independent program evaluator determined that the pilot was unsuccessful.

This proposal also provides more stability in the metric used to determine additional sum and certainty in the amount of incentive that the Company will receive for implementing DSM programs, as allowed by state law, while reducing resources needed by the Company and Staff in reviewing and verifying the data. All certified DSM programs with energy savings will be included in the additional sum calculation. Any authorized additional sum will be specific to the corresponding customer class and will be collected through the Residential and Commercial DSM tariffs. The additional sum included in the DSM tariffs will apply the $0.04 per kWh saved to the total net energy savings estimated in the Company’s 2025 Proposed Case. The Company supports the annual true-up of the DSM tariffs based on reported energy savings and actual revenues collected.
Q.DOES STAFF AGREE THAT THE COMPANY SHOULD RECEIVE AN ADDITIONAL SUM?
A.Yes. Staff Witnesses Barber, Cooper, and Spellman agree that the Company should receive an additional sum. However, they recommend that Company maintain the current DSM additional sum mechanism as approved in the 2022 IRP for market-rate programs. Additionally, Staff recommends the Company earn $0.02 per kWh saved on net energy savings from income-qualified programs.
Q.WHY SHOULD THE COMMISSION APPROVE THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED ADDITIONAL SUM ALONG WITH THE CHANGES IN METHODOLOGY?
A.The Company has proposed an additional sum of $0.04 per kWh saved, which represents a reasonable additional sum and is in line with the average amount per kWh earned since 2012 through the previously approved annual DSM true-up process. Currently, Georgia Power receives little to no additional sum from residential DSM programs that deliver kWh savings. Accordingly, under the proposed methodology, energy savings will be valued from every program equally. This better aligns the additional sum with the energy savings achieved from the customer classes eligible to participate in the Company’s DSM programs. The proposed methodology also provides a simplified approach that will streamline reporting and provide more clarity associated with the annual DSM tariff true-up process.
RENEWABLE PROCUREMENT STRATEGY
Q.PLEASE PROVIDE A BRIEF OVERVIEW OF THE PROPOSED ENHANCEMENTS TO THE COMPANY’S RENEWABLE PROCUREMENT STRATEGY.
A.As detailed in this panel’s Direct Testimony, Georgia Power has proposed several enhancements to the Utility Scale and DG RFPs. For Utility Scale RFPs, the Company proposes adding flexible Required Commercial Operation Dates (“RCODs”), which, if approved, will allow projects to have a range of acceptable in-service dates. The Company will continue to consider the use of Transmission System operational tools and flexibility that support interconnection viability for projects and portfolios of projects. And as discussed further below, the Company proposes a submission price refresh and extended RFP period to allow for the procurement of additional renewable resources to meet customer needs.
For its DG RFPs, the Company proposes to make the energy procured available for subscription by interested customers and seeks to include paired solar and storage resources to deliver additional capacity value to benefit all customers. Similar to the proposed Utility Scale RFP enhancements, the Company seeks to modify the DG RFP process to allow for additional resources to be procured if proven beneficial for customers. Finally, the Company proposes to update the Renewable Cost Benefit framework used in the DG evaluation process to assign value to projects based on their geographic location. By incorporating locational value into the DG RFP evaluation, the Company aims to incentivize strategic siting decisions by developers and secure a portfolio of projects that provides maximum value for customers. 
In the event there are unmet CARES customer subscription needs in either the Utility Scale or DG RFPs, the Company has proposed modifications to the RFP process to allow opportunities for additional projects to be procured. First, the Company proposes a submission price refresh process, where fully evaluated bids would be offered the opportunity to refresh their pricing such that the project will deliver benefits to all customers equal to the average total net benefit of the selected bids. Second, the Company proposes to extend the RFP period to allow for new projects to be submitted at prices that create net benefits equal to the average total net benefit of the selected portfolio, thus allowing interested customers to work with project developers to identify resources to fill those outstanding subscription needs. Proposals submitted as Customer Identified Resources (“CIRs”) in Phase II will undergo a full economic and interconnection analysis by Georgia Power to ensure final pricing that secures additional renewable resources in a manner that preserves benefits and protects all customers.
Q.WHY HAS THE COMPANY PROPOSED ENHANCING ITS RENEWABLE PROCUREMENT STRATEGY?
A.Georgia Power recognizes the dynamic nature of the current energy landscape and proposes these modifications in response to customer needs, feedback from market participants, and recent RFP experience. This information confirms the need for additional flexibility in the Company’s procurement strategy to navigate changing market conditions, including supply chain disruptions and changing policies. 
Q.WHY IS MARKET FEEDBACK IMPORTANT, AND HOW DOES GEORGIA POWER TYPICALLY RECEIVE THAT FEEDBACK REGARDING ITS RENEWABLE PROCUREMENTS?
A.The Company, Staff, the Independent Evaluator (“IE”), and market participants have a long history of constructive and collaborative feedback processes that underpin the continuous improvement of procurement practices. This feedback remains critical to ensure a healthy and competitive procurement environment that attracts competent and committed counterparts to deliver low-cost renewable energy to the benefit of Georgia Power customers. Consistent with the Commission’s RFP rules, each of the Company’s RFPs offers potential bidders and interested parties the opportunity to provide comments and feedback on the RFP process as well as on the draft RFP and pro forma contracts (“RFP Documents”). In addition to asking questions and providing comments, potential bidders and interested parties are encouraged to provide redline changes and proposed revisions to RFP and contract specific language during the comment period. Each comment received and proposed response is reviewed and discussed by the Company, Staff, and the IE. The Company regularly incorporates feedback from market participants appropriate for each RFP, as demonstrated by the evolution of the Company’s RFP Documents over time. After the RFP concludes, Georgia Power offers unsuccessful bidders the opportunity to receive project specific feedback on the bids submitted into the RFP. Further, the Company regularly meets with market participants, including those that intervene in Georgia Power’s IRPs on an individual or group basis, for additional feedback on the RFP process and trends to consider to enhance the overall success of the procurement process. 
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY IMPLEMENT FEEDBACK RECEIVED ON ITS RFPS?
A.When providing suggestions and feedback specific to an active, ongoing RFP, the market participant’s comments are reviewed and considered by Staff and the IE, in addition to the Company. This process allows for RFP, resource, and contract specific feedback to be considered and implemented within the context of the then-current resource procurement process. As evidenced by the enhancements proposed in our direct testimony and discussed above, the Company takes comments and feedback received through its RFPs seriously and, when deemed appropriate to provide additional value for customers, proposes modifications to address concerns raised. The Company also endeavors to adopt improvements made in one Georgia Power RFP across other RFPs, as well as from lessons learned in other jurisdictions, where appropriate. For example, after receiving feedback in the All Source, Winter BESS, and CARES 2023 RFPs, the Company proposed modifications to storage performance metrics to be implemented in each of the upcoming RFPs where storage resources could be procured, including the 500 MW Energy Storage System RFP (approved in the 2022 IRP) and the CARES 2025 Utility Scale Renewable RFP. 
Q.IS THE IRP THE PROPER FORUM TO CONSIDER SPECIFIC FEEDBACK ON THE COMPANY’S RFPS?
A.No. While the IRP is the appropriate place to consider high level decisions such as whether an RFP is needed or if fundamental changes, such as adding storage to renewable resources, should be made, it is not the appropriate forum for detailed RFP and contract specific feedback, which is better addressed within the context of each RFP. Advanced Power Alliance, the Southern Renewable Energy Association, Georgia Solar Energy Industries Association, and other Intervenors now attempt to use the IRP process to circumvent the existing RFP comment period and structure to get outsized and inappropriate influence to modify the Company’s RFPs. To allow this would undermine the process set forth in the Commission’s RFP Rules, which were designed to ensure transparency and fairness of the RFP to all participants. All potential bidders should have an equal opportunity to provide feedback, and the impacts of any changes should be considered across all potential bidders and interested parties. Circumventing the existing process could result in some market participants having more influence than others, undermining the intent of the Commission rules, since not all potential bidders and interested parties participate or are represented in the IRP process. 
Q.WHAT ARE THE GOALS OF THE COMPETITIVE PROCUREMENT PROCESS, AND WHAT ARE GEORGIA POWER’S PRIORITIES IN DEVELOPING AND PROPOSING THE RFPS?
A.The competitive procurement process was created to ensure customers receive the best value from generation resources through careful evaluation of cost, risk and benefits delivered. In developing RFPs, the Company first and foremost complies with the Commission’s rules and regulations. The Company uses the Commission-approved framework to design resource procurements aimed at ensuring reliable resources are economically procured in a manner that protects customers. Many of the recommended revisions to the RFP process put forth in this IRP are being made by industry groups seeking to make the process more favorable to bidders and developers. These proposed changes would not serve to reduce risk in the generation development process but would instead shift those risks from developers to customers, undermining certainty and ultimately reducing the value delivered to customers. 
Q. DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH STAFF’S PROPOSAL TO REDUCE THE AMOUNT OF ADDITIONAL SUM FOR RENEWABLE PROCUREMENTS?
A.No. Staff provided no evidence to support the proposed reduction in additional sum from $4/kW-yr to $3/kW-yr. The Company provided a detailed accounting to support a $7.50/kW-yr additional sum amount in the 2022 IRP and subsequently agreed to the $4/kW-yr amount as part of the stipulation in the 2022 IRP. The additional sum for renewable resources should remain at $4/kW-year.
Customer Identified Resource Option
Q.DOES THE COMPANY AGREE WITH CEBA’S RECOMMENDATION TO IMPLEMENT THE CUSTOMER IDENTIFIED RESOURCE OPTION ON AN UNCAPPED, ROLLING BASIS WITHOUT CONDUCTING A COMPETITIVE RFP? 
A.No. Clean Energy Buyers association (“CEBA”) Witnesses Barua and Alderfer propose, among other things, that the Company make CIRs available on a rolling basis, thereby bypassing the Commission-approved RFP process. As proposed by the Company, the CIR option operates as an extended RFP process, tied to a competitive solicitation, which ensures procurement of resources that benefit the entire system. It allows for potential project developers and potential CARES subscribers to work together to submit project proposals that provide total net benefits that are greater than or equal to the portfolio average from the initial phase of the RFP (either Utility Scale or DG), delivering value to all Georgia Power customers consistent with the Commission’s Rules. This process ensures that the additional resources are fully evaluated and studied, consistent with the Commission’s rules and regulations, and that resources are acquired through the competitive procurement process, support system reliability, are cost effective, and beneficial to all customers, not just individual customers who may subscribe. 
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED CUSTOMER IDENTIFIED RESOURCE PROCESS BENEFIT ALL CUSTOMERS?
A.As mentioned above, the CIR process as proposed is an extension of the competitive RFP and complies with Commission rules, as any project submitted will undergo full evaluation by the Company and will only be selected if deemed beneficial to all customers. These new projects are to be submitted at or below a price target that results in a Total Net Benefit that is greater than or equal to the average Total Net Benefit of the winning portfolio of resources from the Phase I procurement. By matching the price targets for CIRs to the average total net benefit procured in the RFP, the Company is ensuring that the resources provide value to all customers and that no costs are being shifted. As proposed by the Company, this option also ensures that if there is a difference between the term length of the subscribing customer agreement and the term of the supplying purchase power agreement, all customers stand to benefit from the fixed price energy after the subscribing customer agreement ends.
Q.IS THE COMPANY AMENABLE TO WORKING WITH CUSTOMERS TO DEVELOP ADDITIONAL RESOURCE OR PROGRAM PROPOSALS TO HELP ADDRESS THEIR NEEDS FOR RENEWABLE AND CARBON FREE ENERGY? 
A.Yes. As discussed in Section 10.5 of the 2025 IRP Main Document, in addition to the new programs and program enhancements described above, the Company is exploring various innovative solutions related to renewable and carbon free energy resources to help address customers’ needs. These include, for example, technologies that support grid reliability with changing generation dynamics, carbon-free capacity resources like advanced nuclear, and long-duration energy storage. Georgia Power is also engaging with stakeholders to foster a collaborative approach to evaluate and refine such solutions, while ensuring that all initiatives deliver both System reliability and economic benefits for all customers. Once viable concepts are identified and vetted with potential participants, the Company will seek Commission approval of such solutions. 
CUSTOMER PROGRAMS 
Solar + Storage Pilot Program
Q.PLEASE BRIEFLY EXPLAIN THE PROPOSED RESIDENTIAL AND SMALL COMMERCIAL SOLAR + STORAGE PILOT PROGRAM.
A.The Company has proposed two complimentary pathways by which the Company will incentivize the addition of storage resources that can be utilized by the Company to meet System needs. In the Company Directed pathway, participants will receive an upfront incentive for a 10-year contract granting Georgia Power continuous operation of a customer-sited storage resource. In the Customer Directed pathway, Georgia Power will offer participants a smaller annual enrollment incentive for participation and pay additional performance-based incentives during discrete event calls. 
Q. DID GEORGIA POWER INCORPORATE FEEDBACK FROM INTERVENORS INTO THE PROPOSED PILOT DESIGN?
A.Yes. As acknowledged by Staff Witnesses Barber, Kaduk, and Bower, interested parties were given an opportunity to present on potential design options, best practices, and size considerations, and specific program elements suggested by interested parties were incorporated. In addition to the sessions hosted at the Commission, the Company met, and continues to meet, with manufacturers, developers, implementors and other stakeholders. 
Q. IS THE COMPANY WILLING TO ADOPT ANY ADDITIONAL RECOMMENDATIONS SUGGESTED BY INTERVENORS IN THIS PROCEEDING?
A.Yes. The Company proposes to adopt several of the recommendations put forth by Staff and other Intervenors. First, the Company will remove the restriction on grid charging in the Company Directed pathway. Second, the Company will endeavor to avoid calling on resources in advance of widespread extreme weather. In addition, the Company will commit to offering a Customer Directed option for a 10-year term. Finally, the Company will work with its program implementer, once selected, to offer a publicly accessible dashboard of enrolled MW to provide information on pilot status. 
Q.DOES THE COMPANY BELIEVE THAT THE INCENTIVE STRUCTURE AND LEVELS SHOULD BE MODIFIED BASED ON INTERVENOR FEEDBACK?
A.No, not at this time. The proposed pilot program features a hybrid of upfront and ongoing incentives that are straightforward and calculated based on the forecasted avoided generation capacity value associated with assets participating in each pathway. Intervenors suggested competing modifications to the Company’s incentive structure. For example, Georgia Solar Energy Association (“GA Solar”) Witness Hoyt advocates for removing the annual enrollment incentive, while Georgia Coalition of Local Governments (“Coalition”) Witness Richetta recommends providing annual participation incentives in the Company Directed option. The Company’s proposed structure is a fair and reasonable design that will enable learnings on customer response and system benefit.

A.Yes. Coalition Witness Richetta testified that Sonnen has successful programs around the world where a utility continuously operates the battery. Other Intervenors suggested that they did not believe customers would enroll in the Company Directed option or they proposed limits on the Company use. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO THE RECOMMENDATION TO ALLOW PARTICIPANTS TO TAKE SERVICE ON ENERGY-ONLY RATES.
A.Staff and other Intervenors are concerned that the Company will not allow participants to take service under the Company’s energy-only rates—GS and R—on the basis that it may be a barrier to participation. GA Solar Witness Pertwee states that under the Company Directed pathway, customers cannot respond themselves to the price signals. However, the proposed ineligible rates have no TOU or demand price signals to which a customer could respond receive benefit from. Even if the Company Directed dispatch does not perfectly align with TOU periods, the rate signals and dispatch are likely to be highly correlated and thus generate additional economic benefit for customers. Further, the Company maintains the position that energy only rates do not provide for complete recovery of system costs for customers with behind the meter solar generation, thus shifting the recovery of fixed costs to other customers. 
Q.WHY DID THE COMPANY PROPOSE A SYSTEM SIZE LIMIT OF 20 KW FOR RESIDENTIAL SYSTEMS IN THE SOLAR PLUS STORAGE PILOT PROGRAM? 
A.The Company proposed a 20 kW residential limit to enable an efficient enrollment and interconnection process including assurances of the safe and reliable operation of existing equipment used to serve customers and minimizing additional cost to customers due to upgrades that might be required to support systems above 20 kW. 
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RATIONALE FOR MAINTAINING THE 20 KW LIMIT?
A.The Company does not believe that a 20 kW limit will be a limiting factor for the majority of customers based on installed solar system sizes to date. Examples cited to support a higher limit utilized systems that were oversized relative to the load of the home. A size limit is beneficial to discourage the sale and installation of systems much larger than the customer load. In addition, discussions of the ability for Tesla Powerwall 3s to participate conflated the ability to add additional storage capacity with additional export capability. Several Intervenors suggested the Company could manage systems without a limit using inverter settings, software controls, or relying on the improbability of solar and batteries discharging at nameplate together. These are examples of the types of solutions and data the Company can monitor and evaluate as part of a pilot program.
Q.WHY IS IT NOT FEASIBLE TO EVALUATE SYSTEM SIZE ON A CASE-BY-CASE BASIS AS RECOMMENDED BY CAPITAL GOOD FUND WITNESS BATES?
A.In addition to the reasons discussed above, the Company included a system cap for the proposed program to streamline evaluations and analyses that are part of the interconnection process. To completely remove the system cap and instead evaluate each system on a case-by-case basis would result in an administrative burden and significant expenditure of Company time and resources, which would ultimately slow down program implementation and the interconnection process. 
Q.WERE STAFF AND INTERVENORS ULTIMATELY SUPPORTIVE OF THE RESIDENTIAL AND SMALL COMMERCIAL SOLAR PLUS STORAGE PILOT?
A.Yes. While many had recommendations, all Intervenors who commented were supportive of the pilot offering.

CARES Subscription Program

A.In addition to expanding the CARES program to DG procurements and the introduction of the CIR option, the Company proposes refinements to the notice of intent (“NOI”) process, and an updated pricing methodology for the hourly energy credit to reduce financial risks to non-participating customers. 
Q.WHAT DO INTERVENORS RECOMMEND REGARDING THE CARES PROGRAM HOURLY ENERGY CREDITS?
A.CEBA Witnesses Barua and Alderfer and Coalition Witness Nifong both recommend that the CARES hourly energy credits remain uncapped.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY HAVE CONCERNS WITH THE RECOMMENDATION TO LEAVE THE HOURLY ENERGY CREDITS UNCAPPED? 
A.Yes. Georgia Power proposes to update the CARES pricing methodology and introduce reimbursement thresholds to ensure fairness to all customers and prevent negative impacts to nonparticipants. CEBA and the Coalition’s recommendation that there is no need to implement limits on potential credits ignores the possibility that hourly energy credits could exceed the subscription price paid by CARES participants, negatively impacting all other customers.

A.The proposed enhancements to CARES, including CIRs, provide options to customers interested in securing attributes generated from renewable resources to help meet individual customer goals. The CARES program is designed to offer a fair-value proposition to those potential subscribers in a way that also delivers benefits to non-participating customers. Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO CEBA WITNESSES BARUA AND ALDERFER’S RECOMMENDATION THAT THE COMPANY OFFER CAPACITY SUBSCRIPTIONS.
A.CEBA Witnesses Barua and Alderfer recommend that the Company offer customer subscriptions that provide a capacity credit in addition to an energy credit. Because Georgia Power procures capacity for the benefit of all customers, it runs contrary to the regulatory compact and could be harmful to existing customers for Georgia Power to offer a capacity subscription for a capacity resource to a single customer. Georgia Power is committed to accurately reflecting the value these resources bring to the system, including capacity value, but does not offer capacity subscriptions, instead offering subscriptions to the carbon free attributes renewable resources deliver. Georgia Power will continue to evaluate the capacity benefits created by solar and solar and storage resources to ensure proper allocation of costs and benefits to generators and subscribing customers.

A.Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol made recommendations regarding the integration of the Company’s quarterly large load economic development reports to improve and refine the underlying assumptions of the LRM. Georgia Interfaith Power & Light (“GIPL”) and Southface Energy Institute (“Southface”) Witness Hotaling criticizes the Company for allegedly failing to use new data from the Company’s large load quarterly reports to update its assumptions about how large load customers will behave. Specifically, Witness Hotaling discusses highlights from her review of data from each of the Company’s quarterly large load reports and provides information on changes observed between the first quarterly report through the fourth quarterly report. Based on her analysis, Witness Hotaling concludes that Georgia Power did not update its large load model to reflect its new data, that Georgia Power’s growth assumptions are inaccurate, and that Georgia Power’s reliance on these allegedly inaccurate assumptions has led to an overstated load forecast. She ultimately recommends, in part, that the Company’s delay probability should be modified to reflect data from the four 2024 quarterly load reports that are available.
Q.HAS THE COMPANY FILED UPDATED DATA FOR THE LRM IN THIS CASE?
A.Yes. On April 4, 2025, the Company filed a hearing request response containing data relevant to a draft February 2025 LRM sensitivity. Exhibit 1 to our testimony contains the complete details of input assumptions that the Company proactively addressed in the February 2025 LRM sensitivity. Section II.D of our testimony includes (i) a comparison of the Company’s February 2025 LRM sensitivity against Staff’s recommended LRM adjustments, (ii) the February 2025 Load Forecast that aggregates the February 2025 LRM sensitivity and the organic load forecast from the 2025 IRP Load and Energy Forecast, (iii) updated seasonal capacity needs for Georgia Power based on the February 2025 Load Forecast, and (iv) the Company’s recommendation to adopt the February 2025 Load Forecast for use in the 2029-2031 All-Source RFP certification proceeding.
Q.WHY ARE INTERVENOR PROPOSALS TO USE DATA FROM PREVIOUS QUARTERLY REPORTS AND LRM VINTAGES NOT APPROPRIATE?
A.The Company’s quarterly report provides the most up-to-date information each time it is filed. For each iteration of the quarterly report, the Company incorporates the latest information in its models. Therefore, each time the quarterly report gets updated, all past information in previous quarterly reports becomes obsolete.
In short, the Company’s latest quarterly report already accounts for changes in new projects, load removals, and load additions. Therefore, incorporating data from previous quarterly reports into the LRM, as Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol suggest, would double count these data points and lead to inaccurate conclusions. Similarly, any trends or regression analyses observed from one report to another, as discussed by GIPL/Southface Witness Hotaling, would also double count the points that are being analyzed. Put simply, there is limited value to information that is extracted from previous large load quarterly reports because it is incorporated into the most recent report. 
Large Load Materialization
Q.WHAT ADJUSTMENTS DID STAFF PROPOSE TO THE COMPANY LOAD FORECAST? 
A.Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol offer several critiques to the substance of the Company’s load forecast, including critiques of both the organic load forecast and the LRM. However, Staff Witnesses only identified one adjustment to the Company’s load forecast, and that adjustment was to the LRM. Staff proposed to alter the materialization assumption for data centers and cryptocurrency currently included in the LRM to align with the materialization assumption for other industry segments (“Uniform LRM”). Staff’s Uniform LRM proposal has the effect of reducing the Company’s peak load forecast by 2,000 MW through 2032.
Q.WHAT IS THE MATERIALIZATION ASSUMPTION IN THE LRM? 
A.The materialization assumption in the LRM represents the difference between actual metered load versus announced load. Staff attempted to make a similar adjustment in the 2023 IRP Update, to which the Company responded that data centers are expected to use most of their connected load. As a result, the Company assigned a higher materialization percentage for data centers and cryptocurrency. In addition, consistent with past practices, the Company continues to communicate directly with large load data center customers and has used this information, as well as other factors discussed later, to inform the materialization assumption used in the LRM.
Q.DOES THE COMPANY BELIEVE IT IS APPROPRIATE TO ADJUST THE MATERIALIZATION ASSUMPTION USED FOR THE LRM AS STAFF HAS PROPOSED IN THIS CASE? 
A.No. Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol took a single year snapshot of three data center projects—which are still in active development—and relied upon that early load data to justify a change in the materialization assumption for all data centers. We do not agree that this small sample size of three projects still under development justifies changing the materialization assumption as proposed by Staff. As the Company explained in response to a data request in this case, an additional analysis of the project materialization assumption is not appropriate at this time due to the limited sample size of large load projects that have reached the mature stage in their load ramps. The Company does agree that, when an adequate sample size of mature projects becomes available, the LRM assumptions for all industry segments should be reevaluated and adjusted, if needed. 
Q.ARE THERE OTHER FACTORS THAT SUPPORT THE COMPANY’S ASSUMPTION AROUND THE MATERIALIZATION OF DATA CENTER LOADS? 
A.Yes. With more than 73 data centers currently on the Company’s system, the Company has significant experience with the operational characteristics and the load factors of data centers. Although not a substitute for actual load materialization data, operational and load forecast data further inform the Company’s materialization assumptions. In the absence of more mature data on materialization of the new large load economic development customers, this operational experience and load factor data are helpful in supporting the differentiation between the assumptions used for data centers and other industry segments. 
Q.DO YOU HAVE CONCERNS WITH STAFF’S RELIANCE ON PROJECT CANCELLATIONS TO JUSTIFY THEIR PROPOSED MATERIALIZATION ADJUSTMENT? 
A.Yes. Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol’s reference to project cancellations as support for their materialization adjustment mixes and matches two separate concepts. Project cancellations are considered as part of the “Project Success” component of the LRM and not as part of the materialization assumption. During direct hearings of Staff and Intervenors, Staff Witness Pol agreed that cancellations are accounted for in the Project Success component of the LRM and not as part of the load materialization assumption. We further discuss the proper consideration of load additions and load removals in the next section of our testimony.
Load Additions and Load Removals
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE INTERVENOR CRITIQUES OF THE COMPANY’S LARGE LOAD PIPELINE FORECAST.
A.Staff and certain Intervenors focus on the removals of load from the Company’s LRM to argue that the Company’s LRM is overstating the load that is coming to Georgia. For example, Staff Witnesses Trokey, Drugan, and Pol state that “[w]hile the [large load pipeline] continues to add new projects and loads, it also documents numerous project removals and load reductions,” and in light of the “significant rate of project removals and net load reductions, modification to the assumptions of the LRM is necessary.” They further call for “[a]ny new load additions to the LRM” to be “treated with scrutiny, as these projects may also be removed, reduced, or otherwise adjusted as time passes.”
Similarly, GIPL/Southface Witness Hotaling describes the “Load Change,” “Load Removed,” and “Load Ramp Change” as part of her analysis of the quarterly reports submitted by Georgia Power. Witness Hotaling further provides recommendations around capturing the risk of certain customers dropping out of Georgia Power’s load queue.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO THESE CRITICISMS?
A.A focus on load removals places undue emphasis on this aspect of the LRM because it fails to account for an equally, if not more, significant aspect: load additions. For every reduction in load in the Company’s LRM, there has been an equal or greater load increase. As Company Witness Valle noted in direct hearings:
For every megawatt that has been removed, 3 megawatts have been added to the pipeline. And that’s why, if you look at the size of the pipeline over time, it continues to grow. So we have cancellations, but we also have additions that more than offset . . . any cancellations so far.
Thus, it is necessary to consider both the load additions together with load removals. The size and scope of any additions are critical information that contextualizes load removals and informs the entire load forecast. Load additions—even if tempered by load removals—drive the Company’s planning decisions because the Company must plan and build resources if its overall load increases. 
Q.WHAT ARE THE POTENTIAL RESULTS OF AN OVEREMPHASIS ON LOAD REMOVALS?
A.An over emphasis on load removals without due consideration of additions to the pipeline could result in a biased load forecast. The Company’s goal is to have as accurate a forecast as possible, and the Company continues its reasonable practice of revising its forecast assumptions and approach with each iteration to incorporate changes in customer demand and market conditions. Importantly, an over emphasis on any one component reduces the likelihood of producing an accurate forecast by either over-forecasting or under-forecasting load, which could increase reliability risks for customers and the System. To correct for under-forecasting load, Georgia Power might have to shed load or make expensive market purchases to serve load. Unlike the recommendations of Staff and Intervenors, the Company’s approach appropriately balances the impact of load removals and load additions in the large load pipeline, leading to a more reasonable estimation of the load it will be required to serve. 
Q. DOES STAFF’S PROPOSED LOAD FORECAST PROPERLY CONSIDER LOAD ADDITIONS AND LOAD REMOVALS? 
A. It does not. Staff compares its proposed load forecast to the Company’s February 2025 LRM sensitivity and finds that both align through 2028, with Staff’s forecast showing lower loads after 2028. Staff’s adjusted forecast is based upon the same large load economic development pipeline the Company used to develop the 2025 IRP, which largely aligns with the Q2 2024 Large Load Economic Development Report. This, however, is not the pipeline that was used to create the February 2025 LRM sensitivity. The February 2025 LRM sensitivity is based upon a pipeline that is more than double that of the pipeline assumed by Staff in its proposed forecast, which is the primary driver of the differential between the Company’s February 2025 LRM sensitivity and Staff’s proposed forecast. When load additions are appropriately considered using the pipeline from the February 2025 LRM sensitivity, Staff’s Uniform LRM produces significantly higher loads than the Company’s February 2025 LRM sensitivity. This is further illustrated in Section II.D.
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE GIPL/SOUTHFACE WITNESS HOTALING’S CRITIQUE OF THE COMPANY’S ANALYSIS OF LOAD CANCELLATIONS.
A.Witness Hotaling recommends including a probability referred to as “P1A” to reflect the success rate for customers not dropping out of the new large load queue. Witness Hotaling notes that the Company’s “P3 probability, or Georgia Power’s probability for contract success, is included in the [LRM] to reflect the success rate of a customer materializing after a contract with Georgia Power has been signed.” Further, Witness Hotaling alleges that “even with a signed contract, the model does not reflect the probability of a customer leaving Georgia Power’s load queue if they are considered in the technical review stage with no commitments.” Therefore, Witness Hotaling argues that “additional risk of these customers leaving the queue should be incorporated into the [LRM].”
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO WITNESS HOTALING’S CRITICISM?
A.Witness Hotaling describes load materialization in tandem with success rates and other concepts, but each of these terms carry a distinct meaning. There is also a meaningful difference between load materialization and success rate. Load materialization accounts for the difference between the metered load being served, as measured at the customer’s meter, and the load announced (or design capacity) by the customer, expressed as a percentage. Separately, the success rate for each customer is determined by incremental customer decisions toward becoming a Georgia Power customer. These decisions include selecting the state of Georgia (P1), signing a contract with Georgia Power (P2), and reaching commercial operation after the contract is signed (P3).
Further, cancellations and dropouts are already considered as part of project success in the LRM. For a new large load project to be considered successful, all three of the above events (i.e., state selection, signing a contract for electric service with Georgia Power being chosen as the electric service provider, and the project reaching commercial operation) must occur. In other words, project success or failure (dropouts and cancellations) is a binary event the probability of which is captured by the product of each of these three events (i.e., the formula P = P1*P2*P3). Therefore, accounting for additional risk, as Witness Hotaling argues, is duplicative and unnecessary.
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE GIPL/SOUTHFACE’S CRITICISM OF THE COMPANY’S REDUCTION TO THE LOAD FORECAST FOR DEMAND SIDE MANAGEMENT SAVINGS.
A.GIPL/Southface Witness Hotaling argues that “there was a gap in the savings between Georgia Power’s DSM Plan and what was included in the IRP load forecast” and states that she adjusted for that difference in her analysis. GIPL/Southface Witness Sherwood alleges that Georgia Power “used numbers that did not represent the [DSM] Proposed Case’s energy and demand savings,” and that the numbers used were “significantly lower than the Proposed Case,” which could undercount possible Demand Side Management (“DSM”) savings.
Q.PLEASE CLARIFY HOW GEORGIA POWER REDUCES THE ENERGY AND DEMAND FORECAST FOR DSM SAVINGS BEFORE CONSIDERING SUPPLY SIDE ALTERNATIVES. 
A.As an initial step in the planning process, the Company reduces the energy and demand forecast by the amount of projected savings from continuing the Company’s currently approved DSM programs. As explained by Dr. Beppler of the Customer Programs Panel in the Company’s direct hearing, demand response programs and dispatchable demand side options are captured as contributions to the resource plan, and energy efficiency or passive DSM is captured as a reduction to the energy forecast. The Company’s adjustments reflect the continuation of dispatchable demand side options and continued incremental energy savings in the energy forecast that would be generated if the Company’s existing portfolio of DSM programs were to continue into the future. Therefore, a correction is needed to the statement on page 51 of the 2025 IRP Main Document where Georgia Power describes this process. The last sentence of the first paragraph in Section 7.3.2 should read as follows: “[i]n fact, the first step in the Company’s IRP process is to reduce the Company’s load and energy forecasts by the current DSM Plan’s energy and demand impacts, prior to developing supply-side alternatives.”

Q.WHY ARE THE COMPANY’S ADJUSTMENTS TO THE LRM INPUTS USED FOR THE FEBRUARY SENSITIVITY MORE APPROPRIATE THAN STAFF’S UNIFORM LOAD MATERIALIZATION ADJUSTMENT?
A.The Company’s adjustments to the LRM inputs used for the February 2025 sensitivity are more appropriate than Staff’s Uniform Adjustment for several reasons. These reasons are rooted in the dynamic nature of the large load customer portfolio and the need to evolve forecasting models to better reflect the latest available information.
The February LRM Sensitivity was developed by considering new market information that emerged after the Company prepared its 2025 IRP Load and Energy Forecast in August 2024 and filed the 2025 IRP in January 2025. Specifically, there has been a notable increase in the pipeline size and composition of data center customers, which requires adjustments to accurately reflect current conditions. Staff’s Uniform Adjustment does not account for these recent developments because it relies on a modification of the 2025 IRP LRM as originally filed.
In the February 2025 LRM, the Company introduced two key changes to enhance the accuracy of the load forecast:
Revised Materialization Assumptions: The Company now differentiates between types of data center customers based on their likelihood of materialization. Colocator data centers without committed tenants and data center developers are now modeled at lower materialization levels compared to hyperscalers and colocators with committed tenants. Without committed tenants, there is increased uncertainty regarding the level at which facilities will be contracted, and therefore, they are modeled at lower materialization levels. A uniform adjustment, as Staff proposes, would apply a blanket assumption that does not accurately capture these nuances and would not recognize some early evidence of hyperscalers materializing at 80% or higher.
Commitment Likelihood Adjustment: The Company has shifted the commitment likelihood from the likelihood of signing a Request for Service (“RFS”) to a stage-based likelihood of signing a Contract for Electric Service (“CES”). This change provides more certainty to the forecast by focusing on a more definitive stage of customer commitment. The likelihood of signing a CES is a more reliable indicator of actual load materialization than the likelihood of signing an RFS, which is an earlier and less binding stage in the customer engagement process.
In summary, the Company’s adjustments are more appropriate because they incorporate recent market insights and refine the LRM to better capture the complexities and uncertainties in customer behavior, particularly in the data center sector. These changes result in a more accurate and reliable load forecast that supports the planning needs of this filing.

RESERVE MARGIN STUDY
Q.WHAT ARE THE COMPANY’S RECOMMENDED VALUES FOR THE WINTER AND SUMMER TARGET RESERVE MARGINS?
A.The Company proposes to maintain a Winter TRM of 26% and increase the Summer TRM to 20%. For the short term (2024-2026), the Company plans to adopt System targets of 25.5% for winter and 19.5% for summer.
Q.WHAT CONCERNS ARE THERE IF THE TARGET RESERVE MARGIN IS SET TOO LOW?
A.The Company uses the reserve margin for reliability planning purposes. It is designed to minimize the combined expected costs of maintaining planning reserve capacity, production costs, and customer costs associated with service interruptions. Importantly, a reserve margin also balances these costs against risk of outages and minimum levels of reliability. 
While a higher reserve margin may increase costs to customers from having more generation resources available to serve load, a reserve margin that is too low creates far greater risks in terms of reliability. During extreme weather events, like those experienced in recent years, an insufficient reserve margin could leave the Company without sufficient power to serve load. If the Company does not have sufficient resources to serve load, the Company would be required to shed firm load and curtail service to customers to prevent larger-scale blackouts. Put simply, a lower reserve margin increases the risk of a reliability event and the loss of power to customers when they need it most.
While these scenarios may seem theoretical, utilities in the United States have confronted these reliability issues head-on in recent years and months. The brownouts—intentional load shedding—in New Orleans over Memorial Day weekend (May 25, 2025) were ordered by MISO, the area’s regional transmission organization, to avoid blackouts due to increased customer demand, transmission constraints, and generation outages leading to an inability to serve load. This event impacted approximately 100,000 customers in a major metropolitan area on a late spring day, not even during the heat of the summer months to come. 
In a similar vein, the shedding of firm load by some Southeastern utilities during Winter Storm Elliott on December 24, 2022, emphasized the importance of reliable service. While other utilities were forced to shed load, Georgia Power was able to avoid the same outcome due in part to its emphasis on reliability. 
Although the Company recognizes there is a cost to maintain a higher reserve margin, there are also significant costs to not being able to serve customer load, many of which—school and business closures, and in extreme cases, health and safety concerns—do not show up on customer bills.
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY ADDRESS THESE POSSIBILITIES IN ITS RESERVE MARGIN STUDY?
A.The Company designs its Reserve Margin Study to include the cost of reliability, ensuring that the risk of significant outages such as the ones previously described are minimized. Additionally, the Company establishes a TRM that, at a minimum, meets the industry practice of no more than a projected one loss of load event every ten years (“1:10 LOLE”), while also seeking to balance the cost of generation with the cost of unserved energy. 
In other words, the Company seeks to minimize the true costs of reliability to customers and maximize the benefits to customers of avoiding outages, all by ensuring the target reserve margin is appropriately set. 
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE STAFF’S CRITIQUES OF THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED WINTER TARGET RESERVE MARGIN.
A.Staff contends that the Company’s proposed 26% Winter TRM is too high. In support of this conclusion, Staff identifies four issues with the Company’s TRM analysis. They contend: (1) the Company’s Load Forecast Error (“LFE”) distribution was overstated due to model errors, which allegedly overstated the TRM; (2) the Company’s data center load assumptions contributed to an overstated TRM; (3) the Company’s cold weather data training models resulted in overstated weather-adjusted loads for certain extremely low temperature conditions; and (4) the removal of a single month of cold weather would reduce the winter TRM to less than 24%.
Q.IS STAFF’S RECOMMENDED TARGET RESERVE MARGIN OF 24.5% APPROPRIATE?
A.No. As a preliminary matter, Staff acknowledges that the Company’s recommended Winter and Summer TRMs were based on the same general methodology of prior IRPs. As with these prior IRPs, the Company’s proposed 26% margin has served customers well during recent storms such as Winter Storm Elliott in 2022, Winter Storm Heather in 2024, and Winter Storms Enzo and Cora in early 2025. Notwithstanding the consistency in methodology and resulting customer benefits from the Company maintaining an adequate reserve margin during significant weather events, Staff recommends a 24.5% Winter TRM, similar to their recommendations in 2019 and 2022. 
Staff’s recommendation would result in Georgia Power falling below the 1:10 LOLE industry practice, meaning Georgia Power would be expected to have more than one reliability event every ten years. This level of reliability would place Georgia at a reliability level lower than surrounding states, which have followed Georgia Power in moving to seasonal planning and increasing winter Target Reserve Margins over the past several years. That result is unacceptable to the Company and should be to this Commission as well. Further, falling below that industry practice LOLE metric would mean that Georgia Power would be at a reliability level lower than other states with which the state of Georgia competes for economic development opportunities, putting the state at a competitive disadvantage to attract economic development opportunities. 
Finally, uncertainty around the new load—uncertainty that Staff itself recognizes—means that a reduction at this stage of the planning process would not be prudent. Events of unavailability of power in recent years and months show that operating electric grids is becoming more challenging. In a time of changing customer dynamics, increasing importance of electric service in everyday life, and unprecedented economic growth, a reduction in the reliability of the electric grid could lead to catastrophic results.
Load Forecast Error Distribution
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE STAFF’S PROPOSAL REGARDING THE COMPANY’S LOAD FORECAST ERROR DISTRIBUTION.
A.Staff disagrees with the Company’s Load Forecast Error (“LFE”) methodology, the smallest risk component of the Reserve Margin Study, stating that “the LFE distribution should be limited to the effect of the uncertainty associated with the economic drivers of the forecast (i.e., the uncertainty associated with the economy in general) and should not include an additional model error component.” Further, Staff contends that the Company’s inclusion of forecast model statistical error “simply expanded the LFE distribution and overstated the TRM calculation,” even though the Company’s LFE range in this IRP is only 0.23% higher than the LFE range used in the 2022 IRP. In support of its conclusion, Staff looked to reserve margin studies for other utilities that were performed by Astrapé using its SERVM model.
 Q.DESPITE STAFF’S CRITICISM, IS IT APPROPRIATE TO CONSIDER MODEL ERROR IN THE LOAD FORECAST ERROR COMPONENT?
A.Yes. Load forecasts are inherently uncertain, and the LFE component of the Reserve Margin Study is designed to address this uncertainty. Even if the Company had perfect foresight into future economic conditions, it would nevertheless be impossible to produce a perfect load forecast due to the inherent imperfections of the models used to produce the forecast. Therefore, the Company represents this uncertainty as a combination of both economic uncertainty, which Staff agrees is a reasonable consideration, as well as uncertainty associated with attempting to translate economic data into forecasted customer loads. While these model errors are relatively small compared to the overall magnitude of the load forecast, it is both reasonable and appropriate to include them when considering load forecast error in the Company’s Reserve Margin Study. 
Q.IS STAFF’S RELIANCE ON THE RESERVE MARGIN STUDIES FOR OTHER UTILITIES APPROPRIATE?
A.No. Importantly, Astrapé did not perform Georgia Power’s Reserve Margin Study. Therefore, Astrapé’s analysis for other utilities is inapplicable to Georgia Power’s Reserve Margin Study. Moreover, Staff mistakenly applied a 40% adjustment factor to the LFE “to reduce the economic uncertainty factors developed by the Company, consistent with the Astrapé approach.” That adjustment, however, was incorrect to apply to Georgia Power’s LFE because the economic forecast incorporated into Georgia Power’s Reserve Margin Study was different than the GDP-based forecast used by Astrapé in the other studies. Staff’s application of that adjustment factor skews the results of the LFE analysis.
Q.IS STAFF’S PROPOSED RESERVE MARGIN APPROPRIATE EVEN IN LIGHT OF ITS ATTEMPT TO REMOVE LFE?
A.No. Relying on its review of the Astrapé studies, Staff calculated an approximation of a winter TRM after removing the LFE analysis, resulting in a winter TRM of 24.6%. Rather than approximating this analysis, Staff could have relied on the analysis it requested the Company perform, which was to model the results of the Company’s Reserve Margin Study without the impact of LFEs. In response to STF-JKA-4-4, the Company modeled the winter TRM with the LFE set to 0%, which showed that a 25% reserve margin meets the 1:10 LOLE threshold, which Staff acknowledges is the industry standard for reliability. In other words, when removing the impact of LFEs, the reserve margin is higher than Staff’s recommendation.
Q.IS STAFF’S PROPOSED LFE DISTRIBUTION REASONABLE?
A.No. Staff’s proposed LFE distribution of -.95% to 0.57% is unreasonable, especially given that Staff developed its LFE distribution using information from various other utilities whose LFE distributions are wider than Staff’s proposal. For instance, Staff highlights Santee Cooper’s reserve margin study, which included an LFE distribution that ranged from -4% to +4%, which is similar to Georgia Power’s LFE distribution of –6.12% to 5.16%.

Data Center Weather Sensitivities
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE INTERVENOR CRITICISMS REGARDING THE WEATHER SENSITIVITY OF DATA CENTERS.
A.Staff suggests that the Company’s data center load assumptions contributed to an overstatement of the Company’s Winter TRM, namely, that the weather modeling approach used by the Company to adjust the base year load forecast was applied to the entirety of the load forecast even though data center loads are less weather sensitive. Accordingly, Staff argues that data centers should have received a lower adjustment to accurately reflect system impacts.
Q.HOW DOES THE COMPANY ACCOUNT FOR DATA CENTER WEATHER SENSITIVITY IN THE RESERVE MARGIN STUDY?
A.The development of the load shapes used for the Reserve Margin Study includes a model that establishes aggregate load sensitivity to temperature. The model is trained with actual temperature and load data from the most recent five-year period. Existing data center customers are present in the training data, and the model recognizes that not all customer classes have the same sensitivity to temperature. The resulting aggregate impact should represent diversity in customer class. As additional data center load materializes, the aggregate impact of temperature sensitivity will be present in the future model training data. In future Reserve Margin Studies, the Company could segregate data center load from other customer classes if the materialization of data center load and the observed temperature sensitivity justifies this change.  
Q.IS THE COMPANY’S MODELING APPROACH REASONABLE? 
A.The Company’s modeling approach is a reasonable approach given the uncertain and nascent nature of the data center industry. The Company is open to revising and refining its modeling approach if future developments warrant such adjustments, but only after additional information on the data center industry becomes available. Thus, the Company does not agree with reducing the reserve margin based on potential data center load patterns at this time. As stated by Witness Looney in the 2025 IRP direct hearings:
[W]e’re not going to bring forth a recommendation to lower the reserve margin on the speculation that a certain customer class that we expect to materialize over the next five or six years behaves in a way that makes that turn out to be okay . . . . [W]e’re not going to take a risk on setting a reserve margin based on the customer behavior that we have not yet been able to fully implement in a statistically meaningful way.
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE STAFF’S CRITIQUE OF THE COMPANY’S USE OF FIFTY YEARS OF WEATHER DATA TO INFORM ITS RESERVE MARGIN STUDY.
A.Staff testified that it reviewed Southern Company weather data for the years used in the Company’s Reserve Margin Study in this case and found only three years in the 1980s when the minimum temperature fell below five degrees. Staff argues that these years “had a disproportionately large impact on the resulting winter TRM produced by the SERVM analysis.” According to Staff, use of thirty years of data, as opposed to fifty, would result in a more reasonable TRM.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO STAFF’S CONCERN?
A.While the Company is not recommending a reserve margin that ensures it can serve all load during all potential extreme temperatures, there is no reason to ignore such weather data in analyzing risks that the Company might encounter. Indeed, Staff witnesses admit that they are not suggesting that low temperatures from previous timeframes will never happen again. Therefore, including this information in the Company’s analysis plays an important role and such data should not be ignored. The Company’s use of 50 years of data provides a more robust view of the potential weather extremes the system may face. 
Additionally, it is reasonable to expect that in a Reserve Margin Study, a small subset of weather years will have a significant impact on the results. In fact, given the inclusion of 50 years of weather data and a 1:10 LOLE threshold, approximately five weather years should dictate the required level of reserves. In the Company’s Reserve Margin Study, this happens to correlate with years in which temperatures fall below 10 degrees Fahrenheit. Accordingly, excluding such weather years in the analysis simply shifts the reliability risk for customers to higher temperatures. 
SCENARIO DESIGN, EXPANSION PLANNING, AND RESOURCE MIX STUDY
Q.WHY DOES THE COMPANY PERFORM EXPANSION PLANNING MODELING?
A.The Company’s expansion planning analysis identifies the economically optimal mix of resources that reliably meet future customer capacity and energy needs across several plausible planning scenarios. The expansion plans indicate which types of resources provide value to customers. The expansion plans also provide guidance as to the amount of resources to be sought in renewable RFPs. Importantly, the expansion plans are combined with the existing fleet of resources as inputs into more detailed production cost modeling, which produces projected energy costs over the planning horizon. These projected energy costs are used in the Company’s resource evaluations, including RFP evaluations, unit retirement studies, unit upgrade analyses, and demand side and program evaluations. Significantly, these models need to be realistic so that avoided cost signals are representative and provide a firm basis for analysis.
Q.ARE THE RESULTS OF ITS EXPANSION PLANNING MODELING CONSIDERED A FORMAL DECISION ON RESOURCE PLANNING?
A.No. As this panel has stated previously, the Company’s generic expansion plans do not represent a resource planning decision by Georgia Power and are not commitments for resources. Instead, the expansion plans are indicative of what may be an optimal mix of resources within various future scenarios. While expansion planning is an important step in the decision-making process, it does not itself represent a formal plan or decision. In fact, the Company is required to procure supply side resources through an RFP process. In short, these results are not commitments on the Company’s part, and they do not drive the Company’s specific resource actions.
Build Limits
Q.WHAT DO VARIOUS INTERVENORS RECOMMEND REGARDING THE COMPANY’S BUILD LIMITS IN ITS MODELING?
A.GCVEF Witness Roumpani recommends a justification for annual build limits and recommends exploring conditions under which the limits could be relaxed, particularly for renewable energy and storage resources.
GIPL Witness Hotaling expresses concerns about the Company’s “restrictive build limits on solar” and recommends that Georgia Power present data without annual build limits applied to new solar resources.
Sierra Club/SACE/NRDC Witness Stenclik similarly recommends removing annual build limits on solar, wind, and storage in at least one unconstrained scenario to assess least-cost outcomes. He further recommends that the Company be required to justify how its build limits were developed and explain any changes to these build limits.
Finally, SREA Witness Palomino alleges that the IRP does not discuss the drivers for the Company’s annual build limits and fails to elaborate on the reasons for wind and solar limits. Witness Palomino argues that when built capacity reaches the annual limit, “it suggests that the system has a greater appetite, and therefore economic incentive, to build wind and solar capacity than is permitted by Georgia Power’s adopted constraints.”
Q.WHAT IS THE SIGNIFICANCE OF THE COMPANY’S BUILD LIMITS IN ITS EXPANSION PLANNING MODELING?
A.The build limits on generic resources are an important part of the Company’s expansion planning process. As previously noted, expansion plans are combined with the existing fleet of resources as inputs into more detailed production cost modeling to produce projected energy costs over the planning horizon. These projected energy costs are then used in the Company’s resource evaluations, including RFP evaluations, unit retirement studies, unit upgrade analyses, and demand side and program evaluations. Because these cases are used to evaluate and make subsequent resource decisions, it is important for the Company to assume practical limits, based on the Company’s prior experience and knowledge, for all resources in its mix analysis. 
Q.SOME INTERVENORS, LIKE SIERRA CLUB/SACE/NRDC, SUGGEST THAT PLANNING MODEL ASSUMPTIONS SHOULD BE RELAXED WHEN RESOURCE LIMITS ARE REACHED EVERY YEAR. IS THIS APPROPRIATE?
A.No. Intervenors argue that if the Company’s AURORA model were unconstrained by build limits, it might seek to build more solar, wind, or other renewable resources than what the Company’s build limits allow. However, higher amounts of resources may not always be practical to build, and the Company’s build limits help ensure the model is performing its analysis within realistic parameters and providing appropriate projections of avoided energy costs.
In contrast, Sierra Club/SACE/NRDC Witness Stenclik testified that modeling exercises do not need to be realistic. This is a flawed conclusion given that the Company uses its modeling results to inform and evaluate decisions in other contexts. Witness Stenclik acknowledged that actual resource planning decisions are up to the Commission and Company, and therefore, it is crucial for the Company and the Commission to base decisions on realistic models. 
Q.IS IT A COMMON INDUSTRY PRACTICE TO INCORPORATE BUILD LIMITS IN EXPANSION MODELING?
A.Yes. Most electric utilities include build limit assumptions in their expansion plans as part of their Integrated Resource Plans. For example, both Duke Energy Carolinas and Dominion Energy in Virginia have included such limits in their IRPs.
Integrated Modeling
Q.WHAT DOES STAFF PROPOSE REGARDING THE COMPANY’S USE OF INTEGRATED MODELING FOR THE RESOURCE MIX STUDY? 
A.The witness panel of Newsome, Hayet, Sandonato, and Wellborn testified that “[a]lthough an economic analysis was performed in each of [the six different studies performed by the Company], the studies were not integrated.” Staff noted that “an integrated analysis would have allowed the Company to determine a project ranked order based on which incremental project would provide the most value to the system.” Staff further testified that it performed an integrated analysis that concurrently evaluated a host of resource decisions, options, and retirements.
Q.WHAT IS THE COMPANY’S RESPONSE TO STAFF’S CRITIQUE?
A.Staff’s recommendation of an integrated analysis is not practical from the Company’s perspective. As acknowledged by Staff Witnesses Newsome, Hayet, Sandonato, and Wellborn during the Staff and Intervenor hearing, to be able to run an integrated model, all inputs must be ready in order to run that integrated model. Thus, while Staff’s underlying reasoning may be factually accurate, Staff has the benefit of receiving a completed integrated resource plan with all of the Company’s inputs already available to them. In comparison, when the Company develops its IRP for filing, the Company does not have the same benefit of having complete inputs available all at the same time. Rather, many of the Company’s studies supporting the IRP are conducted sequentially and rely on the outputs of a completed study as an input for another study. For example, the Company did not have (and in fact, could not have) the results of the All-Source RFP at the time that it performed its Resource Mix Study, yet Staff nevertheless recommends that the Company perform a complete analysis with missing information.
Q.DESPITE RUNNING AN INTEGRATED ANALYSIS, DID THE STAFF AND COMPANY REACH SIMILAR CONCLUSIONS RELATED TO THE COMPANY REQUESTS IN THIS IRP? 
A.Yes. The Witness panel of Newsome, Hayet, Sandonato, and Wellborn testified that “for the incremental resource additions, we have concluded the same” as the Company.

RENEWABLE INTEGRATION STUDY
Q.WHAT CONCERN DOES STAFF RAISE REGARDING THE RENEWABLE INTEGRATION STUDY’S USE IN THE COMPANY’S PLANNING PROCESS?
A.Staff Witnesses Barber, Kaduk, and Bower state in part that the Company’s Renewable Integration Study is “essentially a theoretical exercise, and the model methodology is not designed to quantify a specific cost actually incurred on Georgia Power’s system.” Staff further alleges that the Company “has not adequately demonstrated that it is appropriate for use in the Company’s planning process.”
Q.WHY DOES THE COMPANY CONDUCT A RENEWABLE INTEGRATION STUDY AND WHY DOES IT APPLY IT IN RENEWABLE ENERGY PROCUREMENTS?
A.As intermittent resources are added to the system, the integration costs identified by the Company’s Renewable Integration Study represent the costs associated with any increase in operating reserves that is necessary to ensure the Southern Company System maintains its ability to meet reliability standard requirements. To account for the additional cost of these operation reserves to respond to variation in generation levels, the study identifies a value that is allocated as a cost to intermittent resources or a benefit to resources, such as BESS, which address the volatility by serving as operating reserves. Therefore, the Renewable Integration Study is more than just a mere theoretical exercise; in fact, the Renewable Integration Study has very real implications to the Company’s resource evaluations and decisions.
UNIT UPGRADES 
Nuclear Unit Upgrades
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED NUCLEAR UNIT UPGRADES.
A.Staff testified that the Company’s proposed nuclear unit upgrades are economic but recommended that the Commission approve the upgrades at Plant Vogtle Units 1-2 first, then delay the upgrades at Plant Hatch Units 1-2 until two years after the Vogtle upgrades are completed. In support of this recommendation, Staff testified that the delay will “allow the Company to incorporate its experience with [the] Vogtle 1-2 upgrade in its execution of the Hatch 1-2 upgrade.”
Q.WILL THE COMPANY’S EXPERIENCE FROM VOGTLE UNITS 1-2 INFORM THE COMPANY’S UPGRADES AT PLANT HATCH?
A.No. While Plants Vogtle and Hatch are both nuclear units, their similarities generally end there. Among other differences, Plant Vogtle uses pressurized water reactors, while Plant Hatch uses boiling water reactors; both units also differ in their containment types. In essence, while both units are nuclear units, they are distinctly different nuclear units. 
Staff argues that the upgrade projects are mostly on the secondary side of the nuclear units—i.e., the thermal side—and that therefore, any difference in the nuclear technology does not matter. However, the Company also has vast experience with the thermal side of power plants, both at these two nuclear plants as well as at every other plant in its fleet with a thermal component. Therefore, the value of any lessons learned or experience from the Vogtle units is outweighed by the economic costs of Staff’s proposed delay.
Furthermore, the Company has performed three prior upgrades at Plant Hatch and two prior upgrades at Plant Vogtle. Accordingly, the Company already has experience at both units to support the timely and efficient upgrades at both plants. Therefore, Staff’s suggestion that the Company can incorporate lessons learned from its experience from one nuclear unit to another is not as practical as the witnesses suggest, and certainly not valuable enough to delay the projects and delay and reduce benefits to customers.
Q.ASIDE FROM EXPERIENCE OR LESSONS LEARNED, IS IT ECONOMICAL TO DELAY THE UPGRADES AT EITHER NUCLEAR UNIT?
A.No. Staff notes that these projects are “capital intensive, will require significant lead time . . . , and will take a relatively long time to break even.” However, Staff acknowledged that these projects were in fact economic, and the capital costs associated with these projects would still be lower than new nuclear. Delaying these projects reduces their economic value and reduces the remaining asset lives over which to enjoy the benefits. Specifically, Staff’s proposed schedule would delay the capacity benefit from Hatch from 2029 to 2032 at the earliest, which immediately reduces the economics of the projects. Delays can also increase costs associated with general inflation, extended AFUDC, labor retention, preventative maintenance of units, and tax credit applicability. Other utilities around the nation are pursing similar nuclear upgrades—this creates increased competition for labor, delays potential procurements, and requires greater storage costs as the Company must store long-lead items more than necessary.
Notably, to support their conclusion, Staff only modeled a two-year delay for these upgrades; Staff did not model and did not include in its testimony any evidence that their proposed delay of these upgrades would maintain the economic value provided by the performance of the upgrades as proposed by the Company. Any assumption that the delay of nuclear upgrades would be economic is unsubstantiated. These nuclear upgrade projects represent an opportunity to obtain additional carbon free, nuclear generation that would not otherwise present itself for many years. 
Hydro Unit Upgrades
Q.PLEASE SUMMARIZE STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION REGARDING THE COMPANY’S PROPOSED HYDRO UNIT UPGRADES.
A.Staff recommends that the Company’s proposed hydro modernization request be denied, but nevertheless supports the expansion of the Company’s proposed hydro modernization program generally and recommends the Commission approve up to $100 million to support preliminary investigation and engineering studies through 2027. Staff argues that the results from these investigations and studies would allow the Commission to better determine how to proceed with hydro modernization.
Q.CAN THE HYDRO MODERNIZATION PROGRAM CONTINUE UNTIL THE 2028 IRP UNDER STAFF’S RECOMMENDATION?
A.No. As proposed by Staff, the preliminary investigation and engineering studies would precede equipment procurement, which is impractical for how hydro units are engineered. In preliminary meetings with Staff, the Company explained that, due to the unit-specific nature of hydro plants, hydro turbines are typically the first step in the engineering process, and engineers design the custom replacement based on the turbine that is procured. Unlike other generation resources, any preliminary engineering performed before the procurement of equipment would not be usable, and the same work would likely need to be repeated after the equipment is actually procured. In other words, Staff’s proposal to front-end investigation and engineering studies would lead to increased spending on engineering and design costs.
Q.ARE ALL CURRENTLY APPROVED HYDRO MODERNIZATION PROJECTS EXPECTED TO BE COMPLETED BEFORE THE 2028 IRP?
A.Yes. Plant Tugalo Unit 4 was completed in April 2025, and the Company currently expects to complete 10 of the 14 remaining approved units by the end of 2026 and the last four approved units in 2027. Therefore, the currently approved projects are all expected to be completed before the 2028 IRP. Delaying any new projects will create a gap in the modernization program, as the program will cease entirely before being approved again and modernization of additional units will not be completed for potentially three to six years beyond the schedule included in the Company’s request. This delay will lead to inefficiencies, increase costs, and likely result in a loss of an assembled and skilled labor workforce. Accordingly, the Company must commence additional units in 2026 in order to optimize the hydro modernization program for the benefit of customers. 
Q.IS THE COMPANY’S HYDRO MODERNIZATION REQUEST IN THIS PROCEEDING THE LEAST-COST PLAN TO CUSTOMERS?
A.Yes. By building on the momentum of the current hydro modernization program, the approval of all additional projects to be completed as soon as possible is the lowest cost option for customers. This path ensures that the Company and customers avoid increased costs caused by delays and decreased efficiencies.
Q.DID THE COMPANY’S COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS FIND HYDRO MODERNIZATION TO BE ECONOMIC FOR CUSTOMERS?
A.Yes. For all plants except Morgan Falls, the Company’s plan found hydro modernization to be economic for customers. As this panel previously testified, the high level of qualitative benefits provided by Morgan Falls support the continued investment and modernization of Morgan Falls notwithstanding the less favorable economics.
TRANSMISSION PLANNING
Q.PLEASE BRIEFLY REINTRODUCE THE TRANSMISSION COMPONENTS OF THE 2025 IRP. 
A. The 2025 IRP includes (i) the 2024 Georgia Integrated Transmission System (“ITS”) Ten-Year Plan, which incorporates generation and load growth updates for Georgia Power, Georgia Transmission Corporation (“GTC”), the Municipal Electric Authority of Georgia (“MEAG Power”), and Dalton Utilities (collectively, the “ITS Participants”), (ii) updates on strategic transmission projects since the 2022 IRP to address South to North transmission constraints, and (iii) additional considerations for evolving System needs beyond the traditional ten-year transmission planning window. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO THE RECOMMENDATION OF SEVERAL PARTIES THAT GEORGIA POWER SHOULD ADOPT A MULTI-VALUE, PROACTIVE TRANSMISSION PLANNING PROCESS.
A.It is important to first highlight that, as explained in response to similar recommendations in the 2023 IRP Update, Georgia Power’s existing planning framework and process already incorporates most of the parties’ suggestions. The Company’s IRP process carefully and intentionally considers the balance between transmission investment and generation deployment. Through the existing robust IRP and RFP processes, Georgia Power can accurately evaluate transmission and generation decisions during the resource planning and procurement processes. Transmission represents one component in this planning process; however, long-term firm commitments by third parties under a FERC-jurisdictional Open Access Transmission Tariff (“OATT”) also drive transmission planning evaluations and decisions.
As discussed more fully below, the Company engages, coordinates, and plans locally with ITS Participants, neighboring systems, and utilities on transmission planning issues. The Southeast Regional Transmission Planning (“SERTP”) transmission planning process also coordinates across the southeast to seek input, identify and review system improvements using least-cost and reliable solutions from all reasonably available alternatives, similar to those recommended by the parties.
Further, Georgia Power is already prudently considering and developing the planning and process improvements that Staff and Intervenor parties recommend as part of evolving transmission planning processes and consistent with requirements of FERC Order 1920.
Q.PLEASE DESCRIBE HOW REGIONAL COLLABORATION AND COORDINATION INFORM THE TRANSMISSION PLANNING PROCESS IN GEORGIA.
A.Sections D1 and E1 of Technical Appendix Volume 3 outline the Georgia ITS and SERTP planning processes. Both processes demonstrate tight coordination in transmission planning efforts among the ITS Participants in Georgia and with neighboring utilities.
Staff and Intervenors offer recommendations that seemingly disregard the integrated nature of the transmission system in Georgia, whereby Georgia Power jointly plans and operates the networked transmission system with all ITS Participants. To that end, the Ten-Year Plan incorporates generation and load growth updates for Georgia Power and all other ITS Participants, including changes since the 2022 IRP and 2023 IRP Update. This collaborative planning approach fosters natural checks and balances within transmission system planning and the development of future transmission project solutions.
Q.DOES GEORGIA POWER COORDINATE AND PARTICIPATE IN REGIONAL PLANNING WITH NEIGHBORING SYSTEMS AND UTILITIES? 
A.Yes. Southern Company and Georgia Power work with neighboring utilities directly and through SERTP to ensure a robust, cost-effective transmission plan that meets customer needs and ensures reliability. The SERTP process is a collaboration of nine transmission planning entities in an 11-state area that coordinates regional transmission planning and provides an open and transparent forum for regional planning and coordination, stakeholder input, and stakeholder engagement. The SERTP planning process consists of a bottom-up planning model that combines the state-level IRP and expansion plans of each sponsor, ensuring that drivers of future needs are incorporated and done in accordance with such state oversight. Importantly, SERTP is made up of vertically integrated utilities who each have an obligation to provide reliable, cost-effective energy to customers.
In addition to SERTP, examples of planning, communication, and coordination with neighboring utilities and systems include biannual coordination meetings between Southern Company and its interconnected neighboring utilities, the SERC Reliability Corporation Long-Term Working Group (“LTWG”), and from time-to-time the Eastern Interconnection Planning Collaborative (“EIPC”). In these venues, studies may be performed as needed to determine impacts resulting from changes in transmission topology on both sides of a given interface.
Q.DOES GEORGIA POWER COORDINATE TRANSMISSION PLANNING ACROSS THE SOUTHERN COMPANY SYSTEM?
A.Yes. Georgia Power jointly plans with Southern Company, Alabama Power Company, and Mississippi Power Company at the system level. The Ten-Year Plan included in the 2025 IRP represents one component of that joint system planning process. 
Q. IS THERE A SHARING MECHANISM FOR ALLOCATING COSTS TO THE OPERATING COMPANIES?
A.Yes. Facility costs are allocated to operating companies through the Transmission Facility Cost Allocation Tariff on file with FERC. 
Q.PLEASE PROVIDE A BRIEF OVERVIEW OF FERC ORDER 1920 AND DISCUSS HOW IT WILL IMPACT GEORGIA POWER’S LOCAL, REGIONAL, AND INTERREGIONAL TRANSMISSION PLANNING PROCESSES.
A.FERC Order 1920 directly includes and requires many of the planning and process improvements that parties recommend in this proceeding. At a high level, together, FERC Orders 1920, 1920-A, and 1920-B mandate a Long-Term Regional Transmission Planning (“LTRTP”) process using a 20-year planning horizon with multiple transmission needs scenarios. FERC Order 1920 also includes specific requirements addressing regional and local transmission planning efforts and consideration of Grid-Enhancing Technologies (“GETs”). 
Q.DOES FERC ORDER 1920 ADDRESS STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT AND PROCESSES?
A.Yes. FERC Order 1920 requires three publicly noticed stakeholder meetings per regional transmission planning cycle to address local transmission planning assumptions, transmission needs, and solutions. 
Q.PLEASE DISCUSS THE TIMELINE FOR DEVELOPING AND IMPLEMENTING A PLAN FOR COMPLYING WITH FERC ORDER 1920. 
A.Georgia Power is currently developing a comprehensive compliance and execution plan for addressing the reforms and improvements required by FERC Order 1920. 
SERTP Sponsors, together with SCRTP sponsors, are also developing their compliance plan consistent with the directives in FERC Order 1920, 1920-A, and 1920-B. This compliance plan will address each requirement outlined in FERC Order 1920 and subsequent Orders 1920-A and 1920-B. Regional requirements compliance plans are due by June 12, 2026, and interregional compliance plans are due by August 12, 2026. Thereafter, Georgia Power will begin compliance with its regional and interregional plans by June 12 and August 12, 2028, respectively.
Q.GIVEN THE EXTENSIVE COLLABORATION, LONG-TERM TRANSMISSION PLANNING EFFORTS, AND PLANNED ENHANCEMENTS ASSOCIATED WITH FERC ORDER 1920 COMPLIANCE, IS ADDITIONAL STAKEHOLDER INVOLVEMENT NECESSARY? 
A.No. As described above, the Company is actively involved in robust and collaborative planning processes that facilitate stakeholder engagement and feedback. Informal and organic engagement opportunities on transmission planning-related topics will likely arise in the future. 
Q.PLEASE RESPOND TO STAFF WITNESS GOGGIN’S RECOMMENDATION THAT GEORGIA POWER SHOULD PLAN FOR HIGHER VOLTAGE AND HIGHER CAPACITY TRANSMISSION SOLUTIONS.
A.The Company disagrees that higher voltage, double-circuit transmission “is significantly more cost effective” than other transmission planning approaches. Simply proposing to plan for more expensive and potentially unnecessary solutions contradicts how transmission planning is coordinated with generation resource planning in Georgia. In deregulated markets, transmission owners build large transmission lines across states hoping that they have sited the infrastructure where independent power producers will seek to interconnect. In Georgia, however, without a clearly justified need to increase transmission capacity further, this approach would likely result in expensive infrastructure investments with no guarantee to alleviate known or anticipated transmission constraints. 
Georgia Power is already implementing the type of forward-looking, high-capacity transmission planning that Staff recommends. Georgia Power builds transmission as needed to deliver power from facilities that have been sited, financed, and have initiated the process of interconnecting to the System. Georgia Power’s integrated approach ensures interconnections actually occur and minimizes transmission investments based upon speculation. As mentioned above and in Technical Appendix Volume 3, Georgia Power jointly plans and operates the networked transmission system with the ITS Participants who are jointly focused on developing transmission plans that prioritize both reliability and affordability in their planning processes. 
Georgia Power’s transmission planning process already incorporates higher voltage and higher capacity solutions where they are most effective and economical. Through the ITS, the Company evaluates 500kV, 230kV, and 115kV options in a tiered, system-wide approach that prioritizes robust, long-term solutions. For example, projects like the Butler–Thomaston 230kV conversion and the Farley–Tazewell 500kV expansion represent examples of high-capacity upgrades that relieve thermal constraints and enhance transfer capability across regions. Importantly, these projects are selected not in isolation, but as part of a coordinated statewide plan that aligns with NERC reliability standards and supports Georgia’s evolving generation mix and load growth. 
Q.STAFF WITNESS GOGGIN ALSO RECOMMENDS THAT GEORGIA POWER EXPAND TIES TO NEIGHBORING UTILITIES TO IMPROVE RELIABILITY. PLEASE RESPOND.
A.The Company examines this option in the transmission planning process and has expanded ties to neighboring utilities and systems when appropriate. For example, in developing the Ten-Year Plan, Georgia Power coordinates with utilities through the SERTP process. In addition, Georgia Power works directly with interconnected utilities to perform joint evaluations, which could result in identifying transmission facilities that could better meet the needs identified in respective plans. The Company plans for firm and future firm obligations, including transmission reliability margin and capacity benefit margin. Furthermore, as described above, Georgia Power will continue considering and evaluating regional and transmission planning including the costs and benefits associated with potential solutions and consistent with the existing FERC regional planning requirements and processes and directives outlined in FERC Order 1920. 
Witness Goggin did not evaluate or consider the costs or benefits associated with expanding ties to neighboring utilities for reliability purposes. In fact, Witness Goggin provided no supporting analysis or evaluation supporting this recommendation as he recommends that Georgia Power perform the required diligence, which the Company already considers through existing planning processes. 
In addition to potential cost barriers, relying on expanded ties with neighboring systems presents several potential risks. For example, simply building interfaces between balancing authorities does not guarantee that the neighboring utility can or will deliver power to the corresponding ties in times of need. If a neighboring system not controlled by Georgia Power fails to deliver power during capacity constrained periods, Georgia Power would still be held responsible. Other potential risks include delayed resource availability, curtailment due to transmission constraints and/or capacity emergencies in neighboring areas, and transmission system stability issues.
Georgia Power is obligated to ensure that energy and generation capacity requirements are met in a reliable, cost-effective manner. Decisions on the value of specific transmission improvements are complex and must consider all relevant factors. Simply put, expanded ties cannot alone replace the value of sufficient generation capacity and energy located in Georgia.

Georgia Power employs a reasonable approach that appropriately focuses on targeted generation pockets to ensure generation capacity is not constrained by transmission in a given area. Importantly, the Company maximizes the value to customers by mitigating physical delivery risks, ensuring the ability to economically dispatch and reducing curtailment risks for firm and future firm generation resources, consistent with the Company’s transmission planning principles.

Q.DOES GEORGIA POWER APPROPRIATELY CONSIDER ADVANCED CONDUCTORS IN ITS TRANSMISSION PLANNING AND DESIGN PROCESS INCLUDING 500KV CONSIDERATIONS?

Georgia Power also utilizes other innovative solutions in its transmission planning and operations processes as described in Section 11 of the Main IRP document and as demonstrated in Technical Appendix Volume 3.

Georgia Power does not currently use advanced conductors as 500kV solutions, however, as GIPL and Southface Witness Patel recommends, because using advanced conductors at this voltage level is not in the best interest of the reliability of the grid nor is it cost-effective for customers. Increasing the total energy capacity of 500kV transmission lines can create new contingency events that are significantly larger than those currently considered in transmission planning studies. If transmission-connected assets are added to the system that increase reliability exposure due to a new largest contingency, that can create further needs to build more transmission infrastructure to ensure the grid can reliably withstand larger contingency events consistent with NERC transmission planning standards. Additionally, creating higher current-carrying capacity on existing 500kV lines through the deployment of advanced conductors instead of increasing the operational voltage could lead to the inability to find terminal equipment with sufficiently high operating characteristics to safely and reliably carry the increased current, thereby limiting the ability to fully achieve the desired increased capacity of the transmission line. 
Georgia Power continues to evaluate the use of innovative technologies such as advanced conductors for transmission projects and includes considerations such as those described to select the best solution for each situation. Further, the Company will continue examining all solutions to solve or improve reliability needs for the transmission grid and that are safe and cost-effective. 
Q.WHY DID GEORGIA POWER NOT EVALUATE BATTERY STORAGE AS A TRANSMISSION SOLUTION FOR CONSTRAINTS IDENTIFIED IN THE 2025 IRP?
A.Battery storage as a transmission alternative is often impractical and uneconomic for several reasons. First, batteries require grid power for charging, which causes it to act like additional load thereby potentially exacerbating the very constraints that the additional resources and transmission solutions are intended to resolve. Second, batteries serving as transmission solutions cannot simultaneously count as both generation capacity and a transmission solution within the Southern Company System. There are numerous contingency events that could create a need for batteries to be deployed as either a generation or transmission solution, and if a battery is not dedicated to be available for its designated generation or transmission use case(s), there is no way to guarantee it will be available to respond as needed in an emergency event. For example, a battery would need to be available for every possible contingency combination that can cause the identified transmission line to overload. Third, a 4-hour battery is not a practical solution. For example, a tornado could create an unexpected outage during hot weather conditions that lasts for a week. For batteries to resolve the resulting transmission constraints, the grid would need enough available capacity to charge the battery system(s), they would need to be of significant size and discharge duration, and there may need to be multiple battery systems to cover the multiple contingencies across different locations identified in a transmission planning analysis. As a result, in this scenario, the overall cost and land requirements to support battery projects that can cover extended duration outages would be cost prohibitive. Finally, Georgia Power would need to run a total net present value of the battery systems versus wire solutions to contemplate the total life-cycle cost of the two alternatives.
If future BESS applications, configurations, and operations demonstrate that BESS can be deployed reliably and economically as transmission solutions, Georgia Power will continue to consider this technology as a solution. In parallel, the Company is continuing to gain operational experience through the successful deployment of the Mossy Branch Battery Facility, the first transmission-connected BESS on the System, and ongoing 500 MW BESS deployments approved by the Commission in the 2023 IRP Update and any others that may come from ongoing or upcoming RFP solicitations.

As we previously testified at the Company’s direct hearings in this docket, we have and will continue to inquire directly with large loads concerning their ability and desire to participate in such programs and their ability to designate all or part of their load as non-firm and curtailable.

7.0FORECAST ADJUSTMENTS FOR LARGE CUSTOMERS
The February 2025 Load Forecast continues to utilize the Load Realization Model developed for the 2023 IRP Update. This model uses a probabilistic approach to evaluate the range and likelihood of future potential outcomes of the load growth from new large-load customers. The results of this approach support the external adjustment applied to the commercial and industrial load and energy forecasts. This Section describes the methodology and assumptions behind the probabilistic approach that is used. 
As in the 2023 IRP Update and the Budget 2025, the February 2025 Load Forecast includes an external adjustment to its baseline, organic forecast to account for the increased large load additions described herein. An external adjustment is needed for these loads since Georgia’s unprecedented economic development growth would not otherwise be captured in the historical trends underlying the baseline forecast. Georgia Power has made external adjustments for large new customers in the past. For example, the Company adjusted its forecast in the 2016 and 2019 IRPs to account for the addition of the Elba Island Liquified Natural Gas facility, which represented a completely new industry to the state. 
Notwithstanding prior large load external adjustments to the forecast, prior to 2023 IRP Update, the Company had never seen such a significant number of new large customer projects materialize in such a short period of time. As a result, Georgia Power developed a way to estimate the impacts of these projects on its system while also addressing the inherent uncertainties around whether such projects will ultimately locate in Georgia, select Georgia Power as the electric service provider, and come online with the anticipated load. The probabilistic model Georgia Power developed to address these considerations, the Load Realization Model, is discussed in the section below.

7.1MODEL OVERVIEW
7.1.1Known Project Inputs
For each large load project the Company has evaluated and included in the February 2025 Load Forecast, the following information is provided:
Commercial Operation Date (“COD”): When the initial load is expected to start.
Ramp-Up: A year-by-year load trajectory.
Announced Load: The design capacity for the project load. This corresponds to the maximum load of the ramp-up schedule provided by each customer. The Company’s Power Delivery organization uses this information to properly size the facilities and purchase equipment to serve the new load. 
Class: Industrial or commercial.
Segment: This criterion corresponds to the particular segment of a business within a customer class, such as cryptocurrency, datacenter, warehouse, battery manufacturing, chemicals, and solar.

7.1.2Modeling Project Uncertainty
Each large load project contains multiple dimensions of uncertainty that must be considered and analyzed. The first element of uncertainty to consider is whether the potential customers will choose to locate in Georgia. A customer may ultimately end up choosing a different state as the location of a project despite initial indications of interest in Georgia. If a large load customer elects to locate in Georgia, there is still uncertainty due to the competitive nature of the bidding process for large load customers among electric service providers in the state, and a customer may choose to sign a contract for electric service (“CES”) with an electric service provider other than Georgia Power. There is even some uncertainty once a customer signs a CES with Georgia Power, as the project could fail to materialize due to unforeseen circumstances such as financial or other economic conditions. In addition, based on Georgia Power’s experience, the load announced by a customer is only an estimate of its metered load, which can materialize at a lower level than that at the time of the customer’s initial announcement. Finally, the commercial operation date of a project can be delayed, thus causing load to materialize later than initially expected. 7.1.3Project Success
The Company uses a probabilistic approach to account for the uncertainties described in the previous section to determine the likelihood of success or failure of an individual project. The success of a project requires each of the following three events:

The project reaches commercial operation after a contract has been signed with Georgia Power.

7.1.4State Selection

Likelihood can be estimated based on a historical selection rate that is calculated based on the number of projects that chose Georgia versus the number of projects that did not.

7.2MODELING THE PORTFOLIO
The portfolio of large load projects being assessed by the Company consists of projects in numerous phases of development, ranging from early stages, such as the state selection or site selection phases, to those under construction and projected to be served by the Company. This creates multiple dimensions of uncertainty for each project that requires a Monte Carlo simulation model to quantify a range of expected load to serve.
Monte Carlo simulation is a mathematical technique that is used to estimate the possible outcomes of a portfolio with uncertain events. Unlike a normal forecasting model, Monte Carlo simulation predicts a set of outcomes based on an estimated range of values versus a set of fixed input values. The simulation assigns random values to input variables with uncertainty based on probability distributions such as the triangular distributions mentioned in Section 7.1.7 and calculates an outcome. It then repeats the process over and over (at least thousands of times typically), each time using a different set of random inputs, to produce a large number of outcomes.

7.2.1 Model Implementation
The model described herein is implemented in Excel with a third-party add-in called @Risk. @Risk allows the incorporation of probability distributions into an Excel spreadsheet so that Monte Carlo simulation and analysis can be done to track the range of potential outcomes and calculate a wide array of statistics (average, standard deviation, percentiles, etc.).
For each project in the model, there are three random numbers being drawn for success/failure, metered/announced ratio, and delay in COD from the distributions described in Section 7.1. For a particular draw, if the success/failure random number is 0, or failure, the load of the project is 0 and the project is excluded from the portfolio. If the success/failure random number is 1, or success, the announced load ramp-up gets scaled by the metered/announced ratio random number and then shifted according to the timing of the ramp-up by the number of months determined by the delay in COD random number. Finally, this adjusted and shifted ramp-up is aggregated year by year into the portfolio level.

7.2.2 Simulation Results 
The procedure described in Section 7.2.1 is repeated 100,000 times. Then, these 100,000 load iterations get ranked for each year in order to calculate load percentiles, which helps the Company to understand the load range and compare the likelihood of load outcomes for the portfolio. Triangular distributions are chosen for this model for the following reasons: 
A triangular distribution has a finite range while other commonly used probability distributions such as normal and lognormal distributions have an infinite range and can even be negative, which is inappropriate to depict the actual/announced ratio.
The min/max/most likely parametric setup of triangular distributions is intuitive and straightforward to interpret. In contrast, the parameters of some other commonly used distributions are somewhat opaque. For example, normal distributions are defined in terms of mean and standard deviation. Additional calculations are needed to translate those into the range of outcomes that are more appropriate for the context of actual vs. announced load.
Other than the standard min/max/most likely parametric setup, triangular distributions can also be defined by a combination of percentiles, min, max and most likely, which offers more flexibility to fine tune the model.

13   Q.   WHAT CONCERNS ARE THERE IF THE TARGET RESERVE MARGIN
14        IS SET TOO LOW?

15   A.   The Company uses the reserve margin for reliability planning purposes. It is
16        designed to minimize the combined expected costs of maintaining planning reserve
17        capacity, production costs, and customer costs associated with service
18        interruptions. Importantly, a reserve margin also balances these costs against risk
19        of outages and minimum levels of reliability.

20        While a higher reserve margin may increase costs to customers from having more
21        generation resources available to serve load, a reserve margin that is too low creates
22        far greater risks in terms of reliability. During extreme weather events, like those
23        experienced in recent years, an insufficient reserve margin could leave the
24        Company without sufficient power to serve load. If the Company does not have
25        sufficient resources to serve load, the Company would be required to shed firm load
26        and curtail service to customers to prevent larger-scale blackouts. Put simply, a

 5        brownouts—intentional load shedding—in New Orleans over Memorial Day

7.0     FORECAST ADJUSTMENTS FOR LARGE CUSTOMERS

The February 2025 Load Forecast continues to utilize the Load Realization Model developed for
the 2023 IRP Update. This model uses a probabilistic approach to evaluate the range and likelihood
of future potential outcomes of the load growth from new large-load customers. The results of this
approach support the external adjustment applied to the commercial and industrial load and energy
forecasts. This Section describes the methodology and assumptions behind the probabilistic
approach that is used.

As in the 2023 IRP Update and the Budget 2025, the February 2025 Load Forecast includes an
external adjustment to its baseline, organic forecast to account for the increased large load
additions described herein. An external adjustment is needed for these loads since Georgia’s
unprecedented economic development growth would not otherwise be captured in the historical
trends underlying the baseline forecast. Georgia Power has made external adjustments for large
new customers in the past. For example, the Company adjusted its forecast in the 2016 and 2019
IRPs to account for the addition of the Elba Island Liquified Natural Gas facility, which
represented a completely new industry to the state.

Notwithstanding prior large load external adjustments to the forecast, prior to 2023 IRP Update,
the Company had never seen such a significant number of new large customer projects materialize
in such a short period of time. As a result, Georgia Power developed a way to estimate the impacts
of these projects on its system while also addressing the inherent uncertainties around whether

7.1.1     Known Project Inputs

For each large load project the Company has evaluated and included in the February 2025 Load
Forecast, the following information is provided:

         Commercial Operation Date (“COD”): When the initial load is expected to start.
         Ramp-Up: A year-by-year load trajectory.
         Announced Load: The design capacity for the project load. This corresponds to the
          maximum load of the ramp-up schedule provided by each customer. The Company’s Power
          Delivery organization uses this information to properly size the facilities and purchase
          equipment to serve the new load.
         Class: Industrial or commercial.
         Segment: This criterion corresponds to the particular segment of a business within a
          customer class, such as cryptocurrency, datacenter, warehouse, battery manufacturing,
          chemicals, and solar.

7.1.4      State Selection

Customers often evaluate sites in multiple states before finalizing the location of a project.
Mathematically, state selection can be treated as a binary event, with one (1) assigned for
customers that select Georgia and zero (0) for customers that select a different state. For those
customers contemplating Georgia as a place for doing business, a probability (referred to as P1)
can be assigned for the likelihood of state selection. Likelihood can be estimated based on a
historical selection rate that is calculated based on the number of projects that chose Georgia versus
the number of projects that did not.

7.1.6   Project Reaching Commercial Operation

The probability of projects reaching commercial operation (referred to as P3) is determined by
reviewing the number of projects that reached commercial operation versus the total number of
projects Georgia Power was selected to serve.

For a new large load project to be considered successful, all three of the above events (i.e., state
selection, signing a CES with Georgia Power, and the project reaching commercial operation) must
occur. In other words, project success or failure is a binary event that is reflected by the probability
formula P = P1*P2*P3.

7.2      MODELING THE PORTFOLIO

The portfolio of large load projects being assessed by the Company consists of projects in
numerous phases of development, ranging from early stages, such as the state selection or site
selection phases, to those under construction and projected to be served by the Company. This
creates multiple dimensions of uncertainty for each project that requires a Monte Carlo simulation
model to quantify a range of expected load to serve.

Monte Carlo simulation is a mathematical technique that is used to estimate the possible outcomes
of a portfolio with uncertain events. Unlike a normal forecasting model, Monte Carlo simulation
predicts a set of outcomes based on an estimated range of values versus a set of fixed input values.
The simulation assigns random values to input variables with uncertainty based on probability
distributions such as the triangular distributions mentioned in Section 7.1.7 and calculates an
outcome. It then repeats the process over and over (at least thousands of times typically), each time
using a different set of random inputs, to produce a large number of outcomes.


7.2.1    Model Implementation

The model described herein is implemented in Excel with a third-party add-in called @Risk.
@Risk allows the incorporation of probability distributions into an Excel spreadsheet so that
Monte Carlo simulation and analysis can be done to track the range of potential outcomes and
calculate a wide array of statistics (average, standard deviation, percentiles, etc.).

For each project in the model, there are three random numbers being drawn for success/failure,
metered/announced ratio, and delay in COD from the distributions described in Section 7.1. For a
particular draw, if the success/failure random number is 0, or failure, the load of the project is 0
and the project is excluded from the portfolio. If the success/failure random number is 1, or
success, the announced load ramp-up gets scaled by the metered/announced ratio random number
and then shifted according to the timing of the ramp-up by the number of months determined by
the delay in COD random number. Finally, this adjusted and shifted ramp-up is aggregated year
by year into the portfolio level.

7.3    TRIANGULAR DISTRIBUTION

The triangular distribution is a continuous probability distribution with the following parameters:

       a: a ∈ (-∞, ∞)
       b: a < b
       c: a ≤ c ≤ b


In the case of metered versus announced uncertainty, a represents the minimum
metered/announced ratio, b represents maximum metered/announced ratio and c represents the
most likely metered/announced ratio.

Triangular distributions are chosen for this model for the following reasons:

      A triangular distribution has a finite range while other commonly used probability
       distributions such as normal and lognormal distributions have an infinite range and can
       even be negative, which is inappropriate to depict the actual/announced ratio.
      The min/max/most likely parametric setup of triangular distributions is intuitive and
       straightforward to interpret. In contrast, the parameters of some other commonly used
       distributions are somewhat opaque. For example, normal distributions are defined in terms
       of mean and standard deviation. Additional calculations are needed to translate those into
       the range of outcomes that are more appropriate for the context of actual vs. announced
       load.
      Other than the standard min/max/most likely parametric setup, triangular distributions
       can also be defined by a combination of percentiles, min, max and most likely, which
       offers more flexibility to fine tune the model.

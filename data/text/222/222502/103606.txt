Q13.PLEASE PROVIDE A SUMMARY OF YOUR TESTIMONY

Summary of Testimony.
The Advanced Power Alliance (“APA”) appreciates the opportunity to provide this testimony on the 2025 Integrated Resource Plan of Georgia Power Company (“GPC” or “the Company”).  APA is an organization that supports and advocates for clean energy in legislative and regulatory forums in the southeastern United States, as well as those located in the Southwest Power Pool and the Electric Reliability Council of Texas.  APA represents most companies that develop utility-scale advanced energy technologies that deliver clean, reliable and affordable power for electricity consumers.
APA is encouraged by GPC’s continued commitment to diversify its generation resources by procuring more low-cost utility-scale renewable energy and storage resources.  However, to reach its targets, APA encourages reforms to the structure of the Company’s request for proposals (“RFP”) processes and its power purchase agreement (“PPA”) terms to best set up the Company and the market for success. 
Accordingly, APA recommends that the Commission direct the Company to convene a workshop process to dialogue with renewable energy developers to improve these elements.  Specifically, such a workshop process should address issues including: RFP timelines, addressing tariff risk, addressing potential changes to the federal Inflation Reduction Act, transparency of system integration costs, and financial security requirements.
Solar RFP Reforms
Q14 PLEASE DESCRIBE HOW THE COMPANY AND THE COMMISSION COULD IMPROVE THE RFP PROCESS.
A14.APA fully supports the Company’s goals to expand its utility-scale renewables and storage procurement in the current IRP.  However, the Company’s RFP process is deficient and slow, frustrating the Commission and the Company’s ability to achieve procurement goals. 
To enact meaningful changes, APA encourages the Commission to direct the Company to initiate a stakeholder process with utility-scale developers to improve its RFP process so that developers can efficiently submit cost-effective proposals that have a high likelihood of PPA execution so that Georgia ratepayers benefit from low-cost renewable generation and storage assets.   
Georgia is now seventh in the nation when it comes to installed solar and is on its way to entering the top 5 of US states, and much of the credit goes to this Commission’s leadership and GPC’s commitment. As modeling in this IRP shows, solar has been and continues to be a major component of an all-of-the-above generation plan that provides reliable power at the lowest cost possible. However, the Company has faced major challenges, headwinds, and uncertainty that have made execution difficult, resulting in a series of RFPs that did not yield the desired volume of low-cost solar. As GPC’s witnesses have explained, “changing interconnection processes and requirements, impacts from regulatory uncertainty and policy changes (tariffs, tax credits, grants, etc.), increasing scrutiny on land use, supply chain issues, and a difference in the timelines of proposed renewable procurements and transmission system improvements have all limited the Company’s ability to meet its renewable RFP targets.” Beppler et al. page 26. 
Further, the Company has recognized: “feedback from market participants” indicates “a need for additional flexibility to navigate changing regulatory policies and supply chain dynamics, including modifications to RCODs. These market conditions, combined with customer desires for subscriptions to incremental renewable resources, drive the need for more flexibility in the Company’s RFP process, including consideration of customer identified resources.”  Id.  The Company has proposed several changes to the RFP process aimed at improving the efficiency and success of its renewable procurements.” Id at 27.
APA appreciates the Company’s recognition that changes could improve the effectiveness of its RFP process, and shares the goal of making GPC’s procurement program more efficient and successful. To that end, we propose several changes below, which we believe will improve transparency and shorten timelines, ultimately reducing risk for the Company and, in turn, ratepayers. Bidders seek greater transparency in the RFP process, as more precise information allows bidders to be more disciplined in their proposals.  Shorter timelines between pricing submission and final award selection will increase the success rate of the Company to execute PPAs with RFP finalists.  Taken together, these changes should enable market participants to develop and deliver projects more cost-effectively, which will in turn benefit GPC’s ratepayers and the state of Georgia. 
To understand our proposed solutions, it might first be useful to understand the current challenges faced by bidders under the existing process. A market participant first makes a bid into the RFP, providing a price premised on calculations of how much it will cost to construct and operate a facility within an assumed timeline. Significant capital to secure land and complete the engineering and design work to make a bid. Additional capital is required for interconnection studies and for shortlist security. Then still more capital is required at PPA execution to cover deliverability upgrades that are unknown until the very end of the bid evaluation process – which could be over a year after the developer first submitted its project bid. 
The end result is that a project developer must invest significant capital and incur significant carrying costs for a long period of time even before a project begins construction – and must also be expected to maintain its initial price even though significant economic changes may have occurred over the 20 months between initial bid and final PPA execution. To make matters more challenging, a bidder will not know the cost of system upgrades for its project until very late in the process, meaning that a developer could invest significant sums in a project that while cost-effective from a construction standpoint will nonetheless turn out to be non-competitive for reasons beyond the developer’s control -- or knowledge. 
With these challenges in mind, APA suggests a number of improvements to improve a lengthy, costly, and risky process to make it quicker, clearer, and cheaper – which will in turn benefit ratepayers through the more orderly delivery of cost-effective projects.  APA encourages the Commission to direct the Company to engage in a stakeholder process with renewable energy developers to improve and streamline the RFP process to set both the Company and the market for success so that ratepayers benefit from low-cost renewable power.  
Q15.HOW COULD THE COMPANY EXPEDITE ITS RFP PROCESS?
A15.The 2023 RFP process has been ongoing for 20 months (June 2023 to present), with bids outstanding for over a year. GPC invited one price refresh, but the Company limited allowable bid updates (i.e., project price and COD); the five months then elapsed before the Company made award decisions. It is commercially unreasonable to require bidders to hold price and COD (among other things included in their original bid from February) despite significant market movements during that period of time (including tariffs) makes the process extremely challenging and leads to project attrition. Unfortunately, the Company has not resolved these timeframe issues in the current 2025 CARES RFP process – draft RFP materials indicate an 11-month window between bid submission and PPA execution, followed by another three months before a final Commission decision.  Even this timeline is crippling for project development.  Past is prologue.  Despite the Company’s best intentions,  APA members have significant concern that this 14-month timeline will drag on much longer.  
At a minimum, APA suggests that the PSC should direct GPC adhere to RFP timeline milestones and to make awards no more than six months from final pricing submittal.
Q16.HOW COULD THE COMPANY CREATE MORE TRANSPARENCY IN THE RFP PROCESS SO THAT BIDDERS CAN MAKE BETTER ECONOMIC DECISIONS ON BIDDING?
A16.Currently GPC’s evaluation of projects includes the imputed cost of Southern Company Transmission System Interconnection Costs, a number that is not shared with bidders. According to 2025 CARES documentation, this cost ranged from $0-$40 million across awarded bidders alone in the 2023 CARES RFP. All parties could benefit if these costs were known sooner rather than later, since that information could enable developers to withdraw projects with high system costs sooner and thus reduce the resources expended in developing and evaluating those projects. Additionally, it is unclear to bidders on what additional system benefits the Company imputes to individual projects during its evaluation.
APA encourages the Commission to direct the proposed stakeholder process to address this issue.  Specifically, GPC should consider providing preliminary system impact costs and value earlier, and more thoroughly, to bidders.  GPC should also consider pathways for bidders to such information to improve the RFP process.  
Providing this type of improved data could mean that projects with significant deficiencies in GPC system value are not dragged along the full bidding process and bidders could focus their efforts on bid iterations that are the most cost-competitive and provide the greatest value to the Company and price those projects competitively.
Q17.WHAT IS APA’S RECOMMENDATION ON HOW TO HANDLE CHANGES IN LAW, TARIFFS, ETC.?
A17.Under the current system, bids are submitted 20 months before a project executes a PPA, and years before it would be constructed and start generating electricity (and project revenue to start paying down the project investment). In the intervening time, however, changes to laws could make the actual cost to construct the project much higher than initially expected. Tariffs that increase the costs of raw materials such as steel, copper, and glass and finished products like solar modules are one example. Another change would be changes to existing federal tax law, which could remove or significantly reduce the value of tax credits or limit their transferability, which would in turn increase a project’s costs.
APA supports reasonable change in law provisions that allow a bidder and the Company to adjust to significant changes in law.  Given the complexities of the changing federal policy landscape, this further underscores the importance of APA’s proposal: a dialogue between the Company and renewable developers to improve the RFP process and resulting PPA.  
Q18.WHAT DOES APA RECOMMEND ON FINANCIAL SECURITY REQUIREMENTS?
A18.PPAs require developers to post significant sums and put those at risk. While APA understands the need to provide effective collateral to ensure that the utility and its customers are held harmless when projects do not proceed, the requirement to post letters of credit (“LOC”) is unnecessarily driving up project costs.
A LOC is a guarantee to pay cash on demand, issued by a financial institution. To issue a LOC, a financial institution will require the posting of significant collateral – basically, cash – from a project developer, since the LOC is redeemable on demand at any time.
In the case of a $25 million Upgrade Security posting, a developer would need to come up with millions to post the LOC years before the project could come on-line and generate revenue. The cost to post that collateral will increase the project cost and thus the bid cost, raising costs for consumers.
Other forms of security cost less. Example: “on-demand” surety bonds, which are essentially payable on demand with no defenses. APA recommends inclusion of surety bonds as an option for the posting of Performance Security and Upgrade Security under the PPA.
APA appreciates the changes GPC proposed to the timing of Upgrade Security postings in its draft 2025 CARES RFP documents filed on April 17, 2025, which break required postings into a payment schedule. We believe a payment schedule cadence is more appropriate and reasonable since the Upgrade Security amount is not known until later in the process (and is therefore harder to plan for) and [GPC would not be reasonably expected to spend 100% of the Upgrade Security immediately following PPA execution].
In addition to significant bid fees, GPC requires a substantial Bid Security that is beyond (i.e., eight times) industry standard, and can be withdrawn should a project not sign a PPA even after 1 year of GPC’s evaluation. The Bid Security size combined with the timespan between bid and PPA presents significant risk for developers and drives up costs. APA submits that a lower bid security more in line with industry standards would reduce costs to ratepayers.
CIR Program Improvements
Q19.PLEASE EXPLAIN APA’S RECOMMENDATIONS FOR GEORGIA POWER’S CUSTOMER IDENTIFED RESOURCES (“CIR”) PROGRAM
A.19.  GPC proposes changes to the current CARES RFP structure by introducing new phases of project procurement: If “customer subscription needs remain after the traditional portion of the Phase I RFP, the Company would offer projects that did not make the Short List a chance to buy-down their project price such that their net benefits are equal to the average total net benefit of the Short Listed portfolio. Further, the Company would add a second phase, which would allow customers to work with developers to “self-identify resources and submit additional proposals to meet additional customer subscription demand not otherwise satisfied by Phase I of the proposed RFP process” – so-called “Customer Identified Resources” or “CIRs.” This would allow project developers and potential subscribers to submit proposals to meet a price target that ensures new projects will deliver Net Benefit greater or equal to the original portfolio’s Total Net Benefits. Phase II would only be made available if there are unmet CARES Utility Scale customer subscription needs.
APA supports the overall program concept, but has suggestions on implementation to ensure the program delivers benefits
A. Accelerate CIR Timing.
Under Georgia Power’s proposal, customers would not be able to bring forward a Customer Identified Resource until a second phase of the 2026 Utility Scale RFP for renewable resources, which would not open until the first phase is complete.[fn Beppler, Goff, Mallard, Phillips Direct Panel at 34:21-26.] Georgia Power Witness Mr. Mallard stated that the Phase II Customer Identified Resource process would likely not begin until 2027 or later, meaning projects would not be completed until well after 2027 [fn: Georgia Power Direct Hearing Day 2, March 26, 2025, 7:10:23-45.]
On the demand side, however, from 2020 to 2024, Georgia Power received more than 9,000 MW of customer interest to participate in its the CRSP and CARES programs,” but only 777.5 MW of renewable resources have been brought online to fill REDI and CRSP subscriptions, [fn IRP Main Document at page 95] suggesting that only a portion of customer demand has been met so far.
Rather than delaying the CIR implementation, APA supports authorizing Customer Identified Resources immediately, which would enable quick deployment of resources that would help GPC meet its pressing system needs.
B. Other CIR Improvements
The CIR program should allow a group of customers to bring forward or select a single Customer Identified Resource to which they all subscribe. This would enable smaller load C&I customers to pool their demand and select larger clean energy projects with greater economies of scale and cost effectiveness than smaller individual projects. At the hearing, Georgia Power Witness Mr. Mallard indicated that Georgia Power is open to this modification. [fn Georgia Power Direct Hearing Day 2, March 26, 2025, 7:13:41-45.], and APA appreciates the Company’s willingness to consider that option.
The CIR program should have no capacity cap, or, alternatively, a much higher cap than proposed. The Company proposed a 3 GW cap for CARES Phase II. However, judging by the figures cited above concerning consumer demand for clean energy (e.g., 9 GW) and the relatively small volumes procured to date, there may be significantly higher demand for these resources than 3GW. Given the ability of CIR to enable economic development with no cost to existing ratepayers, and the potential for such projects to move quickly, there appears to be no reason to cap the CIR program in the face of quickly escalating load growth and challenges meeting that load growth through existing procurement channels. 
 Further, APA has some concerns about the Company’s proposal for bidders to “buy down” an initial bid in order to execute in the CIR program.  Given the high rates of project attrition to date, APA is concerned that using a portfolio average system benefit price could artificially set CIR prices too low for sellers to build and finance projects. For example, if a CARES RFP procures only 20% of the intended volume, the average system benefit price for that portfolio may be far lower than the actual average price required to develop a solar facility in the Company’s territory. Using that price to set the CIR program credit price could result in that credit being so low could that CIR projects become economically infeasible and impossible to develop – resulting in still more volume shortfalls and growing unmet customer demand backlog.
Again, APA supports the CIR program and wants to see it accelerated to meet demonstrated customer demand. APA would appreciate the opportunity to engage with the Company to further finalize details and CIR program design to allow for rapid execution while holding non-participating ratepayers harmless.
Improvements to GPC BESS RFP Process and Contract Terms
Q20.DOES THE PANEL HAVE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENTS TO THE BATTERY ENERGY STORAGE SYSTEM (“BESS”) PROCUREMENT PROCESS AND PPA TERMS?
A20.Yes, we do.  The BESS form Energy Services Agreement (“ESA”) contains several terms which present challenges for obtaining project financing.  There are four key areas, that, if addressed, would allow developers to offer greater value:  1) Performance guarantees should be adjusted to align with project finance realities; 2) Development and operating security requirements should be consistent with market standards and should not disadvantage specific technologies; 3) Optimal Performance Guarantee Measurement; and 4) similar to the above discussion about change of law provisions, allowing for mitigation of associated risks.
Q21 Please explain how performance guarantees can be aligned with project finance realities.  
A21.The ESA should have performance guarantees (along with testing protocols) that largely back-to-back, or match, the guarantees that are provided by Original Equipment Manufacturers (“OEM”s) in their long-term services agreement (“LTSA”), which mitigate any “gap” risk for developers.  Project finance lenders are reticent to finance projects where there could be substantial damages payable under the ESA which are not recoverable through the LTSA or other OEM guarantees. While availability, capacity and Round Trip Efficiency “RTE”  guarantees are commonplace, GPC’s implementation through the Storage Performance Metric intertwines the metrics in a manner that is inconsistent with back-to-back guarantees from OEMs.

Question:

The Intercompany Interchange Contract (“IIC”) provides for coordinated planning between the Operating Companies and for the sharing of temporary surpluses and deficits of capacity. If one or more retail operating companies has a capacity need, but the System has surplus, the System surplus can be used to reduce or even eliminate the need for the retail operating company with such need. While the System Pool affords the participants the ability to rely on temporary surplus capacity on the System, each Operating Company is expected to have adequate resources, including an appropriate level of reserves, to reliably serve its own load obligations.
See STF-JKA-3-10 Attachment A for the requested adjusted Load and Resource table that assumes all excess System capacity resources in each respective year are available to help meet the Company’s capacity need. 
The years of capacity need for the System are projected to be Winter 2027/2028 and Summer 2029. The Company could rely on the System surplus during the 2027 and 2028 summers, delaying the Company summer need to 2029. The Company and the System have the same first winter need year, which is Winter 2027/2028. However, the System need is lower than the Company’s need, which means one or more retail operating companies have some surplus capacity that the Company could temporarily rely on and potentially add less capacity than shown for the Company in that winter season. 
See STF-JKA-3-10 Attachment B TRADE SECRET for the requested diversity percentage calculations that were submitted with the 2023 IRP Update.  See STF-JKA-1-1 Attachment AU TRADE SECRET for the requested diversity percentage calculations used in the 2024 Resource Mix Study for the 2025 IRP.

Refer to tab: “Solar Track” cell: A18. Explain what assumptions were used in Aurora for modeling PTC (45Y) Tax Credits? Are the tax credits captured in the SAM models? 
Refer to tab: “Wind” cell: A18.  Explain what assumptions were used in Aurora for modeling PTC (45Y) Credits?  Are the tax credits captured in the SAM models? Explain if/how the Company decided to model ITC or PTC.
Explain the process the Company used to determine whether to model PTC or ITC for new solar and wind resources. Provide all analysis performed to make such a decision.
Refer to tab: “Nuclear AP1000” cell: A18. Explain what assumptions were used in Aurora for modeling ITC (48E) Tax Credits?  Are the tax credits captured in the SAM models? Explain if/how the Company decided to model ITC or PTC. 

Response:

See Section 2.2.2 and 3.6.1 of Technical Appendix Volume 2, Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET for details on Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”) tax credit assumptions used in Aurora modeling. 

Cell A18 in the “TAS to Aurora – FinalB25 TRADE SECRET.xlsx” workpaper on each of the tabs identified in this question set is not used for tax credit calculations for the 2025 IRP. The tax credit calculation for generic resources modeled in Aurora is performed in STF-JKA-1-22 Attachments A and B TRADE SECRET.  

45Q tax credits for generic combined cycles (“CC”) with carbon capture and sequestration (“CCS”) resources are calculated outside of SAM and are based on an assumed 85% capacity factor with 95% capture rate. 45Q tax credits are modeled as a reduction in the total fixed capital costs, which are annualized for input to Aurora. Since 45Q credits, like production tax credits (“PTCs”), are based on output of the unit, resources offered in Aurora are assumed to run at availability during the twelve years for which the credits are available. Previous Aurora testing showed that applying the credits as variable offsets to operations and maintenance (“O&M”) costs resulted in the units running at availability, justifying this modeling approach.

See response to subpart (a).

PTCs for generic solar resources in the Resource Mix Study are calculated outside of SAM and are based on megawatt hour (“MWh”) expected using the generic solar profile and the IRA assumptions described in Technical Appendix Volume 2, Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET. PTCs for these resources are applied in Aurora as a reduction to fixed capital costs, which are annualized for input to Aurora. 

PTCs for generic wind resources in the Resource Mix Study are calculated outside of SAM and are based on MWh expected using the generic wind profile and the IRA assumptions described in Technical Appendix Volume 2, Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET.  These benefits are applied to reduce the wind resource net present value (“NPV”) by reducing the build capital cost. PTCs for these resources are applied in Aurora as a reduction to fixed capital costs, which are annualized for input to Aurora. See subpart (e) for discussion on selection of investment tax credits (“ITC”) or PTC.

For the Resource Mix Study, the Company based its tax credit assumptions for generic resources on recent bid development experience and expert judgment of the Company’s finance and tax experts. ITC and PTC credits for generic solar and wind resources are not expected to be significantly different from each other, but this could vary over time due to changes in the capital cost of technology and other factors. For generic wind and solar resources, PTCs are expected to provide more benefit than the ITC due to their relatively lower capital cost (versus nuclear) assumed in the Resource Mix Study. On the other hand, nuclear resources have significantly higher capital cost, which makes the ITC more beneficial. 

SAM is used to determine the amount of the ITC to be applied to generic nuclear resources and are based on the IRA assumptions described in Technical Appendix Volume 2, Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET. See subpart (e) for discussion on selection of ITC or PTC.


===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-JKA-3 PD/STF-JKA-3-13.docx =====
STF-JKA-3-13

Question:

Refer to page 9 of “2025 IRP Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET.docx” the Company states, “For modeling purposes, the Company has assumed a 10 percent discount on Clean Electricity (48E and 45Y) tax credits. Because of the nascent state of CCS and associated concerns with commercial deployment of the technology, the Company assumed a 25 percent discount on 45Q tax credits.”
How did the Company determine 10% was a reasonable assumption for Clean Electricity? Please explain.
How did the Company determine 25% was a reasonable assumption for CCS? Please explain.
Does the Company expect to be able to use the tax credits itself or monetize them through subsequent transactions? Please describe how the Company intends to utilize and account for the tax credits. 
Response:
The 10% discount for Clean Electricity tax credits accounts for risks associated with the Company’s anticipated inability to directly utilize large amounts of tax credits generated under the Inflation Reduction Act (“IRA”) and the tax market appetite for credits. Market transactions for tax credits have been observed to trade below the full value of the tax credit due to a number of reasons, including the following:
Transaction Costs: Transaction costs can include fees for advisers and syndicators and for conducting due diligence. 
Recapture: If specified conditions of a project are not met, the federal government may determine that the project was not eligible for the investment tax credit (“ITC”) and that that a portion or all of the tax credits previously claimed must be paid back to the government. There is typically a five-year period for determining whether conditions were met.  
Developer creditworthiness and control: Developers will be responsible for maintaining and operating the system throughout the recapture period in addition to supporting whatever indemnities they give to the buyer. 
Novel or unproven technologies: Novel clean energy technologies, such as carbon capture and sequestration (“CCS”), would be expected to experience a significant discount on IRA tax credits primarily due to risks and uncertainties associated with their early-stage development and commercial deployment. 
The 10% discount was informed by these risks and guided by professional judgment of the Company’s finance and tax experts. The Company will pursue the maximum value possible for customers when specific Company-owned projects are identified and approved.
The 25% discount for 45Q tax credits accounts for risks associated with the Company’s anticipated inability to directly utilize large amounts of tax credits generated under the IRA and the tax market appetite for credits associated with fossil fuel-fired technologies utilizing CCS. The 25% discount was guided by professional judgment of the Company’s finance and tax experts.
The Company assumes that the volume of the tax credits generated under the IRA is likely to be greater than the Company’s tax burden. Excess tax credits would be transferred to third parties in order to monetize the benefits of the credits for customers. Additionally, for ITCs, which can be more beneficial than production tax credits (“PTCs”) for capital intensive projects (e.g., nuclear) and/or projects that might be eligible for additional tax credit bonuses, transfer enables regulated utilities to avoid normalization, which can provide additional economic benefit to customers.

Question:
Refer to “TAS to Aurora - FinalB25 TRADE SECRET.xlsx” tab: “Solar Track” and the renewable integration costs described in cell C14 and on row 108.
Please describe the source and methodology used to derive the integration costs included in the generic solar modeling. 
Provide all workpapers and calculations deriving the time series included in row 108.
Please explain if the Company is also adjusting operating reserves or contingency reserve requirements in conjunction with renewable integration cost modeling.
Did the Company consider modeling integration impacts as change to operating or contingency reserve requirements in Aurora?  If so, explain what assumptions the Company considered. If not, explain why not.
Response:
The solar integration costs were sourced from the 2021 Renewable Integration Study provided with Georgia Power’s 2022 Integrated Resource Plan (“IRP”), Docket No. 44160, Volume 1, Section 3. As identified in the 2021 Renewable Integration Study, the integration cost included in the model should reflect both an increasing solar and storage build to mitigate some of the flexibility and balancing issues. From the 2021 Renewable Integration Study, this cost can be seen in the green line below. 

The regression for this curve trends flat at Battery Energy Storage System (“BESS”) penetration above 10,000 megawatts (“MW”), which indicates that if BESS is built alongside solar in the system, the integration cost will stay relatively the same. Because of this trend, a solar integration cost of $1.52/MWh was selected as the integration costs for generic solar. The $1.52/MWh reflects the average integration cost of 10,000 – 20,000 MW of solar, given the amount of existing, planned, and committed solar in the cases plus the MW of generic solar selected in the expansion plans. 
See the TAS to Aurora – FinalB25 TRADE SECRET workpaper submitted with Technical Appendix Volume 2, Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET. Line 108 of the tab “Solar Track” contains the necessary formula to derive the time series in row 108. The Company established the 2028 value and escalated and de-escalated at the inflation rate included in the attachment.
In the Resource Mix Study, adjustments to operating/contingency reserves are made for existing renewable resources only. Expansion solar resources added in the Resource Mix Study have integration costs included as a separate cost determined by the Renewable Integration Study, so no adjustments are made to reserve requirements for them.
The Company did consider dynamically modifying the reserves to accommodate increased renewable penetration, but determined the level of complexity was unwarranted given that solar integration costs can be accounted for as determined by the 2021 Renewable Integration Study.

Question:
Refer to page 21 of “2025 IRP Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET.docx.” Table 4 indicates a CT with SCR modeled capacity factor limitation at 20%.
How was the 20% level determined?

Does the Company expect a 20% capacity factor limitation at the new Yates CTs? Please explain.

Please explain any constraints to actual operation or modeled operation by unit/plant and provide supporting documentation for such limitations or constraints modeled or otherwise.

Response:
Please see the response to STF-JKA-1-6 subpart (f). 
An annual average capacity factor limit on aggregate combustion turbine (“CT”) operation has been used for many years in most generic capacity expansion analyses / resource mix studies. These resource mix studies are then used for the Unit Retirement Study (“URS”), unit uprates, and other analyses. Modeling analyses to support specific resource decisions will utilize information specific to the situation to reflect any constraints or limitations applicable to that evaluation.  
Individual units may have air permit limits, fuel availability limits, and maintenance or service agreement limits that would limit operation. There are not additional limits that would enforce a 20% annual average capacity factor limit on the actual operation of units.
The 20% annual average capacity factor limit on aggregate CT operation that is used in modeling is a single constraint applied collectively on all existing and new generic CTs. It is intended to broadly represent limitations on combustion turbine operation that could be attributed to permit limitations and performance standards under the Clean Air Act. It is not a physical requirement driving an operational constraint.
The current air permit for the new Plant Yates Units 8-10 CTs includes fuel burn limits equivalent to a capacity factor limitation of 41.5%. These limits may be revised in the future to reflect the 2024 111 GHG Rules, which include emission limits that vary based on a capacity factor as low as 20%. Please refer to Section 2.3.6.2 of the Company’s 2025 Environmental Compliance Strategy. On March 12, 2025, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (“EPA”) announced that the agency will reconsider the 2024 111 GHG Rules. The outcome of the reconsideration is not known at this time and the 2024 111 GHG Rules remain in effect.
NOX-emitting resources have as part of their variable costs an estimate of the current market prices of applicable emission allowances. 
The 20% annual average capacity factor limit on aggregate CT operation that is used in modeling is not a binding constraint in the following cases: MG0, LG0, HG0, MG20. The constraint is binding in some years in the other five scenarios in the Mix Study. The annual value of the capacity factor constraint applied in Aurora and Aurora’s calculated annual aggregate capacity factor for CTs can be found in the Aurora output files, provided in workpapers for Technical Appendix 2 Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET, in the “Constraint_Summary” table. The company has not produced a summary of this information.  The Company re-evaluates all assumptions, including the CT capacity factor limit, as part of its annual planning process and will modify if deemed appropriate in future plans.

===== DKT 56002 & 56003 STF-JKA-3 PD/STF-JKA-3-19.docx =====
STF-JKA-3-19

Question:
Refer to page 7 of “2025 IRP Resource Mix Study TRADE SECRET.docx” which states, “the Company’s CO2 Emissions Limit view imposes a requirement that the Company’s annual aggregate CO2 emissions in 2050 are reduced by about 95% relative to 2007 emissions. This view is implemented in the Company’s Aurora expansion plan modeling through an iterative process, applying a price on CO2 emissions that achieves the 2050 emissions limit target.”
Please describe the “iterative process” and whether the prices changes considered were escalation changes and/or base price changes.
Did the Company enforce the emissions limit (“EL”) gradually or a step down by the 2050 target year?   Describe the price shaping for each iteration and the final pricing used for the EL case.
Please explain if the Company had to add additional constraints and/or release constraints (e.g. add additional resource availability) to accomplish the target result.
Please describe the function of change sets, “Relaxed FT Nuclear,” “B2025 Final IRP – RM Window Fix – MG0 – 40P,” “Set Curtailment Price High,” and “Shadow Price” contained in the “B2025 Final EL_Archive TRADE SECRET.zip” project.
For parts a, b, c, and d above. Please explain and provide all workpapers and analysis performed in developing the inputs required to implement the EL case.
Response:
The iterative process included escalation rate changes, base price changes, and changes to the price in individual years. 
The Company applied a two-step approach to solve the Emissions Limit (“EL”) case. In the first step, the Company applied an annual carbon (“CO2”) emissions limit that declined over time through 2050 to achieve the 95% emissions reduction target (from 2007 levels). Upon completion of the first step, Aurora produced a CO2 price that was used to achieve the annual CO2 emissions reductions. In the second step, Aurora was run with the CO2 price from step one, similar to the other scenarios that include a price on carbon.  
In addition to this two-step approach, the case required iterative solves to determine the combination of constraints necessary to relax to achieve the emissions limit target. Ultimately, the EL case applied relaxed combined cycle (“CC”) build limits, relaxed nuclear build limits, and a relaxed reserve margin limit to meet the 2050 target with minimal demand side curtailment. These changes are described in subpart (d).  
See response to subpart (a).
As described in subpart (a), the EL case required a two-step approach and iterative solves to produce a reasonable solve that achieved the emissions limit target. Ultimately, the EL case used relaxed CC and nuclear build limits and increased the reserve margin limit to 40% to meet the 2050 target with minimal demand side curtailment. These changes are described in more detail in part (d).  
The “Relaxed FT Nuclear” change set increased the number nuclear blocks allowed each year from the default of two blocks, which begins in 2037 in all cases, to three blocks starting in 2045. This change set also increased the cumulative amount of combined cycle megawatts (“MW”) allowed each year.  
The “B2025 Final IRP-RM Window Fix-MG0-40P” change set increased the maximum reserve margin on the system from 30% to 40%. This allowed the model to select low carbon resources earlier to help reach the 2050 emission limit. The “Set Curtailment Price High” change set imposed a higher price on load curtailment (i.e., reduced demand) to incentivize the model to avoid “demand side curtailment”. The “Shadow Price” change set provides the CO2 price stream derived from the step one solve. 
There are not workpapers for the iterative process to identify the CO2 price sequence or the constraint relaxations used to achieve the EL solve. The sequence of CO2 prices used to solve the EL case are part of the Aurora archive for that case, B2025 Final EL_Archive TRADE SECRET.zip.

Aurora version 14.2.1104 is a version update that includes a fix for a problem discovered in how previous versions of Aurora, including 14.2.1084, handled the co-firing of gas at existing coal units. The main difference between solutions produced by the different versions is that in the solves using the 111 view of future CO2 pressure, the co-firing and operational pattern for Plant Bowen and Plant Scherer is reasonable in solves using version 14.2.1104 and not reasonable in solves using version 14.2.1084. For example, some Bowen units co-fired at 40% in every operating hour rather than optimizing the use of coal and gas throughout the year. As it relates to the expansion plans, the Company observed minor changes due to the different versions of Aurora. Based on the Company’s experience conducting specific resource evaluations using Aurora cases, the minor differences observed would not change the results of any recommendations made in this IRP.

As explained on page 16 of the 2024 Reserve Margin Study provided in Technical Appendix Volume 1, Section 2, an aggregate economic load carrying capability (“ELCC”) value was calculated for demand side, solar, and wind resources. For the winter season, the aggregate ELCC for these resources was 21%. For the summer season, the aggregate ELCC was 44%. These aggregate values resulted in the recognized capacity values shown in Table I-5 on page 17 of the 2024 Reserve Margin Study. An increase in the summer target reserve margin is not expected to have a notable impact on summer ELCC values. The Company will reevaluate ELCC values in 2025 incorporating the recommendations from the 2024 Reserve Margin Study.

STF-JKA-3-9

Question:

Refer to Figure 6 in the Reserve Margin Study describing the Summer Target Reserve Margin Floor as a function of Winter Reserve Margin.
Has GPC conducted any studies to determine the optimal way to manage the summer and winter planning reserve margins?  
Has the Company considered cost and/or resource availability in setting the winter and summer target along that curve? Provide the analysis conducted and explain the process and findings, if applicable. 

Response:

The 2024 Reserve Margin Study provides seasonal reserve margin targets that combine to meet an annual 0.1 days per year loss of load expectation (“LOLE”) threshold along with an economic analysis that supports the same values.

Resource cost was a consideration in setting the target reserve margins. The winter risk-adjusted confidence intervals of the economic analysis supported a target reserve margin (“TRM”) as high as 28.25% while the winter reliability analysis required a minimum target of 25.75%. Because the equivalent summer reserve margin is 24.76% at a winter TRM of 26.00%, increasing the summer TRM to 20.00% is not expected to drive additional System costs as described on page 71 of the 2024 Reserve Margin Study. Alternatively, if the winter TRM had been increased in order to maintain a lower summer TRM, additional capacity would need to be procured and costs incurred at the same peak load forecasts. To ensure consistent long-term planning stability, system reliability, and to minimize cost to the customers, the decision was made to recommend an increase in the summer TRM to 20% while maintaining a 26% winter TRM.

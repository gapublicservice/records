Q.On whose behalf are you testifying in this proceeding?
A.We are presenting this panel testimony on behalf of GSEIA. GSEIA was created in late 2010 primarily to serve as a regulatory advocate for the advancement of solar photovoltaic (“PV”) generation.  GSEIA has 12 members who are primarily based in Georgia.  More than 5,639 people are employed in Georgia’s solar energy industry.  
GSEIA has actively participated in Georgia Power’s triannual Integrated Resource Plan proceedings since 2013.  Today, our organization focuses on standalone and customer-sited Distributed Generation (“DG”) solar projects, which generally range in size from one (1) MW to six (6) MW.  By contrast, other intervenors focus their advocacy on different segments of solar energy generation, such as utility scale (“US”) solar generation and residential and commercial rooftop solar generation.  

Q.  What is the purpose of the panel’s testimony in this 2025 IRP proceeding?
A.  Our testimony discusses the advancement of DG solar in Georgia in the past fifteen (15) years, the importance of DG solar to customers, the state, the grid, and Georgia Power Company (the “Company”); the Company’s proposals for DG solar in the 2025 IRP; and GSEIA’s recommendations for slight, but significant enhancements to the Company’s procurement framework for DG solar.  In short, our testimony addresses the portions of the Company’s 2025 IRP filing which proposes deployment of new DG solar procurements and suggests improvements to DG requests for proposal (“RFPs”), programs, and power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) that will result in continued success of DG solar programs.  Our goal is to ensure that the Company’s proposals and the Commission’s policy decisions for DG solar are implemented as thoroughly and successfully as possible. 

Q. What is Distributed Generation (“DG”)?  
A.DG refers to any solar PV generation that is interconnected to the Company’s grid at the distribution level (rather than the high voltage transmission level).  DG solar generation can include: (1) behind-the-meter, grid tied roof-top; (2) Customer-Sited Buy All/Sell All (“BA/SA”), where the customer sells all energy it produces to the Company and buys all the energy it uses from the Company; or (3) stand-alone ground-mount solar projects, which are interconnected to a local distribution feeder.  In essence, DG encompasses smaller scale, decentralized solar projects located closer to load centers, as opposed to large, remote utility scale (“US”) solar farms.  Our testimony addresses (1) the latter two types of DG solar—BA/SA and stand-alone ground-mount solar—with an emphasis on stand-alone DG solar projects, which compose the majority of DG solar in Georgia and (2) the Company’s proposals for the same in the 2025 IRP. 

Q.  Why is DG solar’s versatility important?
A.DG solar is currently the most under-utilized form of solar PV generation in Georgia, yet it is in many ways the most beneficial to ratepayers, the grid, the state, and the Company.  It creates employment and tax revenue that stay in Georgia.
DG solar’s versatility comes from its ability to generate energy in many geographic locations.  DG solar projects require at most 30 acres.  Because of their small footprint and dispersed nature, DG solar generation is not tied to a handful of locations where massive generation plants must be constructed to generate electricity for transportation to the location of demand across hundreds of miles of the state or region.  Instead, with DG solar, generation is spread across the state, not concentrated in a few sites.  This geographic dispersion enhances grid security and reliance and reduces the transmission costs.  DG solar also provides widespread economic benefit, bringing investment to many more communities.  In our view, DG solar represents the future of electric generation in Georgia.  The economic values and benefits resulting from DG solar deployment are well documented, and they will change over time as technology adds to DG solar’s versatility.  
Indeed, the Company in the 2025 IRP has again affirmed the value of DG solar.  Initially, the Company’s RCB Framework credited DG solar generation with more benefits than US solar, although the framework did not fully recognize all the benefits DG solar can provide.  Now, in the 2025 IRP, the Company is updating the Renewable Cost Benefit (“RCB”) Framework to “incorporat[e] . . . locational value in DG procurement evaluations.”  By valuing location specific benefits, the RCB Framework will yet again confirm that DG is the form of solar generation that creates the broadest geographic value throughout Georgia.  

Q. How did Georgia develop a successful DG solar landscape? 
A. Due to the policy leadership of the Commission and GPC’s commitment to solar generation, Georgia has a robust solar industry.  In fact, solar generation presently accounts for about 7% of the Company’s capacity mix.  These forward-looking strategies have propelled Georgia into the top tier of solar-adopting states over the last decade.  The success of solar in Georgia has been achieved without mandates, such as renewable portfolio standards (“RPS”), that have been employed in other states.  Due to their scale and the availability of land in South Georgia, the majority of solar projects in Georgia have been US projects in the southern part of the state, and many of these have been driven by GPC IRPs.  As the Company predicts unprecedented load growth, the benefits of and opportunities for DG solar increase.  To that end, there are opportunities, which we describe later in our testimony, to ensure that the MW the Commission has determined are in the best interest of the state, ratepayers, the grid, and the Company to add to the system are fully procured.

Q. What is the status of the DG procurements from the 2013, 2016, 2019, and 2022 IRPs and the 2023 IRP Update?
A. The Company’s DG procurements have delivered mixed results.  Beginning with the 2013 IRP, the original 100 MW of competitively bid solar DG was mostly procured, with 11.6 MW remaining after the Company entered into 191 PPAs totaling 88.4 MW of capacity.  The unawarded 11.6 MW were carried forward into the DG procurement resulting from the 2016 IRP.
After the 2016 IRP, the Company sought to acquire 100 MW of DG solar through an RFP and 50 MW of customer-sited DG.  The 11.6 MW remaining from the 2013 IRP was rolled over into the customer-sited program.  In that program, the Company acquired 47.5 MW of the total 61.6 MW.  The 14.1 MW shortfall was shifted to the 100 MW DG RFP, raising the total target capacity to 114.1 MW.  In that RFP, the Company acquired 27.4 MW of DG solar capacity.  The capacity remaining from that RFP (27.4 MW) was redirected to add 10 MW to a then-ongoing 50 MW Biomass RFP.
In its Order Adopting Stipulation as Amended in the 2019 IRP, the Commission directed the Company to acquire 210 MW of DG solar, in a 160 MW DG RFP and a 50 MW customer-sited DG program.  The Commission subsequently issued an order reducing the MW for the customer-sited DG program known as the Customer-Connected Solar Program (“CCSP”) to 25 MW and allocating the other 25 MW to the REDI Customer Sited (“CS”) II program (“REDI CS II”).  In that order, the Commission also directed that any unsubscribed capacity from the CCSP program would roll into the REDI CS II DG Program, and any remaining capacity from the REDI CS II DG Program would roll over into the 2020 DG RFP Program.  Then, in a September 8, 2020 order, the Commission directed any unprocured MW in the 2020 DG RFP to be rolled over into a future DG RFP program.  CCSP is still ongoing. In the REDI II CS program, the Company procured 9.724 MW, leaving 15.276 MW unallocated. 
In the 2020 DG RFP, the Company entered into 42 PPAs, 5 of which were terminated, resulting in the Company procuring 89 MW of solar capacity. In the 2023 Distributed Generation RFP, which stemmed from the 2022 IRP, the Company procuring 89 MW of the 193 MW target.  The 2024 DG RPF has a procurement target of 250 MW and is ongoing.

Q. What has or will happen to the unsubscribed capacity from prior and ongoing DG RFPs and programs?
A. Presumably, these MW will roll forward as they historically have for both DG and US projects.  The 2025 IRP is not clear on whether these unfulfilled DG MW will roll forward to future DG RFPs and programs. The 2025 IRP, however, is clear that previously unsubscribed US solar MW will be rolled forward to successive US solar RFPs.  DG should be treated no differently.  If the Commission directs the Company to procure MWs and those MWs are unsubscribed, then those MWs should roll forward to future programs and RFPs that seek to procure similar resources, thereby ensuring that the Commission’s policy decisions—which have been proven to create a successful energy environment that has driven Georgia’s reputation as the number 1 state to do business—are fulfilled. 

Q.Are DG solar projects confronted with additional programmatic challenges?
A.  Yes.  There are a number of issues with DG RFPs that deter deployment of DG solar.  Most problematic are PPA terms that have proven difficult to financially underwrite, which have caused prior DG RFPs and programs to be undersubscribed, as detailed above.  
Prior PPA terms have required participants to post substantial collateral—in the form of a letter of credit or cash collateral—that are onerous and that the capital and debt markets have resisted.  For example, in prior programs, a 5 MW project would have had to post $725,000 in collateral.  Even if the upfront collateral requirements were acceptable to financial underwriters, the collateral requirements do not decrease over the life of the PPA, even though it would be logical that collateral requirements would decrease over the term of a PPA as the contracted energy amount is delivered.  Similarly, the types of acceptable collateral poses impediments to DG solar deployment.  Bonds are more cost efficient as compared to letters of credit and cash collateral, while having no negative effect on the ability of the Company to retrieve funds in the event it must access the collateral.  If the Company would accept bonds, in addition to letters of credit and cash collateral, DG solar projects would be easier to finance without detriment to the Company or ratepayers.  
Financiers have also expressed concern about the events of default in current PPAs.  The Company’s current PPAs do not include as an event of default an event where the Company does not pay for electricity delivered to the grid.  Additionally, the Company’s current PPAs do not require the Company to pay damages in the event that it defaults under a PPA.  GSEIA acknowledges that such events are highly unlikely, given the Company’s strong financial position and success.   However, financial markets see that there is the ability for the Company to default under PPAs without consequence.  Although such events are unexpected and improbable, simple modifications to the PPAs can provide certainty to the markets and lenders that would likely result in more solar DG projects being successfully underwritten and therefore constructed. 
Likewise, current PPAs allow the Company to terminate PPAs for force majeure events that last more than 6 months.  DG solar projects are typically underwritten for 30 year terms.  Capital and debt markets have expressed significant concern, and in certain instances declined to finance DG solar projects, due to this PPA term.  Increasing the time—from 6 months to 12 months—of an extended force majeure event that allow the Company to terminate PPAs would provide more certainty for financers and allow more DG projects to be underwritten.  As a result, more of the DG solar MWs that the Commission has determined will be beneficial to the state will be constructed. 
Moreover, current PPAs provide that if the Annual Energy Contract Amount (“AECA”) is not met after 2 consecutive years, the generator is in default.  This provision has also made it difficult to finance PPAs.  For example, a failure of critical equipment in early winter may cause production loss for 2 consecutive years putting the generator in default.  Modifying the PPAs to provide that an event of default occurs if the AECA is not met for 3 consecutive years would result in more DG solar projects being financed and therefore constructed and approved.  While the likelihood of such an event of default occurring is low, from an underwriting perspective it has caused financers to have significant enough concern that projects do not get underwritten and as a result, solar MW have been left unfilled.
Additionally, Section 4.5 of the standard Company interconnection agreement provides that termination of a PPA results in termination of the related interconnection agreement.  As a result, DG solar projects that have had a PPA terminated cannot sell energy to the Company as a qualified facility (“QF”).  This provision, although unlikely to be triggered, also has resulted in difficulty in financing DG solar projects.  As the Commission has seen, issues with interconnection challenges have proven problematic.  Georgia has a shrinking pool of developable land with suitable interconnection attributes.  The available land is quickly diminishing due to economic development.  In some instances, projects are being rejected by the Company if any transmission upgrades are required. There should be inexpensive transmission upgrades allowed that do not cost too much to kill a project.  
Program fees are also higher for DG solar projects than US projects.  Specifically, bidder’s fees that are imposed on all projects submitted in the solar solicitation are substantially higher (per kW) for DG solar projects than US.  Additionally, winner’s fees collected from successful projects are also substantially higher (per kW) for DG solar projects than US.
There are other examples of instances in which DG solar projects have not been financed because of nuances in PPAs that could be resolved without detriment to ratepayers, the grid, or the Company.  The 2025 IRP acknowledges that DG solar resources provide unique benefits.  As a result, a robust evaluation of RFP and PPA terms by the Commission will result in more DG solar MWs being deployed throughout the state.

Q. How are DG solar generation attributes affected by the co-location of storage with solar?
A.They are all improved.  Pairing solar and storage to create a hybrid resource enhances the capability, reliability, and efficiency of the grid.  System operators can use the storage to firm up solar power, balancing the output during transient event such as passing clouds.  The storage can also be used to extend the useful output of the project later into the evenings to help meet summer peaks, or store energy until the following morning to help address winter morning loads.  Storage can enhance the controllability of the hybrid plant and allow it to respond more dynamically to control signals, providing frequency regulation support.  
Pairing these two resources allows for better utilization of existing grid infrastructure, ensuring that resources can fully utilize but never exceed the limitations of the grid. This meaningfully reduces the need for costly system upgrades. 
Considering that all of these attributes increase the value of the underlying zero-emission, zero-marginal cost energy, it is no wonder that the solar industry is rapidly shifting to solar plus storage hybrid projects.
The 2025 IRP, however places this segment of the market (solar plus Battery Energy Storage System (“BESS”)) as a traditional solar resource, while there are still many lessons to be learned about how such resources operate and benefit the grid, the Company, the State, and ratepayers.

Q. Are there lessons to learn from a pilot projects for DG solar plus BESS? 
A. Yes, the Company has a number of programs where it has permitted resources to be combined with BESS.  It also has standalone BESS that it owns and that is owned by third parties.  However, the Company has yet to implement a program for DG solar coupled with BESS.  Such a program would allow the Company, the Commission, and solar developers to build out this valuable resource mix, learning more about the benefits that such systems can add to the grid and gaining insight into pricing structures that are best suited for a resource that provides dispatchable resources that solar generation not paired with storage cannot.  For instance, in such a program, the Company could provide pricing thresholds that, if met by a bidder, would result in the project being selected.  GSEIA recommends a pilot for 200 MW of DG solar plus BESS. This pilot program would be the first step to design more comprehensive programs, so it should not be required to be entirely cost-neutral. 

Q. What is a virtual power plant (“VPP”)?
A. A virtual power plant or “VPP” aggregates Distributed Energy Resources (“DERs”) like rooftop solar with behind-the-meter (“BTM”) BESS, electric vehicles and chargers, electric water heaters, smart thermostats, and flexible commercial and industrial (“C&I”) loads that can match electricity demand with supply and deliver large-scale, high-quality grid services similar to those provided by traditional power plants.  Unlike traditional demand response, VPPs that include BTM solar plus BESS (“solar+storage”) are capable of injecting power into the electric grid and should be compensated for total BESS dispatch, regardless of whether it results in load reduction of the customer or injections into the grid. 

Q. What is the benefit of a solar+storage VPP? 
A. A solar+storage VPP can help meet load growth and resource adequacy concerns in the State of Georgia.  The Company’s projections for load growth in the state, due to the State’s reputation as the Empire State of the South and the Number One State to do business, necessitate innovation to meet unprecedented demand.  Data center growth across the county has doubled since 2022 when ChatGPT commenced commercial operation.  Data center investment across the country is expected to exceed $150,000,000,000 by 2028.  The aggregation of solar+storage VPPs has been a useful tool in meeting the growth of new energy demand throughout the country and can help Georgia strengthen its grid, avoid capital intensive infrastructure projects, and make homes and businesses more resilient to outages.
Solar+storage VPPs help balance supply and demand by aggregating distributed solar and BESS, allowing Georgia to meet growing electricity needs without relying solely on new large-scale infrastructure.  By decentralizing energy resources and enabling localized backup power, solar+storage VPPs reduce vulnerability to outages and improve the grid’s ability to respond to extreme weather and emergencies.  Compared to traditional power plants and grid upgrades, solar+storage VPPs can be deployed quickly—can be operationalized in 6 months—making them well-suited to address near-term capacity needs.  VPPs are often significantly cheaper than new power plants and grid expansions, helping utilities avoid capital-intensive investments while lowering long-term electricity costs for consumers.  Solar+storage VPPs unlock latent grid value and improve utilization of already-installed assets, deferring or avoiding costly upgrades.
And as importantly, VPPs, on an operational basis, provide the means to rapidly dispatch load when needed. Dissimilar to traditional power plants, VPPs do not need to ramp up or ramp down but can be dispatched immediately. Any other forms of generation, inclusive of a gas peaker plant, will be less efficient on a ramp up, ramp down basis than a dispatch of stored power in batteries.

A. No, presently there are no solar+storage programs in Georgia.

Q. Can the Company, the Commission, and the Georgia solar energy industry use lessons learned from other VPP programs in its pilot? 
A. Yes, as detailed above, VPP programs aggregate DERs, particularly rooftop solar plus BTM BESS, and help the grid realize the benefits of these resources on as if they were larger resources.  The Connected Solution VPP program among other successes, as referenced above, have demonstrated the benefits of VPP.  These programs compensate for dispatched BESS dispatch and exports.  This differs from demand side management tools that reduce load.  Instead, these programs utilize inverter data to ensure that minimize costs associated with VPP programs and eliminate the need for redundant metering.  
Additionally, these programs set a minimum and maximum from called dispatch events, and they set a maximum length of controlled events.  For example, Connected Solutions, in Connecticut, sets a max dispatch window of three hours, which has been mimicked by the Energy Storage Solutions VPP program. Connected Solutions has also set a maximum number of called grid events at 60 and a minimum at 30.  This approach sets customer and aggregator expectations for how often a BESS will be used over the course of a dispatch season and ensures fair compensation when customers are receiving pay-for-performance renumeration. And putting a cap on the number of events can guarantee that the Company is able to use the battery to provide a positive value for ratepayers without excessively using or burdening a battery system for which the customer primarily paid.  Additionally, it is difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of upfront compensation without some outer bound of usage to account for system degradation according to frequency of called events. Financing systems can become more difficult without such parameters.  Moreover, such programs do and should permit customers to opt-out of call events at any time regardless of whether it is utility or customer controlled, without incentive clawbacks or punishment.
Successful VPP programs also align compensation with the value customers are providing to the grid. And for Georgia to make the most of a VPP pilot, the annual capacity incentive and one-time upfront incentive should be reflective of the benefits it will provide to the grid, which have been referenced above. Therefore, a program’s capacity incentive value should not be discounted, considering the grid benefits and ultimate cost savings it could be providing for non-participating customers. Capacity being procured in this pilot should be compensated at 100% of its calculated value. 
In the alternative, we recommend that the Company should be given the ability to move quickly to adjust the discount percentage—including to zero—if customer interest and uptake in the pilot is insufficient. The point of a pilot is to learn customer behaviors and activities with these technologies. Thus, achieving deployment targets should be a short-term priority and fine tuning the net benefits of the programs to create a sustainable pathway for customer-sited solar+storage should be the longer-term objective.
To ensure that this pilot program provides sufficient learnings, uptake should be prioritized. Incorporating the above best practices would ensure participation, while providing the state with the necessary data to confidently create a fully formed, permanent program. 

GEORGIA SOLAR’S RECOMMENDATIONS
Q.Does GSEIA have recommendations for the Commission regarding deployment of DG resources in Georgia?
Yes, GSEIA recommends that the Commission modify the IRP as follows: 
Direct the Company to roll over all MW approved by the Commission in prior IRPs to the DG RFPs and programs for which the Company seeks approval in the 2025 IRP; 
Direct the Company to create a 200 MW DG solar plus BESS pilot program whereby the Company provides a pricing threshold that, if met by a proposed project, will result in the project being selected, on a first-come, first-served basis, until the 200 MW threshold is met. 
Reconstitute the DG working group with the goal of refining the program guidelines for DG RFPs and PPA terms and (most importantly) direct that a comprehensive report on the working group be filed with and reviewed by the Commission in a separate docket, to ensure that the Commission’s policy decisions are effectuated and fulfilled so that the DG solar MWs that the Commission has seen and in this IRP sees fit for advancement of solar in the State of Georgia are fully subscribed. 
Create a 50 MW VPP pilot program that allows the Company and the Commission to learn lessons on the benefits that VPPs can provide to ratepayers, the system, and the Company and that utilizes the VPP pilot program best practices included in the testimony above (e.g. minimum and maximum called grid events).

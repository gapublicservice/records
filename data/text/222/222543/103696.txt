14   Q.      WHAT IS A RESERVE MARGIN AND HOW DOES IT IMPACT CUSTOMER

15           BILLS?

16   A.      A reserve margin is the amount of generation capacity, in excess of expected utility peak

17           load, that is required to ensure that a utility can provide reliable service in the event of a

18           loss or disruption of resources or higher than expected loads due to weather events or other

19           factors. From a planning perspective, the higher the TRM the more generation capacity the

20           utility must acquire. While this additional reserve capacity provides reliability to the

21           system, it also has a cost, which will increase customer bills.

22                  As in prior IRPs, Georgia Power performed a study to derive the TRM for the

23           Southern Company System that is both economic and meets the System’s reliability targets.

 4   Q.         HOW IS THE AMOUNT OF RESERVE CAPACITY DERIVED FROM A

 5              RESERVE MARGIN?

 6   A.         As a simple example, assume a utility forecasts it will have a maximum peak demand of

 7              20,000 MW in a given year. If the TRM is set at 20%, then the utility would need a reserve

 8              margin of 4,000 MW (20,000 MW x 20%), and its total capacity requirement would be

 9              24,000 MW (20,000 MW + 4,000 MW).

 3   Q.    DOES SERVM HAVE THE ABILITY TO CALCULATE THE TRM IN

 4         DIFFERENT WAYS?

 5   A.    Yes. SERVM can calculate a TRM based on three different approaches. First, it can

 6         calculate a TRM based on an economically optimal reserve margin (“EORM”) where it

 7         can find the optimal TRM by trading off customer outage, capital addition, and production

 8         costs. The EORM is then the reserve margin that produces the minimum value of these

 9         components collectively. Second, since SERVM operates using a Monte Carlo

10         probabilistic method and evaluates numerous iterations in determining the expected value

11         result, it can also be used to find the TRM at a higher level of reliability for marginal

12         increases in cost, which the Company refers to as its Value at Risk (“VaR”) approach.

13         Third, SERVM can calculate a TRM that meets the requirement that a loss of load event

14         (“LOLE”) will not occur more than so many times in a 10-year period, such as one day in

15         10-years, which is generally recognized as being the industry standard.

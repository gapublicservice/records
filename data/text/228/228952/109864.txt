 4                                   PURPOSE OF REAL-TIME PRICING

 5   Q:      WHAT IS THE BASIS OF GEORGIA POWER’S REAL-TIME RATE DESIGN?

 6   A:      Georgia Power’s real-time rate design is intended to recover the full marginal cost of

 7           meeting electric demand. The rate design is founded on the peaker method, which

 8           “reproduces the true optimal-mix marginal cost.”4 Georgia Power further states that, “At

 9           the least-cost mix, the peaker-based total exactly equals the true cost built up tier-by-tier –

10           peaking, intermediate, and base.”5

11           Practically speaking, the RTP rate gives customers an option to take on price risk in

12           exchange for potential cost savings.

13           The total RTP rate design, which I will summarize later in my testimony, is intended to

14           contribute both fuel and base revenues. The RTP fuel credit represents the revenues to

15           cover fuel costs and the remainder represents a contribution to base revenues, also referred

16           to as “capital substitution.”

17           For large load customers on TOU-SC MAC, the Monthly Access Charge (MAC) also

18           recovers base revenues and all energy is billed at the hourly RTP-DA price. For other RTP

 5   Q:   DOES CAPITAL SUBSTITUTION REPRESENT DOWNWARD PRESSURE ON

 6        RATES?

 7   A:   No. Capital substitution is a core component of the peaker method and represents the

 8        contribution from lambda to the cost of more efficient generation. As an example, if a

 9        utility built all peakers, then system lambda would be approximately equal to average fuel

10        costs, and there would be no surplus to provide capital substitution – but capital substitution

11        would be unnecessary because the marginal cost of generation, based on the peaker cost,

12        would also be covered. This is because the marginal cost of generation is set to cover the

13        full cost of a peaker unit. Thus, if a customer’s energy use is fully priced using RTP, then

14        in theory the RTP rate covers the full cost of that peaker unit.

15        However, Georgia Power does not fully rely on peakers, at some periods the system lambda

16        recovers more revenues than are credited to cover fuel costs. This surplus is needed to

17        cover the higher cost of generation that operates more frequently than peakers (e.g.,

18        intermediate units). This is a necessary cost of service, and from an economics perspective

19        does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates.

 4   Q:          DO YOU AGREE THAT THE PEAKER METHOD IS SOUND?

 5   A:          Yes, provided that the underlying assumptions hold. There are two basic ways that

 6               underlying assumptions may be violated. First, if Georgia Power’s actual costs are greater

 7               than predicted by application of the peaker method, cost recovery through the RTP rate

 8               would be less than cost recovery using an embedded cost rate. Second, even if actual costs

 9               are commensurate with the peaker method, if some cost categories are not actually

10               considered in the components of the RTP rate, then the RTP rate may under-recover costs.

11   Q:          ARE THERE ANY CONDITIONS ON GEORGIA POWER’S SYSTEM THAT

 2   Q:   DO YOU SUPPORT THE CURRENT RTP FUEL CREDIT METHOD?

 3   A:   No. Several categories of costs recovered from FCR customers are not included in either

 4        the Georgia Power unit costs or the pool purchases/sales cost. The credit – on a $/MWh

 6        incremental load priced at RTP. This shifts costs to FCR customers.

 7        While in this proceeding, Georgia Power has agreed to make some changes, it argues that

 8        the current method is fair, as any revenues that are not recorded as a credit in the FCR are

 9        recorded as base revenues, and serve to diminish the revenue requirement for other

10        customers collected through base rates. While I agree with this concept, it neglects two

11        critical factors: cost allocation in the FCR differs from cost allocation in base rates, and the

12        FCR is subject to under- or over-recovery that is carried forward as a balance, which may

13        affect either an FCR filing or a rate case.

14        Accordingly, the Commission should resolve this proceeding by appropriately identifying

15        costs in the FCR that should be recovered from incremental load priced at RTP and

16        allocating an appropriate portion of those costs to the RTP fuel credit.

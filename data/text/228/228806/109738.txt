     I.   PETITIONERS ARE NOT “INTERESTED PARTIES” UNDER THE
          TERRITORIAL ACT AND FAIL TO SATISFY RULES REQUIRING THEIR
          OWN RIGHTS BE THREATENED

          The Territorial Act creates two groups of legal interests: the assigned supplier’s exclusive

right to serve the premises in its territory, and the customer’s right to adequate, dependable, and

nondiscriminatory service from that supplier. Neither of these rights belongs to the Petitioners.

They are an advocacy organization and four residents who live miles from the Serverfarm property,

take no service from VoltaGrid, and claim no dispute with their own electric supplier. Petitioners’

objection is to the burning of natural gas, which the Territorial Act neither regulates nor protects

against. Because the only rights at stake under the Territorial Act belong to Georgia Power as the

assigned electric supplier, Petitioners can assert no interest the Territorial Act was enacted to

protect and they are not “interested parties” under O.C.G.A. § 46-3-13. For the same reason, the

Petition fails to identify any right of the Petitioners that is “uncertain or insecure,” as Commission

Rule 515-2-1-.12 requires, and no amendment could supply one.

          A. The Territorial Act vests exclusive supply rights in the electric supplier

          The Territorial Act was enacted to give the Commission authority to assign a monopoly

service area to each electric supplier, whether Georgia Power, electric membership corporation or

municipal systems.27 It declares the policy of this State that “every geographic area within the state

shall be either assigned to an electric supplier or declared unassigned as to any electric supplier.”28

Within its assigned area, “the electric supplier shall have the exclusive right to extend and continue

 II.     THE COMMISSION LACKS JURISDICTION OVER VOLTAGRID BECAUSE IT
         DOES NOT FURNISH ELECTRIC SERVICE TO THE PUBLIC

         Petitioners’ lack of statutory interest and failure to comply with the Commission’s rules

alone warrant dismissal of their entire Petition. To the extent Petitioners have standing to pursue

any of their claims, however, the Commission lacks jurisdiction over VoltaGrid as a matter of law

and the action cannot proceed. The jurisdiction of the Public Service Commission is limited, by

statute, to companies “furnishing service to the public.”44 This jurisdictional limitation has been

enforced consistently for over a century to recognize that entities that service a single bespoke

customer, or even a small number of customers, under a privately negotiated contract are not

“furnishing service to the public” and thus not subject to the jurisdiction or regulation of the

Commission.

         A. The Commission’s jurisdiction and the Territorial Act’s prohibitions are limited
            to electric companies that “furnish service to the public”

          B. Serving one specialized, on-site entity under a private contract is not “furnishing
             service to the public”

          VoltaGrid’s provision of power services to a single, on-site, specialized end user does not

render it subject to the Commission’s jurisdiction as a “public” power plant “furnishing service to

the public.” This precedent has been applied consistently by the Georgia Supreme Court, the

Attorney General, and this Commission.

                   i.   The Georgia Supreme Court required service to the public, not just one
                        customer

          The Georgia Supreme Court issued the controlling standard in Atlanta Gas Light Co. v.

Georgia Public Service Commission, 228 Ga. 347 (1971). There, Atlanta Gas Light Company

(“AGLC”) admitted it was a “public utility company” subject to the Commission’s jurisdiction,

but it argued that the specific transaction at issue was “a private, non-utility contract” not subject

to Commission oversight because it involved provision of electricity to only a single customer —

a landlord in Peachtree Center. The Court reasoned that though the landlord was a single customer,

the electricity would serve over 16,000 people in the buildings, a “significant segment of the

public.”56 Further, the Court recognized that AGLC was providing the same “total energy” package

On this basis, the Court concluded the transaction involved servicing the public.58

service constitutes “service to the public” is, therefore, whether the power ultimately reaches a

“significant segment of the public.” As described below, the Attorney General applied exactly that

standard a year later to hold that a generator servicing only three industrial customers was not

furnishing “service to the public” and thus not subject to Commission regulation. Here, VoltaGrid

has a service contract with one entity, Serverfarm, which does not in turn provide electricity to any

other party, much less a “significant segment” of the public. Under the standard set forth by the

Georgia Supreme Court, VoltaGrid does not furnish service to the public and is not subject to

Commission jurisdiction.
